DEFINITIONS

Toxicology

It is the branch of the medical science that deals with poison in relation to their sources, properties, action, clinical features, fatal dose, diagnosis, treatment, autopsy findings and medico-legal aspects.

The word toxicology is derived from the Greek word 'Toxon' that refers to a bow used for shooting arrows.

Toxicology deals with all aspects of poison. The overall investigations of a medico-legal case includes three basic constituents namely the investigation of circumstance of the death including medical history, a thorough autopsy and lab investigations including toxicology. So without toxicology a forensic investigation is never complete.

Classification of Toxicology

  1. Forensic toxicology
  2. Clinical toxicology
  3. Environmental toxicology
  4. Industrial toxicology

Forensic Toxicology

It is the branch of toxicology that deals with establishment of evidences related to poisoning for the purpose of legal treatment and hence justice.

Poison (2003, 2008, 1999, 2001, 2007, 2006, 2011)

It is any substance if taken in any account by any route produces harmful effect (namely disease, deformity or death) over the body.

Clinical Toxicology

It is a branch of toxicology that deals with causation, diagnosis and management of poisoning in humans during clinical practice.

Toxinology

It is the study of toxins (a toxin is a poisonous substance produced during the metabolism and growth of certain microorganisms and some higher plant and animal species, e.g., venom of snake, bee, etc.).

Drug

It is any substance or product that is used or intended to be used to modify or explore physiological systems or pathological states for the benefit of the recipient.

Note: Types of Poisoning

  • Fulminant poisoning: Massive dose results in rapid death, e.g., Oxalic acid poisoning if dose > 15g.
  • Acute poisoning: Single episode of high dose poison intake or multiple episodes over a short period resulting in acute worsening type of clinical features, e.g., Snake bite.
  • Subacute poisoning: Showing features of both acute and chronic poisoning when small doses of poison are taken on daily basis for months or years, e.g., Arsenic poisoning.
  • Chronic poisoning: Showing progressive deteriorating type of clinical features when small doses of poison are taken on daily basis for months or years, e.g., Arsenic poisoning by using well water in Bangladesh; Anemia, Burtonian lines, Colic, etc. in Lead poisoning.

Parasuicide (attempted suicide or pseudocide): A supposed suicide attempt that was not intended to succeed. It is a conscious, often impulsive, manipulative act, undertaken to get rid of an intolerable situation. Drug ingestion is the commonest form.

Action of Poison

  • Local action: Is at the site of application, e.g., burns due to acid on the face.
  • Systemic action: Is at a place far from site of application through blood (or body fluids) or sometimes by reflex neurogenic shock. Aconite causes cardiac toxicity and death.
  • Both local and systemic action: Is both at the site of application and also at a site far from the local site through means of blood (or body fluids) or by reflex neurogenic shock. E.g., Phenol corrodes skin while at the same time affects CNS causing headache, convulsions and coma.

Note: There are no poisons that have only systemic actions. The so-called systemic poisons have major harm through the systemic action and their local action is not much significant though is always present to some extent. But the so-called local poisons are presumed to have no systemic action.

Factors Modifying the Action of Poisons (2001, 2004, 2008)

@Remember the components of pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics and enlist the factors affecting them
  1. Type of poison (some poisons like lead tend to have cumulative action)
  2. Amount or dose or concentration of poison
  3. Routes of application or administration
  4. Condition of the site of absorption (diseases) e.g., GI fibrosis causes reduced absorption of alcohol and state of sleep/intoxication (e.g., action is delayed in sleep state)
  5. Physical state of poison
  6. Tolerance

  9. Route of elimination
  10. Age
  11. Sex

The first five points are related to absorption of poison. The next two are related to the action of the poison. Point no. 8 is related to metabolism of poison, 9 is related to elimination, while the last three points affect all four aspects namely absorption, action, metabolism and elimination. A systemic poison passes through the four major steps also called as fate of poison namely absorption, action, metabolism and elimination. The action part of the poison is its pharmacodynamics component while the absorption, metabolism and elimination parts are its pharmacokinetics components. Some poisons like lead escape elimination and accumulate in the organs.

Routes of Administration

  • Local: dermal
  • Systemic: oral, sublingual, rectal, vaginal, dermal, inhalational, nasal, parenteral

Note: The rectal, vaginal, and nasal routes are usually considered systemic routes in toxicology since significant absorption occurs through these sites and death occurs due to systemic action rather than the local action. The dermal route can be local or systemic depending upon the major effect on the skin or systemic site respectively. The fat-soluble poisons like organophosphates are systemic poisons since they cause no effect on the skin though are absorbed through the skin to exert effect on the muscles and CNS. Here the dermal route is the systemic route.

DIAGNOSIS OF POISONING

Diagnosis in Living

Diagnosis in living is based upon: 1. History   2. Symptoms   3. Signs   4. Laboratory examination

History and Symptoms

  • Sudden onset of vomiting / diarrhea / pain in abdomen
  • Onset of symptoms to many persons who had taken food together
  • History of consumption of poison or suspicious material
  • Symptoms are uniform in character and increase rapidly in severity

Non-Specific symptoms:

  1. Persistent vomiting and/or diarrhea with or without dehydration
  2. Pain in abdomen
  3. Altered sensorium / drowsiness / unconsciousness / coma
  4. Breathlessness, constriction in chest, choking sensation
  5. Convulsions
  6. Palpitations, arrhythmias, chest pain
  7. Hypothermia
  8. Paralysis

Clinical Examination (Signs)

General Examination: Peculiar odor, Taste, Temperature (hypothermia/hyperthermia), Ocular changes, Oral manifestations.

Systemic Examination:

  • Central Nervous System: Grading of coma (patient may be drowsy, deliriant or in coma), Convulsions, Tremors, Neuropathy, Movement disorder, Paralysis, Headache, Paresthesia, Gait, Delirium, Encephalopathy, Tingling, Ataxia, Psychosis.
  • Respiratory System: Dyspnea, Pulmonary edema, Respiratory distress, Cough, Laryngospasm, Bronchitis, Emphysema.
  • Gastrointestinal System: Vomiting, Diarrhea, Constipation, Gastroenteritis, Melena, Abdominal pain, Abdominal distension, Ileus, Thirst, Dysphagia, Odynophagia, Pancreatitis.
  • Genitourinary System: Color of urine, Albuminuria, Hemoglobinuria, Glycosuria, Hematuria, Porphyrinuria, Oliguria, Polyuria, Dysuria.
  • Musculoskeletal System: Myopathy, Myalgia, Rhabdomyolysis, Fasciculation, Smooth muscle depressant/stimulant.
  • Blood Manifestations: Anemia, Blood dyscrasia, Thrombocytopenia, Leukocytosis, Leukopenia, Pancytopenia, Polycythemia, Stippling, Bone marrow depression, Hemolysis, Methemoglobin formation, Sulfhemoglobinemia.
  • Dermal Manifestations: Rashes and Ulcers.
  • Ear Manifestations: Rhinorrhea, Deafness, Vertigo, Tinnitus.
  • Others: Poisons causing fever.

Lab Investigations: Various lab investigations specific to the poisons are sent. For example, plasma cholinesterase level is reduced in OP poisoning, silver-impregnated paper mask test is used in Aluminium phosphide poisoning, Liver function tests are done in Paracetamol poisoning, etc. Some poisons may be excreted in the urine, e.g., Amphetamine is excreted in urine and can be detected by urine analysis.

Note: Most of the symptoms/signs in poisoning are non-specific. So poisoning should be kept in mind with any symptoms of acute onset. Specific lab investigations are more diagnostic, but delay in basic management should not be done while waiting for investigations.

Diagnosis in Dead

Diagnosis of poisoning in dead is done by: 1. Autopsy findings   2. Laboratory examination

Autopsy Findings

  • External Examination: Evidence of soiling of clothes, presence of bottle/container/label in pocket, presence of suicide note, vitriolage, trickling of poison/stigmata of poison over skin, presence of injuries, smell from body. Rigor mortis (may appear early or delayed). Postmortem lividity (poisons may impart peculiar colour). Some poisons resist decomposition or some may hasten it.
  • Internal Examination:
    • Cranial cavity: Presence of odour / cerebral edema / hemorrhages.
    • Chest cavity: Note for presence of pleural effusion, pericardial effusion, state of lungs and heart, hemorrhages, pulmonary edema.
    • Abdominal cavity: Stomach contains gastric contents of various colours. Mucosa may show erosions, ulcers, hemorrhagic gastritis, or gastric mucosa may be stained with the colour of poison. Examine the state of intestine, liver, spleen, kidneys, pancreas, bladder, esophagus, lip, and oral cavity.

Laboratory Examination

It consists of analysis of collected viscera, body fluids and blood for poisons. The laboratory method consists of separation of drug/poison from biological tissue followed by purification and finally analysis proper in qualitative or quantitative assays:

  • Qualitative assays: Type of poison is known (e.g., Thin layer chromatography is a simple and inexpensive technique).
  • Quantitative assays: Type of poison as well as quantification can be known. Examples: Gas chromatography, High performance liquid chromatography, Mass spectrometry, Radio-immunoassay, Atomic absorption spectrophotometry, Neutron activation analysis.

COLLECTION AND PRESERVATION OF VISCERA AND BODY FLUIDS FOR CHEMICAL ANALYSIS

For chemical analysis, it is the duty of the doctor to collect and preserve viscera and other exhibits properly.

Mode of Preservation

  • Viscera is submerged in preservative inside a clean glass container.
  • 1/3rd of the container should be empty.
  • Container should be airtight.
  • In all cases, the sample of the preservative used should be preserved in a separate clean container.
  • Stomach and intestine: one container
  • Liver and kidneys: one container
  • Other viscera: separate container
  • Blood, urine and skin tissue preserved in separate glass containers.
  • Vomit, feces, etc. are also preserved depending upon relevance in clean glass containers.
  • Bone, hair, nails and clothes (after drying): in polythene packets.
  • Properly labeled and sealed.

Label requirements: Name, age, sex of the deceased; Name of the police station; Case reference; Name of the mortuary; S.No. of PM examination; Date of PM; Preservative used; Signature and designation of the autopsy surgeon.

Preservation of Viscera

After collection of viscera, it is important to preserve them properly with proper preservative.

Rectified spirit / absolute alcohol is used as a preservative for viscera in all cases of poisoning EXCEPT the following poisons:

  • Phosphorus
  • Alcohol
  • Phenol (carbolic acid)
  • Paraldehyde
  • Acetic acid
@Mnemonic: "PAPPA is not preserved in rectified spirit".

The viscera are also preserved in a saturated solution of common salt (sodium chloride). Preserving viscera in saturated solution of common salt is routinely practiced because it is economical. Saturated solution of common salt is used to preserve viscera in all poisoning cases EXCEPT:

  • Inorganic acid (mineral acid) poisoning
  • Vegetable poisoning

In suspected cases of rabies, 1 to 2 cm cube of brain is collected and preserved in 50 percent glycerol solution for demonstration of Negri bodies.

For histology: tissues should be cut into small pieces and submerged in 10% formal saline solution.

Collection of Body Fluids and Preservatives

  • Urine: Usually 20 ml preserved in a sterile glass container without any preservative. If desired, boric acid, formaldehyde, or thymol can be used.
  • Blood: Fluoride, oxalate, EDTA, etc. (depends on the type of test and type of poisons). Drawn from subclavian vein with a large 13 gauge bore needle with 50-60 cc syringe. 50 ml blood divided as:
    • Two glass tubes each with 20 ml in NaF (doesn't allow bacterial decomposition)
    • One glass tube with 10 ml in potassium oxalate (anticoagulant) and NaF
  • Vitreous humor: All VH should be collected (reflects drug level in blood 1-2 hr antemortem) and sent in frozen condition.

Failure to Detect Poison in Certain Cases

  1. Poison may have been eliminated by vomiting and diarrhea (e.g., irritant poison).
  2. Poison has disappeared from the lungs by evaporation or oxidation.
  3. Poison after absorption may be detoxified, conjugated, and eliminated from the system.
  4. Some alkaloidal poisons cannot be definitely detected by chemical methods.
  5. Some drugs are rapidly metabolized, making extraction difficult.
  6. Biological toxins and venoms which may be protein in nature cannot be separated from body tissues.
  7. Some organic poisons, especially alkaloids and glucosides, may detoxify by oxidation during life or due to faulty preservation or from decomposition of the body, and cannot be detected chemically.
  8. In a slow-acting poison, death may be delayed and by then the poison may have been completely excreted following production of irreversible changes.
  9. Many drugs may be present in small amounts and these may require a considerable amount of viscera for their identification.
  10. Wrong or insufficient material may have been sent for analysis.

MANAGEMENT OF POISONING

The complete management of poisoning will depend on the results of investigations which may take some time, but basic management should be done while waiting for the results. The following basic principles of management are usually followed in almost every poisoning:

  1. Stabilization and evaluation
  2. Decontamination (Eye, Skin, Gut: Emesis, Gastric lavage, Catharsis, Activated charcoal, Bowel irrigation)
  3. Poison elimination
  4. Antidote administration
  5. Nursing care
  6. Psychiatric care

1. Stabilization and Evaluation

  • A: Maintenance of airway
  • B: Maintenance of breathing
  • C: Maintenance of circulation
  • D: Disability (neurologic status) management: assess level of consciousness
  • E: Elimination of poison

Levels of Consciousness (@AVPU / Gradings):

  • Level 0: Fully conscious (Alert)
  • Level 1: Drowsy but responding to verbal command
  • Level 2: Maximum response to minimal painful stimuli
  • Level 3: Minimal response to maximum painful stimuli
  • Level 4: Unresponsive to painful stimuli, loss of all reflexes including pharyngeal, laryngeal, and corneal

2. Decontamination

Eye Decontamination: If ocular exposure occurs, eye should be irrigated with copious water for 15 to 20 minutes.

Skin Decontamination: Absorption of poison is possible by dermal route. Affected area should be washed thoroughly with plain water, clothes removed, and area irrigated copiously with water.

Gut Decontamination:

A. Emesis

  • Agents used: Syrup of ipecacuanha, Apomorphine, Stimulation of posterior pharyngeal wall.
  • Indications: Conscious patient, ingestion of poison within 4 to 6 hours.
  • Contraindications (@REMEMBER BY 4Cs and V): Coma / convulsion / unconscious patient, Impaired gag reflex, Corrosive / volatile poison ingestion, Pregnancy indicated by cessation of menses.
  • Complications: Aspiration pneumonia, Mallory-Weiss tears, Laryngospasm.

B. Gastric Lavage

  • Indications: Conscious patient, ingestion of poison within 1 to 2 hours.
  • Contraindications (@REMEMBER BY 4Cs and V): Coma / convulsion / unconscious patient, Impaired gag reflex or lack of spontaneous swallowing, Corrosive / volatile poison ingestion, Marked hypothermia or cold body, Esophageal varices, Significant electrolyte imbalance, Hydrocarbon poisoning, Cardiac arrhythmia.
  • Complications: Aspiration pneumonia, Laryngospasm, Mallory-Weiss tears, Perforation of esophagus/stomach, Hypothermia, Bradycardia, Electrolyte imbalance (hyponatremia, hypochloremia).

Gastric Lavage Setup Diagram Components:

Funnel → Rubber tube → Suction bulb → Mouth gag → Ewald tube / Boas tube / Ryle's tube inserted into patient.

Procedure & Equipment for Gastric Lavage:

Gastric lavage (stomach washout) should only be undertaken if the patient has ingested a potentially life-threatening amount of a poison and presents within 1 hour of ingestion. If performed later, the amount of poison removed is insignificant and lavage can make the situation worse by pushing unabsorbed poison into the small intestine.

  • Equipment: Stomach tube / Boas tube (14 mm diameter in adults / red rubber gastric lavage tube), Funnel, Rubber tube, Suction bulb, Mouth gag, KY jelly, Oxygen, Powerful suction equipment, Bucket, Jug (250-500 ml), Cuffed ET tube, PPE (gloves, apron, boots).
  • Step 1 (Positioning): Place patient in left lateral decubitus position so that the mouth is lower than the larynx to aid respiratory drainage and prevent aspiration.
  • Step 2 (Introduction of Tube): Remove artificial dentures. Lubricate tube and insert by asking patient to swallow gently or using tongue depressor. Push down till the 50 cm mark (in adults) coincides with upper incisor teeth. Fix mouth gag.
  • Step 3 (Confirmation): Force a little air down the tube with a syringe and listen for bubbling sounds over the stomach with a stethoscope. If the tube enters the trachea, a hissing noise is heard and reflex coughing occurs in a conscious patient.
  • Step 4 (Washing): Pour lavage fluid (≤ 250 ml) and raise funnel to allow fluid to flow in. After a few minutes, lower the funnel and apply suction to siphon contents out. Repeat until fluid is clear, odorless, and same as fluid poured.
  • Step 5 (Antidote): Leave a small quantity of fluid containing antidote / activated charcoal in the stomach to neutralize remaining poison.

C. Activated Charcoal

It is a tasteless, black, fine powder that adsorbs poisons in the stomach and decreases systemic absorption. Given as 1 gm/kg body weight mixed with water.

  • Disadvantages: Unpleasant taste, provokes vomiting, may cause diarrhea/constipation.
  • Contraindications: Paralytic ileus, intestinal obstruction, perforation of stomach.

3. Antidote Administration (2078)

Definition: Antidotes are substances used to counteract or neutralize the effects of poison.

Classification of Antidotes:

  1. Physical or Mechanical or Non-Specific Antidotes: Retard absorption of poison by mechanical action.
    • Examples: Activated charcoal (adsorbs poison into its pores), demulcents such as milk, starch, egg-white / egg albumin (form protective coating on gastric mucosa).
  2. Chemical Antidote: Counteract action of poison by neutralizing it or forming harmless/insoluble compounds.
    • Example: In corrosive alkali poisons, weak vegetable acids like citric acid or lemon juice may be used.
  3. Physiological or Pharmacological Antidote: Act on the tissues of the body via:
    • Reduces toxic conversion: Ethanol in methyl alcohol poisoning (competes for alcohol dehydrogenase).
    • Competition at receptor site: Naloxone in opiate poisoning (competes at opioid receptors).
    • Blocking the receptor site: Atropine in organophosphate poisoning (blocks muscarinic receptors).
    • Chelating action: Interacts with poison to form an inert complex excreted from the body (primarily in heavy metal poisoning). Examples: BAL, Succimer, Disodium edetate, Penicillamine, Desferrioxamine, Calcium disodium edetate.

Universal Antidote (2080)

Used when the nature of poison is unknown or when multiple poisons are taken. It is an obsolete antidote of historical importance.

@Composition (CoMmenT):

  • Charcoal (2 parts): Adsorbs poisons (burnt bread gives charcoal)
  • Magnesium oxide (1 part): Neutralizes acids (milk of magnesia)
  • Tannic acid (1 part): Precipitates alkaloids, certain glycosides and metals (tea has tannic acid)

4. Poison Elimination

Indications: Severe poisoning; Progressive deterioration despite supportive care; High risk of morbidity and mortality; Normal route of excretion impaired; Poison produces delayed but serious toxic effects.

Methods of Elimination:

  • Hemodialysis
  • Hemoperfusion
  • Peritoneal dialysis
  • Plasma perfusion
  • Alkaline diuresis and Acid diuresis
  • Diaphoretics use (e.g., Pilocarpine, Salicylates)

PREVENTION OF POISONING

  1. Education
  2. Keeping drugs and poisons in locked cabinets
  3. Food and poison shouldn't be kept in the same room
  4. All poison products should be kept in their original containers
  5. Keeping caps and tops on bottles closed
  6. Keep poisons away from the reach of children
  7. The label should be read before using
  8. No drug or poison should be used in the dark
  9. Proper disposal of expired or remaining drugs or poisons
  10. Drugs in child-proof packages should only be purchased
  11. Children should be taught not to eat plants or berries
  12. Well ventilation in the kitchen
  13. Special care to emotionally distressed people and mental patients
  14. Prevention of air and water pollution
  15. Occupational safety in exposure occupations like agricultural and factory workers

Punishments Related to Poisoning

  • Intent (mens rea) to cause harm or death using poisons is important in poisoning as in other offences.
  • Murder by poisoning is no different legally from murder by other means.
  • Any act of omission or commission done using poisonous substance as to endanger human life shall be punished with imprisonment up to 6 months or with fine.
  • Whoever administers poison to another person or causes poison to be taken by another person with intent to hurt the other person shall be imprisoned up to 10 years and fined.
  • Adulteration of drugs is punishable with imprisonment up to 6 months.

Ways Poisoning is Used in Criminal Offences

Murder, Injury, Stupefying to facilitate crimes like robbery (e.g., Dhatura, called railroad poison, used in robbery), Criminal abortion, Torture, Careless use.

Duties of a Doctor in Case of Poisoning

  • Medical Duty: Care and treatment to save the life of the patient irrespective of the manner of poisoning.
  • Legal Duty: Assist police to determine the manner of poisoning:
    1. Note preliminary particulars: name, age, sex, occupation, address, date and time, brought by whom, identification marks, and history.
    2. In suspected homicidal poisoning, confirm suspicion before expressing an opinion:
      • Collect and preserve evidences suggestive of poisoning (empty bottles/containers, soiled clothes, remaining food, vomitus, feces, gastric lavage). Deliberate omission to collect or preserve evidence is punishable. Hand over collected exhibits to the Investigating Officer in properly sealed conditions.
      • Observe and record symptoms in relation to food, changes in color, taste, or smell, and other persons affected at the same time.
      • Consult in strict confidence a senior practitioner.
      • Engage trusted nurses to administer medicine/food and allow no one to be alone with the patient. Seek consent for removal of the patient if adult.
    3. If a private practitioner is convinced of homicidal poisoning, he is bound to inform the police or Magistrate.
    4. If sure it is suicidal/accidental poisoning, a private practitioner is not bound to inform police.
    5. If summoned by the investigating officer (I.O.), the doctor is bound to give all information regarding the case.
    6. A government medical officer is required to report all cases of suspected poisoning (accidental, suicidal, or homicidal) to the police.
    7. If condition is serious, arrange to record a dying declaration.
    8. If patient dies and exact diagnosis/cause of death cannot be made out, or if brought dead, inform police and do not issue a death certificate.
    9. Opinions about the nature of poison should only be given after receiving the report from the forensic science laboratory.
    10. Notify Public Health Authorities in cases of food poisoning.
    11. Preserve indoor patient records up to 3 years; provide within 72 hours if requested by relatives.

Note on Drug Automatism: According to this hypothesis, the patient develops a state of toxic delirium after ingestion of one or several doses of a drug (usually depressant drugs, like alcohol), after which he takes additional doses of the drug without realizing it.

MEMORY BOOSTER SUMMARY

Based on Nature of Poison:

  • I. Corrosive:
    • Strong acids: Mineral/inorganic (H2SO4, HCl), Organic (carbolic, acetic, oxalic acid)
    • Strong alkalis: e.g., sodium hydroxide
    • Metals: e.g., mercuric chloride, ferric chloride
  • II. Irritants:
    • Inorganic: Non-metal (phosphorus, iodine), Metal (arsenic, lead)
    • Organic: Animal (snake, scorpion), Plant (castor, calotropis)
    • Mechanical: chopped hair, metal pieces
  • III. Systemic:
    • Cerebral poisons (Somniferous, Inebriants, Stimulants, Deliriant, Depressant, Psychotropics)
    • Spinal poisons (e.g., strychnine)
    • Peripheral nerve poison (e.g., curare)
    • Cardiac poison / cardio-toxic (e.g., aconite)
    • Asphyxiants (e.g., carbon monoxide)
    • Nephrotoxic (e.g., mercury)
    • Hepatotoxic (e.g., phosphorus)
  • IV. Miscellaneous: e.g., food poisoning
