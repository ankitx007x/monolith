General Principles of Identification

Definition

Identification is the determination of the individuality of a person based on certain physical characteristics, i.e., exact fixation of personality.

Types of Identification

  • Complete / Positive / Absolute Identification
    • Establishes the identity of one unique individual.
    • The person cannot be mistaken for anyone else.
    • Based on scientific methods such as fingerprints or DNA.
  • Partial / Incomplete / Presumptive Identification
    • Suggests identity but does not prove it conclusively.
    • Based on matching physical characteristics or circumstantial evidence.
    • Requires confirmation by definitive methods.

When is Identification Required?

  1. Living persons
  2. Recently dead bodies
  3. Decomposed bodies
  4. Mutilated bodies
  5. Skeletal remains
Corpus Delicti ("Body of the Crime")
  • Means the essential facts proving that a crime has occurred.
  • It is not simply the dead body; it refers to proof that a crime was committed.
  • Identification of the victim is often an important part of establishing corpus delicti.
Example If X murders Y, corpus delicti requires proving:
  • The victim is dead.
  • The death resulted from a criminal act.
  • The victim has been correctly identified.
  • The accused caused the death.
High-Yield Viva Points
  • Record at least two identification marks in every medicolegal case.
  • Before identifying an accused in court, verify the same identification marks recorded previously.
  • Visual identification becomes unreliable in:
    • Advanced decomposition
    • Burnt bodies
    • Explosions
    • Mutilation
    • Aircraft crashes
    • Natural disasters

Details of Identification Data

A. Presumptive (Partial) Identification

Suggests identity but does not conclusively prove it.

  • Visual recognition
  • Travel records
    • Flight manifest
    • Passenger lists
    • Tickets
  • Anthropometry (Bertillon's criteria)
  • Personal information
    • Age
    • Sex
    • Marital status
    • Occupation
    • Religion
    • Ethnicity
    • Address
    • Date missing
  • Physical examination
    • Height
    • Weight
    • Skin colour
  • Personal belongings
    • Mobile phone
    • Jewellery
    • Wallet
    • ID card
    • Driving licence
    • Photographs
  • Exclusion of other possible persons

B. Positive (Definitive) Identification

Scientifically proves identity beyond doubt.

  • Fingerprints (Dactylography)
  • Palm prints
  • Footprints
  • Dental records (Dentition)
  • Comparison of ante-mortem and post-mortem radiographs
  • DNA profiling (Gold standard)
Exam Pearls
  • Voice, handwriting, gait, speech, memory and mannerisms are used only for presumptive identification.
  • Comparison of ante-mortem and post-mortem X-rays is possible only in dead bodies.
  • Primary characteristics of identification:
    • Age
    • Sex
    • Stature
    • Race/Ancestry

Principles

  • The proportion of body parts differs from person to person.

Microscopic Sex Determination

1. Barr Body (Sex Chromatin)

  • Condensed inactive X chromosome.
  • Seen as a dark basophilic body attached to the inner nuclear membrane.
  • Present in females.
  • Usually absent in normal males.
  • Common specimen: Buccal mucosal smear.

2. Davidson Body (Drumstick Body)

  • Small drumstick-shaped nuclear projection on neutrophils.
  • Seen in females.
  • Measures approximately 1.5 μm.
  • Diagnosis requires drumsticks in approximately 6% of neutrophils.
  • Specimen: Peripheral blood smear.

3. Definitive Laboratory Methods

  • Karyotyping
  • Y-chromosome demonstration
  • DNA analysis (most accurate)
High-Yield Viva Points
  • Barr body = inactive X chromosome.
  • Best sample for Barr body → Buccal smear.
  • Davidson body is found in neutrophils.
  • DNA profiling is the most accurate method for sex determination.

Tanner Staging (Sexual Maturity Rating)

Tanner staging (Sexual Maturity Rating - SMR) is a system used to assess the progression of puberty based on the development of secondary sexual characteristics. It consists of 5 stages (I–V).

Remember:
  • Boys: Genital development (G) + Pubic hair (PH)
  • Girls: Breast development (B) + Pubic hair (PH)

Tanner Staging in Boys (Genitalia & Pubic Hair)

Stage Genital Development (G) Pubic Hair (PH)
Stage I
  • Pre-pubertal
  • Testes, scrotum and penis are child-like
  • No pubic hair
Stage II
  • Testes and scrotum enlarge
  • Scrotal skin becomes thin, red and wrinkled
  • Minimal penile enlargement
  • Few, lightly pigmented hairs
  • Straight or slightly curly
  • Mainly at the base of the penis
Stage III
  • Penis increases mainly in length
  • Further enlargement of testes and scrotum
  • Darker, coarser and curlier hair
  • Spreads over the pubic region
Stage IV
  • Penis increases in length and breadth
  • Glans develops
  • Scrotum becomes darker
  • Adult-type hair
  • Not yet spread to medial thighs
Stage V
  • Adult genitalia
  • Adult distribution
  • Extends to medial thighs

Tanner Staging in Girls (Breast & Pubic Hair)

Stage Breast Development (B) Pubic Hair (PH)
Stage I
  • Pre-pubertal
  • No glandular tissue
  • Only nipple elevated
  • No pubic hair
Stage II
  • Breast bud appears
  • Small mound forms
  • Areola enlarges
  • Sparse, lightly pigmented hair
  • Mainly along the labia
Stage III
  • Further enlargement of breast and areola
  • No separation of contours
  • Darker, curlier and coarser hair
  • Spreads over pubis
Stage IV
  • Areola and nipple project above breast
  • Secondary mound formed
  • Adult-type hair
  • Not yet on medial thighs
Stage V
  • Adult breast
  • Secondary mound disappears
  • Only nipple projects
  • Adult female distribution
  • Extends to medial thighs
Approximate Age of Tanner Stages
Stage Girls Boys
I <10 years <11 years
II 10–11 years 11–12 years
III 11–13 years 13–14 years
IV 13–15 years 14–15 years
V 15 years or older 16 years or older

These ages are approximate and show normal variation.

High-Yield Viva Points
  • Tanner staging is also called Sexual Maturity Rating (SMR).
  • Assessment is based on secondary sexual characteristics, not chronological age.
  • Boys: Genital development + Pubic hair.
  • Girls: Breast development + Pubic hair.
  • Stage I = Pre-pubertal.
  • Stage V = Fully mature adult development.
  • Tanner staging is useful in:
    • Age estimation
    • Assessment of delayed puberty
    • Assessment of precocious puberty
    • Pediatric and forensic examinations

Age Estimation — Dentition

Dentition

  • Humans develop two sets of teeth during life.
  • Temporary (Deciduous / Milk)
    • Total teeth = 20
    • No premolars
  • Permanent
    • Total teeth = 32
    • Includes premolars and third molars

Dental Formula

Temporary 2 Incisors : 1 Canine : 0 Premolars : 2 Molars 20
Permanent 2 Incisors : 1 Canine : 2 Premolars : 3 Molars 32
Remember
  • Milk teeth have no premolars.
  • Permanent teeth have 2 premolars and 3 molars per quadrant.

Mixed Dentition (6–11 Years)

  • Mixed dentition is the period when both temporary and permanent teeth are present.
  • Occurs between 6 and 11 years of age.
  • Age can be estimated by counting the erupted permanent teeth.
Age Estimation Formula

Age (years) = (Number of Permanent Teeth ÷ 4) + 5

Gustafson's Method (Adult Dentition)

  • Used for individuals older than 21 years.
  • Based on age-related changes in a single tooth.
  • Each feature is scored from 0 to 3.
  • Average error: ± 3.6 years.

2. Ossification of Bones
  1. It is an important criterion for the determination of the age of an individual.
  2. The earliest centre of ossification appears at the end of the 2nd month of intra-uterine life.
  3. The process of ossification centers appearance and fusion (i.e. union of epiphysis and diaphysis) has a sequence. In general, ossification begins centrally in an epiphysis and spreads peripherally as it gets bigger.
  4. Most bones are ossified from several separate centres (primary centres), one of which appears near the middle of the future bone. This centre is concerned with progressive ossification towards the bone ends. The end regions are ossified by separate multiple centres, called secondary centres.
  5. Some bones are ossified from a single centre such as carpal and tarsal bones.
  6. The appearance and fusion of secondary centres of important bones in various joints are given below:

General Rules of Ossification & Age Determination:
  1. Ossification occurs earlier in females as compared to males by about one year.
  2. The aging of bones is more accurate with respect to the appearance of the centres of ossification than the union of epiphysis. Thus their reliability in accurately determining the age is variable and it increases with age. It depends on health, hereditary, endocrine, nutritional, and environmental factors.
  3. In general, the secondary centres of the extremities bones that appear first are the last to fuse.
  4. 16 years: The centres around elbow joints fuse.
  5. 17 years: The centres at hip joint and around ankle joint fuse.
  6. 18 years: The centres around shoulder joint and knee joint fuse.
  7. 19 years: The centres around wrist joint fuse.
Ossification of Mandible
  • Mandible ossifies in dense fibro-membranous tissue.
  • Each half ossifies from one centre which appears near mental foramen about the 6th week after birth (just after the appearance of primary centre for clavicle).
  • Two halves of mandible join from below upwards in the first year, but a trace of separation near the alveolar margin may still be visible at the beginning of the 2nd year.
  • The head of mandible completely ossifies by the 3rd decade (21-30 years).
Ossification of Skull
  • Fontanelles:
    • Lateral and occipital fontanelles close within the first 2 months.
    • Posterior fontanelle closes in 6 to 8 months.
    • The anterior fontanelle closes between 1.5 to 2 years.
  • The condylar portion of the occipital bone fuses with squama at the third year, and with basioccipital at the 5th year.
  • The basioccipital fuses with the basisphenoid at about 18-21 years.
  • Vault of skull: The closure of sutures begins on inner side 5 to 10 years earlier than on the outer side. The coronal, sagittal, and lambdoid sutures start to close on their inner side at about the age of 25 years.
  • On the outer side, fusion occurs in the following order:
    • Posterior one-third of sagittal suture at about 30-40 years.
    • Anterior one-third of sagittal and lower half of coronal at about 40-50 years.
    • Middle sagittal and upper half of coronal at about 50-60 years.
    • The lambdoid suture starts closing near the lambda and union is often completed at about 45 years.

Rules for Foetal Age Calculation from Crown-Heel (C-H) Length:
  • Haase's Rule (Up to 5 months): Length (cm) = (Age in lunar months)2
  • Morrison's Rule (More than 5 months): Length (cm) = 5 × (Age in lunar months)
Mnemonic: Haase's comes before Morrison's alphabetically, so up to 5 months use Haase's formula, and after 5 months use Morrison's rule.

GENERAL DEVELOPMENT AND STATURE

If the body has been dismembered, the approximate stature may be determined by:

  1. The length from the tip of the middle finger to the tip of the opposite middle finger, when arms are fully extended, closely equals the height.
  2. Twice the length of one arm with 30 cm added for two clavicles, and four cm for the sternum is equals to the height.
  3. The length of forearm measured from tip of olecranon process to tip of the middle finger is equal to 5/19 of the stature.
  4. The length from the vertex to the symphysis pubis is roughly half of stature. After 14 years of age the symphysis pubis lies about halfway up the body. Before 14 years the trunk is longer than the lower limbs.
  5. The height of head measured by the vertical distance from the top of the head to the tip of the chin is about one-seventh and the length of skull is about one-eighth of the total height.
  6. The length from the sternal notch to symphysis pubis multiplied by 3.3 gives the stature.
  7. The length of vertebral column is 35/100 of the height.
  8. To the length of entire skeleton, add 2.5 cm for the thickness of the soft parts.
  9. Maximum foot length divided by 0.15 gives stature.

HAIRS

  • Trichology is the study of hair.
  • Hair grows at the rate of 0.4 mm/day.
  • The examination of the hair is undertaken to fine out:
    1. Hair or some other fiber?
      Hair consists of bulb or root and a shaft. Considerable force is required to pluck out a lot of healthy growing hair from scalp. An adult can be lifted or dragged by the hair and the scalp may even be torn from the skull.
      In most hair, there are three well-defined layers:
      1. CUTICLE: This is the outer layer and consists of thin, non-pigmented scales.
      2. CORTEX: This is the middle layer and consists of longitudinally arranged, elongated cells without nuclei. Within these cells are fibrils on which there may be granules of pigment.
      3. MEDULLA: This is the inner layer composed of keratinized remains of cells.
    2. Human or animal?
    3. From what part of the body derived?
    4. Sex? @ barr bodies
    5. Age?
    6. Has the hair been altered by dyeing, bleaching or diseases?
    7. Is the hair identical to the hair of the victim or the suspect?
    8. Did it fall naturally or was it forcibly removed?
    9. What is the cause of injury?

Medicolegal Importance of Hair

  1. Hair is important in crime investigation, for it remains identifiable on the clothes, body and the alleged weapons in crimes committed long before. It often provides the only connection between a weapon or even the accused and the victim of an assault.
    1. Motor vehicles responsible for injuries may be identified by the detection of hair on the vehicle.
    2. In rape and sodomy, the pubic hair of the accused may be found on the victim or vice versa. In bestiality, animal hair may be found on the body or underclothing of the accused, of his pubic hair may be found about the genitals of the animal.
    3. Stains on the hair may indicate the nature of the assault, e.g., mud stains in struggle, seminal stains in sexual offences, salivary stains in asphyxial deaths, blood stains in injury, etc. Stains may be got from the walls, doors, furniture, etc., and may indicate the scene of crime.
  2. Nature of weapon can be made out from the injuries to the hair, and hair bulb.
  3. Hair is useful in identification especially when there has been some known peculiarity of the hair, dyeing, bleaching or artificial waving.
  4. Age of a person may be determined from the growth of hair on different parts of body.
  5. Sex may be determined from their distribution on body, texture and from Barr bodies.
  6. Singeing of the hair indicates burns or a close range firearm injury.
  7. It is helpful in differentiating scalds from burns.
  8. In chronic poisoning with heavy metals, e.g., arsenic, the poison can be detected in the hair.
  9. The time of death can sometimes be determined from the length of the hair on the face.

DEFORMITIES AND OTHER PECULIARITIES

  1. Congenital deformities, such as cleft lip and cleft palate, bilobed ear, polydactyly (more than five fingers in the hand or foot), web-fingers or toes etc. can often be very helpful in the identification of the dead body, even if spoiled due to putrefaction.
  2. Moles or Nevus:
    1. These are pigmented grey to brown or jet black pinpoint spots over the skin. They are usually situated on the face, neck or back. These properties make them a very good identificating feature.
    2. A mole is a congenital condition, but it may appear in an adult life.
  3. Birth marks, like capillary haemangioma (network of capillaries filled with blood) may be present in a body since birth. Common sites are: skin, mucus membrane of the nose, lip, tongue or gum.
Note: For Role of Blood in Identification refer to Forensic Science Laboratory.

SCARS

  1. Scars is a fibrous tissue covered by epithelium devoid of hair follicles, elastic tissue, sebaceous glands, sweat glands and pigment.
  2. It is usually produced after the healing of a wound which involves the dermis. If the wound involves only epidermis, it will regenerate without the formation of a scar.
  3. Scars once formed are permanent. That is why they serve as good identification marks.
  4. The shape of a scar is variable and depends upon the causative factors. Sometimes from the shape of the scar, indeed one can reasonably infer the type of injury caused, or even the sequence of events occurring just before the causation of injuries.
    Example:
    1. An incised wound leaves a linear scar. (Exception: incised wound in Axilla or genitals is irregular due to the loose fold of skin.)
    2. A lacerated wound and a suppurating wound leave a prominent, irregular, firm scar.
Note:
• Scars sustained in infancy or childhood grow with the growth of a child.
• In a dead body, faint scars can be detected more easily by microscopy after staining the tissue with elastic tissue stain.
• As scars lack elastic tissue, no elastic tissue staining would be seen under the microscope.
• The histology of scar does not help in the determination of its age.
• The scar can surgically be removed by excision and skin grafting.

Age of Scars

  1. One week: When a clean linear wound is inflicted (i.e., a surgical operation or a knife slash), the edges become mechanically strong within one week. At this stage, the scar is angry looking reddish scar.
  2. Two weeks: Scars begin to become pale. However, these retain their sensitivity. No further change takes place until about two months.
  3. Two-six months: Scar becomes white and glistening. It may be tough and wrinkled. Thereafter the scar may remain like that indefinitely, without undergoing any major change.

Medicolegal Importance of Scars

  1. They form important marks for identification of a person.
  2. The shape of the scar may indicate the nature of weapon or agent that caused the injury.
  3. The age of the scar is important in a criminal offence. If the age of a wound corresponds with the date of the attack it may have value as circumstantial evidence.
  4. Linea albicans may indicate previous pregnancy.
  5. If a person is disfigured due to scars, it becomes a grievous (अंग भंग) hurt.
  6. Scar causing contracture at a joint restricting movement or functions of the joint becomes grievous hurt.
  7. The accused may attribute scars of wounds to disease or therapeutic procedures.
  8. To charge an enemy with assault, a person may claim that scars due to disease are those of wounds.

TATTOO MARKS

Tattoo are special marks, designs, pictorial diagrams (e.g. of gods and goddesses), alphabetical messages (e.g. name of self, husband or friend, etc.) made or written permanently on the skin of the body.

Medicolegal Importance

  1. Identity: If there is large number of tattoos, positive identification can be made by tattoos alone. Initials and dates, regimental or nautical details, identity numerals, one's own name, etc., provide more scientific basis for identification.
  2. Religion
  3. God of worship
  4. Indicate culture or life-style.
  5. The distribution of tattooing and the nature of designs and figures may indicate a particular country or region.
  6. They reflect travel, history, war, occupation, sex interest, etc.
  7. Gang members may wear a tattoo of allegiance and symbolism to indicate status or other aspects relevant to their particular group.
  8. Illicit drug users may have tattoos that identify them as belonging to a particular group or to obscure injection sites.
  9. Homosexuals may tattoo on the back of hand between base of thumb and forefinger.

OCCUPATIONAL MARKS (OR STIGMATA)

  1. Occupational marks or stigmata are special marks on the body of a person, produced as a result of his profession or habits. These may sometimes be helpful in identifying a person.
  2. Cuts, pen-callosities (hardness of skin), scars and hyperkeratosis (horny and roughness) of hands indicate that the person is a manual worker (carpenter, blacksmith, typist, etc).
    In olden days, when clerks used to write with rough pens grasped between their fingers, they used to develop callosities within their fingers, especially in the last joint of the right middle finger. These were known as "pen callosities".
  3. A depression on the sternum is often found in shoemakers due to the constant pressure of the "shoemaker's last" against it.
  4. Tailors may also have multiple needle prick marks on their hands.
  5. Dyers, photographers, engineers and chemists may have their hands stained with various dyes and chemicals.
  6. The hair of an extractor of copper may be greenish, while that of indigo workers and cobalt miners may be bluish.
  7. Coal miners usually show blue scars over their hands and faces which are caused by the coal dust entering small lacerations.

DACTYLOGRAPHY (FINGERPRINTS)

  • Dactylography (taken from two Greek words daktylos meaning 'finger' and graphein meaning 'to write') refers to fingerprinting. Dactylography refers to the study of patterns formed by papillary or epidermal ridges present on bulbs of fingertips called fingerprints.
  • Fingerprints are formed in the 5th month of intrauterine life. It is the most important and accurate method of positive identification.
  • Sir William Herschel (in India) and Henry Faulds (in Japan) were the first persons who started using fingerprints.
  • Sir Francis Galton went further and established fingerprinting as the unique method of identification. The first Fingerprint Bureau in the world was started in Kolkata in 1897.

Principle:

It is based on the principle that fingerprints are the impressions of patterns formed by papillary or epidermal ridges present on bulbs of fingertips and these patterns are constant for a person and persists throughout the life.

These patterns are identical to a single individual only and do not match even in identical twins.

Each millimeter of papillary ridge contains 9-18 pores which are actually opening of sweat glands. Sweats secreted from these pores when mixed with sebum secreted from sebaceous glands forms the impressions of fingerprints.

Thus, whenever fingers of a live person come in contact with a certain object, it leaves impression on them. This is in accordance with Locard's principle of exchange.

Note: It has been estimated that chances of two person having identical fingerprints is about 1 in 64 billion.

Classification

  1. Loops (60-70%)
    1. Radial
    2. Ulnar
  2. Whorls (25-35%)
    1. Concentric
    2. Spiral
    3. Double spiral
    4. Almond shaped
  3. Arches (6-7%)
    1. Plain
    2. Tented
    3. Exceptional
  4. Composite (1-2%)
    1. Central pocket loops
    2. Lateral pocket loops
    3. Twinned loops
    4. Accidentals

Identification:

The final identification of fingerprint is not by comparing the patterns, but it is done by comparing many details which occur throughout the ridges like ridge endings, bifurcations, lake and island formations.

The range of the number of matching points for positive identification varies between as little as 7 to 8 prints and a maximum of 16 prints.

Techniques of fingerprinting:

At first, hands are washed, cleaned and dried. Then, fingerprints are recorded on an unglazed white paper using printer's ink. There are two methods of taking impression:

  1. Plain impression: A plain imprint is taken by applying ink to the tip of fingers and placing the fingers directly on paper.
  2. Rolled impression: It is taken by rolling the fingers on paper from outward to inward direction in such a way, so as to obtain an impression of whole tip. This is a better technique as it provides complete impression.

Types of fingerprints:

  1. The latent print (chance print) is an invisible or barely visible impression left on a smooth surface.
  2. Visible prints are formed by fingers stained with blood or ink or other medium.
  3. The plastic print is an impression made on a soft surface, such as soap, cheese, mud, pitch, candles, thick dried blood, adhesives, etc.
Note:
1. In criminal cases, impressions of all the ten fingers are taken, but for civil purposes, the left thumb impression only is taken.
2. Fingerprinting is a better method of identification than even DNA profiling. In identical twins, DNA profiling would be the same but fingerprints are different.
3. Fingerprints can be sent to all parts of the world by wireless telegraphy.
4. Computers can also scan and digitally encode fingerprints, which is called Automatic Fingerprints Identification System (AFIS).
5. Galton mathematically worked out the number of possible variations and he came up with the figure of 64 billion different fingerprints. He also gave the concept of minutia (meaning small details). Figure, which he mentioned as a permanent feature of fingerprints, does not change during a person's lifetime, are thus used as definitive parameters for identification purposes.

Collection of Fingerprints

Steps:

  1. Dusting of Prints: Done for latent prints. Dusting of the prints involves applying the fine particles of various coloured substances so that may give a distinct colour to the prints which makes them easily visible. The various coloured substances used are iodine, ninhydrin, silver nitrate, etc.
  2. Lifting of Prints: Lifting of prints is done by making a copy of the prints for transfer to the laboratory where these can be examined. This involves various techniques like photography, using adhesive tapes, laser, etc.
Note:
1. The burnt skin of fingers, on healing, shows the original fingerprints pattern.
2. Even in decomposed bodies, fingerprints can be taken from the dermis after epidermis is lost.
3. In a dead body, if rigor mortis is well developed, incision can be given at proximal interphalangeal joint on palmar surface to enable the fingers to be straight.
4. Fingerprint impression may persist for years, if not disturbed by cleaning.

Medicolegal Importance

  1. The recognition of impression left at a scene of crime, e.g., on weapons, furniture, doors, utensils, clothes, etc establish the identity of the criminal.
  2. The identification of suicides, deserters, persons suffering from loss of memory or those dead of unconscious after being involved in an accident and of decomposing bodies.
  3. Identification in case of accidental exchange of newborn infants.
  4. The prevention of impersonation.
  5. To maintain identity records.
  6. Cheques, bank notes and other legal documents can bear a fingerprint.

Mutilation of fingerprints:

  1. Fingerprints are not destroyed unless true skin is completely destroyed. Criminal may self inflict wounds or burn, apply corrosives on fingers but some parts are always left unaffected. So, they are not easily mutilated unless grafts are placed.
  2. Conditions where fingerprints are mutilated are:
    Temporary: Coeliac disease, Dermatitis, Eczema, Acanthosis nigricans, Scleroderma, Dry or sclerotic skin
    Permanent: Leprosy, Electric injury, Radiation exposure
  3. Patterns are not altered but distance between the ridges may be altered in: Infantile paralysis, Rickets, Acromegaly

Poroscopy

  1. It is the study of the impression of sweat pores. These sweat pores are there on the friction ridges of the palmar and plantar surfaces of hands and feet respectively.
  2. The pores (9-18 in each mm), like the ridges used in fingerprinting, are permanent, unchanged and individual, and so form a strong basis to be used for fingerprinting, especially in those cases where sufficient ridge patterns are not available for any reason.
  3. Characteristic of pores that are used for identification:
    • Number of pores
    • Distance between pores
    • Size of pores
    • Shape of pores
    • Position of pores on the ridge
    Developed by Edmund Locard.

FOOTPRINT (PODOGRAM)

  • Footprint, like fingerprints, are peculiar to an individual.
  • Therefore, these are also used for positive identification.
  • Their importance can be judged from the fact that (in hospitals) the footprints of the newborn babies are taken so that in case of mixing up of babies, these prints may be matched.
  • But difficulty is that you don't have another footprints of assailant recorded to compare.

OTHER PRINTS

A. Palatoscopy (Palatal Rugoscopy)

  1. It is the study of palatal rugae to establish the identity of a person.
  2. The anterior part of the palate has rough raised ridges (palatal rugae) which are different in every individual and quite prominent in males.
  3. Palatal rugae are irregular, asymmetrical ridges of mucous membrane on the palate and more on the left side.
  4. These rugae are formed in the third month of intrauterine life and do not undergo any change except in length.
  5. A print of palatal rugae is called palato-print, which can be used and matched for positive identification.
  6. Advantages: Especially useful in cases where there are no fingers to be studied, as in the case of decomposed bodies, severely burnt bodies, deaths by explosion, etc.
Note:
1. Palate, being surrounded by tough anatomical structures, survives destruction by fires or explosions.
2. Palate also resists decomposition.

B. Lip prints (Cheiloscopy)

  1. Like fingerprints, lip prints have several patterns which are different in any two individuals.
  2. These patterns are identified and then matched to obtain a positive identification.
  3. Lip prints are not fully established yet and are not routinely used.
  4. However, in cases where mouth and lips have come in contact with the crime scene (such as a criminal drinking from a glass at the scene of crime), lip prints may be used for criminal investigation.

Classification: These are mainly of five types:

  1. Type I represents the lip possessing full vertical grooves.
  2. Type II pronounced "one-dash", has partial grooves running vertically on the lip.
  3. Type II represents branched grooves.
  4. Type III represents intersected (diamond) grooves that look similar to crosses.
  5. Type IV represents the reticular (rectangular) pattern similar to wire mesh or boxes.

C. Ear prints

Like fingerprints, the shape of the pinna (oval, round, triangular or rectangular) and various anatomical landmarks are matched after taking a print and comparison with those lifted from the scene of crime.

SUPERIMPOSITION

  1. Superimposition is the technique of reconstructing the face of an individual where only the skeleton or a badly damaged face is available.
  2. This is based on the fact that soft tissues have a set amount of thickness at different parts of the body which helps reconstruct the face.
  3. Techniques:
    1. Video or photographic comparison: Here the actual video or photograph of a person is compared with the video or photograph available from the scene of crime (in cases, where CCTV footage is available).
    2. Skull photo superimposition: In this technique, the photograph or video of a person is superimposed on the skull that is available.
      This technique is useful especially when there is a reasonable degree of certainty that the skull found belongs to a particular person and he leads the investigating authorities to the site where he had buried the body.
    3. Computerized assisted superimposition

Skull-Photo Superimposition

  • This technique was applied by Glaister and Brash in 1935 in Ruxton case.
  • Superimposition is the technique applied to determine whether the skull is that of the person in the photograph.
  • The photograph need not to be front view of the face; even lateral and semi-lateral view of face can be used.

VII. Forensic DNA Profiling

DNA profiling identifies an individual by analysing Short Tandem Repeats (STRs), which are highly variable DNA sequences.

Important Principle
  • DNA can establish identity, biological relationship, or source of biological material.
  • DNA evidence alone does not prove criminal guilt; it only links a person to a biological sample.

Common Sources of DNA

  • Blood
  • Semen
  • Saliva
  • Hair with intact root
  • Bone
  • Teeth
  • Soft tissues
  • Nails

Collection & Preservation of DNA Samples

Sample Collection & Preservation
Liquid Blood
  • Absorb on sterile gauze/cotton
  • Air-dry completely
  • Pack in a paper bag or envelope
Blood-stained Clothes/Fabrics
  • Air-dry away from direct sunlight
  • Pack each item separately in paper
Soft Tissue
  • Collect approximately 100 g
  • Store in chilled normal saline surrounded by ice
Bones & Teeth
  • Preserve fresh bones in crystal salt
  • Send dry long bones (preferably femur)
  • Alternatively send 2–3 molar teeth
Swabs
  • Allow to air-dry completely
  • Store in paper envelopes
Hair
  • Collect hairs with intact roots
  • Store dry in paper envelopes
Golden Rules for DNA Preservation
  • Always air-dry moist samples.
  • Use paper, not plastic, for packaging.
  • Keep samples cool and dry.
  • Avoid contamination by using sterile instruments and gloves.
  • Label every specimen properly and maintain chain of custody.
