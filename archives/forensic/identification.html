General Principles of Identification

Definition

Identification is the determination of the individuality of a person based on certain physical characteristics, i.e., exact fixation of personality.

Types of Identification

  • Complete / Positive / Absolute Identification
    • Establishes the identity of one unique individual.
    • The person cannot be mistaken for anyone else.
    • Based on scientific methods such as fingerprints or DNA.
  • Partial / Incomplete / Presumptive Identification
    • Suggests identity but does not prove it conclusively.
    • Based on matching physical characteristics or circumstantial evidence.
    • Requires confirmation by definitive methods.

When is Identification Required?

  1. Living persons
  2. Recently dead bodies
  3. Decomposed bodies
  4. Mutilated bodies
  5. Skeletal remains
Corpus Delicti ("Body of the Crime")
  • Means the essential facts proving that a crime has occurred.
  • It is not simply the dead body; it refers to proof that a crime was committed.
  • Identification of the victim is often an important part of establishing corpus delicti.
Example If X murders Y, corpus delicti requires proving:
  • The victim is dead.
  • The death resulted from a criminal act.
  • The victim has been correctly identified.
  • The accused caused the death.
High-Yield Viva Points
  • Record at least two identification marks in every medicolegal case.
  • Before identifying an accused in court, verify the same identification marks recorded previously.
  • Visual identification becomes unreliable in:
    • Advanced decomposition
    • Burnt bodies
    • Explosions
    • Mutilation
    • Aircraft crashes
    • Natural disasters

Suggests identity but does not conclusively prove it.

  • Visual recognition
  • Travel records
    • Flight manifest
    • Passenger lists
    • Tickets
  • Anthropometry (Bertillon's criteria)
  • Personal information
    • Age
    • Sex
    • Marital status
    • Occupation
    • Religion
    • Ethnicity
    • Address
    • Date missing
  • Physical examination
    • Height
    • Weight
    • Skin colour
  • Personal belongings
    • Mobile phone
    • Jewellery
    • Wallet
    • ID card
    • Driving licence
    • Photographs
  • Exclusion of other possible persons

B. Positive (Definitive) Identification

Scientifically proves identity beyond doubt.

  • Fingerprints (Dactylography)
  • Palm prints
  • Footprints
  • Dental records (Dentition)
  • Comparison of ante-mortem and post-mortem radiographs
  • DNA profiling (Gold standard)
Exam Pearls
  • Voice, handwriting, gait, speech, memory and mannerisms are used only for presumptive identification.
  • Comparison of ante-mortem and post-mortem X-rays is possible only in dead bodies.
  • Primary characteristics of identification:
    • Age
    • Sex
    • Stature
    • Race/Ancestry

  • The proportion of body parts differs from person to person.

  2. Identification Marks

  3. Body Measurements

    • Sitting height
    • Head length & width
    • Right ear length & width

Microscopic Sex Determination

1. Barr Body (Sex Chromatin)

  • Condensed inactive X chromosome.
  • Seen as a dark basophilic body attached to the inner nuclear membrane.
  • Present in females.
  • Usually absent in normal males.
  • Common specimen: Buccal mucosal smear.

2. Davidson Body (Drumstick Body)

  • Small drumstick-shaped nuclear projection on neutrophils.
  • Seen in females.
  • Measures approximately 1.5 μm.
  • Diagnosis requires drumsticks in approximately 6% of neutrophils.
  • Specimen: Peripheral blood smear.

3. Definitive Laboratory Methods

  • Karyotyping
  • Y-chromosome demonstration
  • DNA analysis (most accurate)
High-Yield Viva Points
  • Barr body = inactive X chromosome.
  • Best sample for Barr body → Buccal smear.
  • Davidson body is found in neutrophils.
  • DNA profiling is the most accurate method for sex determination.

Intersex Conditions

Condition Description
Gonadal Agenesis Testes or ovaries fail to develop; nuclear sex is negative.
Gonadal Dysgenesis External genitalia are present, but gonads fail to develop properly at puberty.
True Hermaphroditism Presence of both ovarian and testicular tissue in the same individual.
Pseudo-hermaphroditism Gonads belong to one sex, while external genitalia resemble the opposite sex.

Age Estimation — Dentition

Dentition

  • Humans develop two sets of teeth during life.
  • Temporary (Deciduous / Milk)
    • Total teeth = 20
    • No premolars
  • Permanent
    • Total teeth = 32
    • Includes premolars and third molars

Dental Formula

Dentition Dental Formula (Per Quadrant) Total Teeth
Temporary 2 Incisors : 1 Canine : 0 Premolars : 2 Molars 20
Permanent 2 Incisors : 1 Canine : 2 Premolars : 3 Molars 32
Remember
  • Milk teeth have no premolars.
  • Permanent teeth have 2 premolars and 3 molars per quadrant.

Mixed Dentition (6–11 Years)

  • Mixed dentition is the period when both temporary and permanent teeth are present.
  • Occurs between 6 and 11 years of age.
  • Age can be estimated by counting the erupted permanent teeth.
Age Estimation Formula

Age (years) = (Number of Permanent Teeth ÷ 4) + 5

Gustafson's Method (Adult Dentition)

  • Used for individuals older than 21 years.
  • Based on age-related changes in a single tooth.
  • Each feature is scored from 0 to 3.
  • Average error: ± 3.6 years.

Trichology (Study of Hair)

Average growth rate: 0.4 mm/day

Structure of Hair

  1. Cuticle
    • Outermost layer
    • Thin, transparent, non-pigmented scales
  2. Cortex
    • Middle layer
    • Contains pigment granules
    • Made of elongated, non-nucleated cells
  3. Medulla
    • Innermost core
    • Contains keratinized cell remnants
Medicolegal Importance of Hair
  • Highly resistant to decomposition.
  • Links suspect, victim and weapon (Locard's exchange principle).
  • Useful in chronic heavy metal poisoning (e.g. arsenic).
  • Singeing indicates burns or close-range firearm injury.
  • Helps determine:
    • Species
    • Body site of origin
    • Sex (Barr bodies in root cells)
    • Nature of assault

IV. Scars, Tattoos & Occupational Marks

Scars (Cicatrix)

A scar (cicatrix) is fibrous tissue formed after healing of a wound that extends into the dermis.

Characteristics

  • Covered by epithelium.
  • Lacks:
    • Hair follicles
    • Elastic fibres
    • Pigment cells
    • Sweat glands
    • Sebaceous glands

Age of a Scar

Age Appearance
1 week Strong, reddish ("angry"), vascular
2 weeks Paler; remains sensitive for about 2 months
2–6 months White, glistening, tough and permanently fixed
Remember
  • Scar forms only when the wound reaches the dermis.
  • Mature scar → White, glistening and fixed.

Other Identification Marks

Mark Medicolegal Importance
Moles (Nevi) Congenital or acquired pigmented lesions; useful for positive identification.
Birth Marks Usually capillary haemangiomas involving skin or mucous membrane.
Tattoos Permanent pigment deposited in the dermis; helps identify occupation, religion, culture, gang affiliation or drug abuse.
Occupational Marks Characteristic marks produced by long-term occupational habits.

Common Occupational Stigmata

  • Pen callosity → Writers / Clerks
  • Sternal depression → Shoemakers
  • Multiple needle pricks → Tailors
  • Blue-black skin staining → Coal miners
  • Green hair discoloration → Copper workers

V. Dactylography & Biometric Identification

Dactylography (Fingerprints)

  • Study of epidermal ridge patterns of the fingertips.
  • Develop during the 5th month of intrauterine life.
  • Remain permanent throughout life.
  • No two individuals have identical fingerprints, including identical twins.
Why Fingerprints are Ideal for Identification
  • Unique
  • Permanent
  • Easy to record
  • Remain unchanged throughout life

Fingerprint Patterns

Pattern Frequency Types
Loops 60–70% Radial, Ulnar
Whorls 25–35% Concentric, Spiral, Double spiral, Almond
Arches 6–7% Plain, Tented, Exceptional
Composites 1–2% Central pocket, Lateral pocket, Twinned, Accidental
Easy to Remember
  • Loops → Most common.
  • Composites → Rarest.

Other Biometric Methods

Method Application
Poroscopy Study of sweat pores along fingerprint ridges (Locard).
Podogram Footprints; useful for newborn identification.
Palatoscopy Study of palatal rugae; resistant to burns and decomposition.
Cheiloscopy Study of lip-print patterns.
Retinal / Iris Scan Biometric identification using retinal vessels or iris pattern.
Frequently Asked Viva Questions
  • Poroscopy → Locard.
  • Podogram → Identification of newborns.
  • Palatoscopy → Palatal rugae.
  • Cheiloscopy → Lip prints.
  • Retinal pattern may change in Diabetes Mellitus.

Bite Mark Analysis
  • Bite marks are patterned injuries caused by teeth.
  • Usually appear as abrasions or contusions.
  • Human bite → Circular or oval.
  • Animal bite → Narrow U-shaped.
  • Can establish:
    • Direct physical contact
    • Identity of the assailant (when corroborated with other evidence)

  • Scar forms only after injury reaches the dermis.
  • Loops are the most common fingerprint pattern.
  • Composites are the least common.
  • Fingerprints develop during the 5th month of intrauterine life.
  • Poroscopy → Sweat pores.
  • Palatoscopy → Palatal rugae.
  • Cheiloscopy → Lip prints.
  • Podogram → Footprints.
  • Human bite → Circular/Oval.

Forensic Anthropology Approach to Unknown Skeletal Remains

Unknown skeletal remains should be examined in a systematic sequence:

Step Question How it is Determined
1 Bone or Artifact?
  • Look for nutrient foramina/canals
  • Look for muscle attachment markings
  • Confirm by histological examination if required
2 Human or Animal?
  • Comparative morphology
  • Precipitin test
  • DNA analysis
3 One Individual or More?
  • Separate bones by size and morphology
  • Look for duplication of bones
  • UV fluorescence if required
4 Who was the Individual?
  • Age → Dentition & ossification
  • Sex → Pelvis and skull
  • Stature → Long bones
  • Race (where applicable)
5 How and When did Death Occur?
  • Fractures and injuries
  • Weapon marks
  • Cause of death
  • Time since death
Quick Sequence

Bone → Human → Number → Age → Sex → Stature → Cause of Death → Time Since Death

VII. Forensic DNA Profiling

DNA profiling identifies an individual by analysing Short Tandem Repeats (STRs), which are highly variable DNA sequences.

Important Principle
  • DNA can establish identity, biological relationship, or source of biological material.
  • DNA evidence alone does not prove criminal guilt; it only links a person to a biological sample.

Common Sources of DNA

  • Blood
  • Semen
  • Saliva
  • Hair with intact root
  • Bone
  • Teeth
  • Soft tissues
  • Nails

Collection & Preservation of DNA Samples

Sample Collection & Preservation
Liquid Blood
  • Absorb on sterile gauze/cotton
  • Air-dry completely
  • Pack in a paper bag or envelope
Blood-stained Clothes/Fabrics
  • Air-dry away from direct sunlight
  • Pack each item separately in paper
Soft Tissue
  • Collect approximately 100 g
  • Store in chilled normal saline surrounded by ice
Bones & Teeth
  • Preserve fresh bones in crystal salt
  • Send dry long bones (preferably femur)
  • Alternatively send 2–3 molar teeth
Swabs
  • Allow to air-dry completely
  • Store in paper envelopes
Hair
  • Collect hairs with intact roots
  • Store dry in paper envelopes
Golden Rules for DNA Preservation
  • Always air-dry moist samples.
  • Use paper, not plastic, for packaging.
  • Keep samples cool and dry.
  • Avoid contamination by using sterile instruments and gloves.
  • Label every specimen properly and maintain chain of custody.
