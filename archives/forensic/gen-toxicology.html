1. Conceptual Definitions & Taxonomy

Toxicology is the branch of medical science dealing with poisons regarding their sources, physical and chemical properties, mechanism of action, clinical features, fatal dose, fatal period, diagnosis, management, autopsy findings, and medicolegal implications. Etymologically, it originates from the Greek 'Toxon' (bow used for shooting poisonous arrows). Comprehensive forensic investigations rely on a triad: circumstantial inquiry/medical history, thorough autopsy dissection, and laboratory toxicological analysis.

Core Sub-Disciplines

  • Forensic Toxicology: Application of toxicological analysis to establish legal evidence for judicial outcomes.
  • Clinical Toxicology: Focuses on the diagnosis, etiology, and management of human poisoning in clinical practice.
  • Environmental & Industrial Toxicology: Concerns environmental pollutant impacts and workplace chemical hazards.
  • Toxinology: The specific study of biological toxins produced by microorganisms, plants, or venomous animal species.
Key Legal & Clinical Definitions:
• Poison: Any substance (solid, liquid, or gas) which, when administered or introduced into the living body via any route, produces harmful physiological effects, disease, injury, or death. (Note: In law, any substance administered with intent to cause harm is treated as a poison).
• Drug: Any chemical agent used to modify or explore physiological systems or pathological states for therapeutic benefit.
• Parasuicide (Pseudocide): Non-fatal, often impulsive manipulative self-harm behavior without true lethal intent, commonly executed via drug ingestion to express distress or alleviate an intolerable situation.

Temporal Typology of Poisoning Exposures

  • Fulminant: Massive toxicity inducing near-instantaneous collapse and death within minutes (e.g., Oxalic acid > 15g, Potassium Cyanide).
  • Acute: Rapid onset of severe clinical manifestations following a single high-dose exposure or repeated doses over a brief interval (e.g., OP poisoning, Snake envenomation).
  • Subacute: Blended features of acute and chronic toxicities resulting from small, repeated doses over weeks to months.
  • Chronic: Progressive organ deterioration from persistent micro-exposures over months or years (e.g., endemic Arsenicosis via contaminated groundwater; Lead poisoning with Burtonian lines, colic, and severe anemia).

2. Taxonomic & Pharmacodynamic Matrices

A. Pathophysiological Classification (Mechanism of Action)

  1. Corrosives: Produce local chemical destruction of tissues.
    • Inorganic acids: Sulphuric acid (Oil of Vitriol), Nitric acid (Aqua Fortis), Hydrochloric acid (Muriatic acid).
    • Organic acids: Carbolic acid (Phenol), Oxalic acid, Acetic acid.
    • Alkalis: Caustic Soda (Sodium hydroxide), Caustic Potash (Potassium hydroxide), Carbonates, Ammonium hydroxide.
    • Metallic Salts: Mercuric chloride, Ferric chloride, Zinc chloride.
  2. Irritants: Produce inflammation, congestion, and ulceration of gastrointestinal or epithelial surfaces.
    • Inorganic Non-metallic: Phosphorus, Iodine, Chlorine, Bromine.
    • Inorganic Metallic: Arsenic, Lead, Mercury, Antimony, Bismuth, Copper.
    • Organic Animal: Snake venom, Scorpion venom, Wasp/Bee stings, Cantharides.
    • Organic Plant: Castor (Ricin), Calotropis, Croton tiglium, Marking nut (Semicarpus anacardium), Abrus precatorius.
    • Mechanical: Powdered glass, chopped animal hairs, diamond dust.
  3. Systemic Poisons: Act primarily on specific target organ systems after absorption.
    • Cerebral Somniferous: Opium, Morphine, Codeine, Heroin.
    • Cerebral Inebriant: Ethanol, Methanol, Ether, Chloroform.
    • Cerebral Stimulant: Amphetamines, Cocaine, Caffeine.
    • Cerebral Deliriant: Dhatura, Cannabis sativa, Hyoscyamus niger, Atropa belladonna.
    • Cerebral Depressant: Barbiturates, Benzodiazepines, Chloral hydrate.
    • Spinal & Peripheral: Nux vomica (Strychnine), Curare, Conium maculatum.
    • Cardiotoxic: Aconite, Digoxin, Oleander, Nicotine, Hydrocyanic acid.
    • Asphyxiants / Respiratory: Carbon monoxide, Hydrogen sulfide, Carbon dioxide, Cyanide, Organophosphates.
    • Organ-Specific: Nephrotoxic (Mercury, Oxalates), Hepatotoxic (Phosphorus, Paracetamol, Carbon tetrachloride), Myotoxic (Sea snake venom).
  4. Miscellaneous: Food poisoning (Botulism, Staphylococcal), War gases (Mustard gas, Phosgene, Nerve agents).

3. High-Yield Specific Toxicants (Systemic Toxicology)

1. Organophosphate (OP) Poisoning & Carbamates

Mechanism of Action: Organophosphates bind irreversibly (via phosphorylation) to the active site of the enzyme Acetylcholinesterase (AChE). This inhibits the hydrolysis of acetylcholine, leading to massive accumulation of acetylcholine at central, muscarinic, and nicotinic nerve terminals. Over time, "aging" of the enzyme-inhibitor complex occurs, making reactivation impossible unless oximes are given early.

Clinical Features (Symptom Triad)

  • Muscarinic Effects (@DUMBELS): Diarrhea/Diaphoresis, Urination, Miosis (pinpoint pupils), Bronchospasm/Bronchorrhea (excessive respiratory secretions), Emesis, Lacrimation, Salivation. (Also @SLUDGE: Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Defecation, Gastrointestinal upset, Emesis).
  • Nicotinic Effects: Muscle fasciculations, cramping, weakness, flaccid paralysis, hypertension, tachycardia.
  • Central Nervous System Effects: Confusion, ataxia, convulsions, respiratory center depression, coma.

Management & Antidotal Therapy

  1. Decontamination: Remove clothes, wash skin with soap/water, gastric lavage if within 1–2 hours.
  2. Atropine Sulfate (Specific Antidote): Competitively blocks muscarinic receptors.
    • Dose: 1.8 to 3 mg IV bolus, doubling every 5–10 minutes until full atropinization is achieved.
    • Signs of Atropinization: Clear lungs on auscultation (dry secretion), pupil dilation, HR > 80–100 bpm, dry skin, flushing.
  3. Cholinesterase Reactivators (Oximes): Pralidoxime (2-PAM) 1–2 g IV slow infusion over 30 min. Effective only before enzyme "aging" occurs (within 24–48 hours).

2. Carbon Monoxide (CO) Poisoning

Circumstances & Sources: Incomplete combustion of carbonaceous materials (gas heaters, charcoal braziers in unventilated rooms, exhaust gases, burning structures).

Pathophysiology: CO has an affinity for hemoglobin approximately 200–250 times greater than oxygen, forming Carboxyhemoglobin (COHb). This shifts the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve to the left (Haldane effect), preventing tissue oxygen delivery and inhibiting cellular cytochrome oxidase c.

Clinical Manifestations (Based on COHb Levels)

  • 10–20% COHb: Mild frontal headache, tightness across forehead.
  • 20–30% COHb: Throbbing headache, nausea, dizziness, impaired judgment.
  • 30–50% COHb: Severe headache, weakness, confusion, visual disturbances, syncope on exertion.
  • > 50–60% COHb: Convulsions, coma, Cheyne-Stokes respiration, fatal collapse.

Autopsy Findings

  • External: Characteristic Cherry-Red Post-Mortem Lividity (due to carboxyhemoglobin color visible in light-skinned individuals and mucosal surfaces). Tissues and blood remain unusually fluid and bright red.
  • Internal: Cherry-red discoloration of viscera, skeletal muscles, and brain. Bilateral symmetrical necrosis of the globus pallidus in the basal ganglia is pathognomonic in delayed deaths. Pulmonary edema with hemorrhagic frothy fluid in tracheobronchial tree.

3. Dhatura (Dhatura stramonium / fastuosa) Poisoning

Active Alkaloids: Hyoscyamine, Scopolamine (Hyoscine), and Atropine. Commonly known as "Roadside Poison" or "Stupefying Poison" used by criminals to rob travelers.

Classic Clinical Mnemonic ("Dhatura Triad / Mnemonic"):
• Dry as a bone (Inhibition of sweat and salivary secretions)
• Blind as a bat (Mydriasis / dilated fixed pupils with cycloplegia)
• Red as a beet (Cutaneous vasodilation and flushing)
• Hot as a hare (Hyperpyrexia due to lack of sweating)
• Mad as a hatter (Delirium, hallucinations, picking at bedclothes - "carphologia")

Cardinal Symptoms: Dry mouth, intense thirst, dysphagia, wide fixed dilated pupils, flushed skin, tachycardia, muttering delirium, active physical agitation, carphologia (picking at imaginary objects), urinary retention.

Management: Gastric lavage with 1:5000 Potassium Permanganate (KMnO4) or Tannic acid. Antidote: Physostigmine 1–2 mg IV slowly (crosses blood-brain barrier). Supportive care, catheterization, dark room.

4. Ethanol & Methanol Toxicity

A. Acute Ethanol Intoxication & Blood Alcohol Concentration (BAC) Stages

> 400 – 500 mg%Fatal StageRespiratory paralysis, cardiovascular collapse, death.

Certification of Drunkenness & Examination

Based on combination of smell of breath, clinical signs (gait instability, finger-nose test failure, slurred speech, dysarthria, nystagmus, dilated pupils reacting sluggishly to light) and laboratory BAC quantification (> 30 mg% is the legal threshold for driving in many jurisdictions; presence of clinical intoxication required for certification).

Modes of Death in Alcohol Toxicity

  • Direct acute respiratory center depression.
  • Aspiration of gastric contents (vomitus) causing asphyxia or Mendelson's syndrome.
  • Fatal trauma secondary to impaired motor control (traffic accidents, falls).
  • Vagal inhibition / positional asphyxia while intoxicated.
  • Acute hemorrhagic pancreatitis.
  • Rupture of esophageal varices secondary to portal hypertension in chronic cirrhosis leading to massive fatal upper GI hemorrhage.

Autopsy Findings in Chronic Alcoholic Bleeding to Death (Natural Death in Bed)

  • External Examination: Emaciated or bloated appearance, jaundice/icteric sclera, spider nevi on chest/neck, palmar erythema, gynecomastia, caput medusae around umbilicus, pallor of skin and mucous membranes due to exsanguination, blood pooled around mouth/nostrils and on bed bedding.
  • Internal Examination:
    • GI Tract: Dilated, tortuous, ruptured esophageal varices at distal esophagus; large blood clots filling stomach and intestine; congestive gastropathy / Mallory-Weiss tears.
    • Liver: Micronodular cirrhosis (hobnail liver), fatty liver change (steatosis), firm yellow-brown liver tissue.
    • Spleen: Splenomegaly (congestive splenomegaly due to portal hypertension).
    • Cardiovascular & Brain: Pale internal organs (exsanguination), alcoholic cardiomyopathy, cerebral atrophy with ventricular enlargement.
  • Cause of Death: Massive upper gastrointestinal hemorrhage secondary to ruptured esophageal varices due to hepatic cirrhosis.
  • Mechanism of Death: Hypovolemic shock / Exsanguination.
  • Manner of Death: Natural.

B. Methanol Toxicity

Pathophysiology: Methanol is metabolized by Alcohol Dehydrogenase (ADH) into Formaldehyde, which is then rapidly converted by Aldehyde Dehydrogenase into Formic Acid. Formic acid causes severe metabolic acidosis and optic nerve toxicity (retinal ganglion cell damage causing blindness).

Clinical Signs: Latent period of 12–24 hours, visual disturbances ("snowstorm vision", blurring, optic atrophy, blindness), severe high anion gap metabolic acidosis, abdominal pain, coma.

Management: Ethanol infusion (competes for ADH enzyme with ~10–20x higher affinity) or Fomepizole (4-methylpyrazole, direct ADH inhibitor); Sodium Bicarbonate for severe acidosis; Hemodialysis.

5. Arsenic Poisoning (Acute & Chronic)

Toxico-Dynamics: Binds to sulfhydryl (-SH) groups of cellular enzymes, inhibiting pyruvate dehydrogenase and disrupting cellular ATP generation.

Acute Arsenic Poisoning

  • Clinical Presentation: Severe gastrointestinal irritation resembling cholera ("Choleraic form"): burning throat pain, severe dysphagia, violent rice-watery stools mixed with blood, projectile vomiting, dehydration, muscle cramps, oliguria, collapse.
  • Autopsy Findings: Velvet-red inflammatory gastric mucosa ("Red velvet appearance"), submucosal petechial hemorrhages, yellow subendocardial ecchymoses on the left ventricle septum.

Chronic Arsenic Poisoning (Arsenicosis)

  • Skin Changes: Hyperkeratosis of palms and soles ("Raindrop pigmentation" - hyperpigmented patches intermingled with hypopigmented macules on skin).
  • Nails: Transverse white lines across fingernails (Mees' Lines).
  • Nervous System: Peripheral neuropathy ("Glove and stocking" paresthesia, motor weakness).
  • Systemic/Malignancy: Garlic breath odor, liver cirrhosis, blackfoot disease (peripheral vascular gangrene), increased risk of skin, lung, and bladder carcinomas.
  • Autopsy Findings in Chronic Death: Emaciation, hyperkeratosis, Mees' lines, liver cirrhosis, toxic hepatitis, nephritis, presence of arsenic in preserved bone, hair, and nail samples.

6. Chronic Lead Poisoning (Plumbism / Saturnism)

Sources: Lead paints, battery manufacturing, plumbing, smelting, occupational exposure.

Pathophysiology: Inhibits delta-aminolevulinic acid dehydratase (ALAD) and ferrochelatase, disrupting heme synthesis.

Clinical Features (@ABCDEF)

  • A - Anemia & Abdominal Colic: Microcytic hypochromic anemia with Basophilic Stippling (punctate basophilia) of RBCs; severe spasmodic abdominal pain (Lead colic).
  • B - Burtonian Line: Bluish-black line along the gingival margin (Burton's line) formed by lead sulfide precipitation.
  • C - CNS / Encephalopathy: Lead encephalopathy (headache, convulsions, delirium, coma, raised ICP).
  • D - Drop (Wrist Drop / Foot Drop): Peripheral motor neuropathy affecting extensor muscles (radial nerve palsy causing Wrist Drop).
  • E - Facial Pallor / Emaciation: "Saturnine cachexia" and facial pallor.

7. Snake Envenomation & Venomous Species

Classification of Venomous Snakes & Venoms

Family Representative Species Predominant Venom Profile Key Clinical Signs
Elapidae Cobra (Naja naja), Krait (Bungarus caeruleus) Neurotoxic & Cardiotoxic Ptosis, diplopia, dysarthria, bulbar paralysis, respiratory failure.
Viperidae Russell's Viper, Saw-scaled Viper Vasculotoxic / Hemotoxic Severe local swelling, blistering, active bleeding from bite/gums, incoagulable blood, DIC, renal failure.
Hydrophiidae Sea Snakes Myotoxic Generalized myalgia, rhabdomyolysis, myoglobinuria (dark red urine), hyperkalemia.
Why Snake Charmers Do Not Show Signs of Poisoning:
1. Surgical Defanging: Snake charmers routinely break, extract, or cauterize the primary venom fangs of cobras/vipers, rendering them unable to inject venom effectively.
2. Venom Duct Ligation / Gland Removal: Surgical excision or obliteration of the venom glands/ducts.
3. Acquired Immunity ( Mithridatism ): Repeated exposure to sub-lethal bites or intentional administration of minute venom doses induces neutralizing IgG antibodies.
4. Dry Bites: Up to 30–50% of defensive bites by venomous snakes are "dry bites" where no venom is injected.
5. Handling Defenseless / Dehydrated Snakes: Snakes kept in captivity by charmers are often dehydrated, malnourished, or kept at lower temperatures, reducing strike velocity and venom yield.

8. Aluminium Phosphide (Celphos) Poisoning

Mechanism of Action: In the presence of moisture/gastric hydrochloric acid, aluminium phosphide liberates Phosphine Gas (PH3). Phosphine gas inhibits cytochrome c oxidase in the mitochondrial respiratory chain, causing cellular hypoxia, free radical generation, and multiorgan failure.

Clinical Signs: Burning retrosternal pain, intractable vomiting, severe hypotension/refractory shock, metabolic acidosis, cardiac arrhythmias, pulmonary edema, death within hours.

Autopsy Findings (10 Celphos Tablets Ingested):

  • Pungent, characteristic Garlic / Decaying Fish Odor upon opening body cavities.
  • Massive pulmonary edema, pulmonary congestion, bloody alveolar fluid.
  • Gastric mucosa hyperemic, congested, desquamated, showing petechial hemorrhages.
  • Centrilobular necrosis of liver, acute tubular necrosis of kidneys.
  • Positive Silver Nitrate Test: Epigastric/gastric gas or soaked filter paper turns black when exposed to silver nitrate solution due to phosphine gas reaction.

9. Corrosive Acid Ingestion (Nitric Acid Ingestion)

Mechanism: Nitric acid is a powerful inorganic corrosive acid causing coagulative necrosis of tissues. Reacts with aromatic amino acids in cutaneous and mucosal proteins to form bright yellow xanthoproteic acid (Xanthoproteic Reaction).

Autopsy Findings in Fatal Nitric Acid Ingestion:

  • External: Characteristic Yellowish / Lemon-Yellow Staining and Corrosion around lips, mouth, chin, and hands. Corrosion lines running down chin and neck.
  • Internal:
    • Yellowish discoloration and extensive chemical sloughing of tongue, mouth, pharynx, and esophagus.
    • Gastric walls hardened, friable, dark-yellow to brown/black; extensive gastric mucosal necrosis and ulceration.
    • Gastric perforation with chemical peritonitis and dark amber fluid in peritoneal cavity.
    • Laryngeal edema, mucosal sloughing of upper respiratory tract from acid vapors.
  • Cause of Death: Corrosive shock / Chemical peritonitis / Laryngeal edema with asphyxia.

10. Cannabis Sativa

Active Chemical Principles: Tetrahydrocannabinol (Delta-9-THC), Cannabinol, Cannabidiol.

Common Preparations:

  • Bhang: Prepared from dried leaves and flowering shoots; lowest THC concentration (1–3%).
  • Ganja: Prepared from unfertilized female resinous flowering tops; moderate THC (4–8%).
  • Charas (Hashish): Pure resinous exudate extracted from leaves/tops; high THC (10–20%).
  • Hashish Oil: Concentrated liquid extract; maximum THC potency (20–60%).

Clinical Signs: Euphoria, altered time sense, distortion of space perception, conjunctival injection, tachycardia, increased appetite, panic reactions ("bad trip"), amotivational syndrome in chronic users.

• Microscopic examination of skin biopsy from needle track site demonstrates acute perivascular inflammatory infiltrate, focal necrosis, and cutaneous scarring. Histology of lungs confirms severe pulmonary edema and alveolar hemorrhage.

5. VISCERA / OTHER SAMPLES COLLECTED:

• Viscera Jar 2: 500g Liver + Gallbladder + Half of each Kidney in Saturated NaCl.
• Blood Sample: 50 mL subclavian venous blood preserved with Sodium Fluoride (NaF) & Potassium Oxalate.
• Urine Sample: Entire available volume (35 mL) in clean container without preservative.
• Vitreous Humor: Collected in clean test tube.

6. CAUSE OF DEATH:
• Cause of Death: Acute pulmonary edema and respiratory depression secondary to acute Opioid (Heroin) overdose.
• Manner of Death: Accidental / Substance Abuse overdose.

Preservation Protocols & Chemical Reagents

Containers must be clean, leak-proof glass jars filled to 2/3 capacity to allow space for gas expansion. A separate sample container of the pure preservative medium must always be submitted as a control.

Preservative Selection Rules:
1. Rectified Spirit (Absolute Alcohol): Standard preservative for routine cases.
CONTRAINDICATED IN (@PAPPA): Phosphorus, Alcohol, Phenol (Carbolic acid), Paraldehyde, Acetic acid.

2. Saturated Sodium Chloride (Common Salt) Solution: Economical alternative used in alcohol/phenol cases.
CONTRAINDICATED IN: Inorganic Mineral Acid poisoning and Plant/Vegetable Alkaloid toxicity.

3. Blood Preservatives: Sodium Fluoride (NaF - 10 mg/mL) serves as an enzyme inhibitor to prevent anaerobic glycolysis and ethanol degradation; Potassium Oxalate (3 mg/mL) acts as an anticoagulant.

Failure to Detect Poison Post-Mortem

  • Complete elimination via pre-mortem hyper-emesis or severe diarrhea.
  • Rapid pulmonary exhalation or volatile evaporation of low-boiling-point solvents.
  • Total hepatic biotransformation into non-detectable end-stage metabolites.
  • Degradation of organic alkaloids/glucosides due to post-mortem putrefaction.
  • Extended survival interval following single exposure, permitting total renal clearance prior to death.
  • Inappropriate preservative selection causing chemical destruction of the toxicant in transit.

6. Clinical Management of Acute Poisoning

Systemic Resuscitation & Decontamination Sequence

  1. Stabilization (Primary Survey): Airway security, Mechanical ventilation/Breathing support, Circulation support (IV fluids/inotropes), Disability evaluation.
  2. Ocular & Dermal Decontamination: Flush eyes with isotonic saline or copious water for 15–20 minutes. Remove contaminated clothing; cleanse skin thoroughly with soap and water.
  3. Gastrointestinal Decontamination:
    • Emesis: Syrup of Ipecac (largely obsolete); limited to conscious patients presenting within 1 hour.
    • Gastric Lavage: Mechanical gastric emptying via large-bore tube.
    • Activated Charcoal: Administered at 1 g/kg (aqueous slurry); adsorbs toxins within the gastrointestinal lumen.
    • Whole Bowel Irrigation: Polyethylene glycol administration for sustained-release or enteric-coated preparations.
  4. Enhanced Elimination: Hemodialysis, Hemoperfusion, Urinary alkalinization (Sodium Bicarbonate for Salicylates/Phenobarbital).
  5. Antidote Administration & Supportive Care.

Gastric Lavage: Protocol, Contraindications & Complications

Standard Procedural Steps

  1. Position patient in Left Lateral Decubitus position with head tilted downwards (reduces risk of aspirating gastric contents into the respiratory tract).
  2. Insert lubricated large-bore Ewald or Boas tube (14 mm diameter in adults) through mouth gag to approximately 50 cm mark.
  3. Verify placement by insufflating air while auscultating over epigastrium or aspirating gastric fluid.
  4. Instill aliquots of 200–300 mL warm normal saline or tepid water; siphon off via gravity drainage. Repeat until effluent is clear and free of odor.
Contraindications for Gastric Lavage (@4Cs & V):
1. Corrosive Ingestion (Risk of Esophageal/Gastric Perforation).
2. Convulsions / Uncontrolled Seizures.
3. Coma / Loss of Airway Reflexes (unless protected by Cuffed Endotracheal Tube).
4. Cardiac Arrhythmias or Severe Ischemic Heart Disease.
5. Volatile Hydrocarbon / Petroleum Distillates (High risk of Chemical Pneumonitis via aspiration).

7. Antidotes & Pharmacological Therapeutics

Classification of Antidotes

  • Mechanical / Physical: Retards systemic absorption by physically coating gastric mucosa or adsorbing molecules (e.g., Activated Charcoal, Egg white/albumin, Demulcents).
  • Chemical: Neutralizes poison via direct chemical interaction to produce an inert compound (e.g., Weak organic acids for alkali exposure).
  • Physiological / Pharmacological: Counteracts systemic toxicity by opposing pharmacodynamic mechanisms at receptor sites or alter metabolic pathways:
    • Receptor Antagonism: Atropine (Muscarinic blocker in OP toxicity), Naloxone (Opioid receptor antagonist).
    • Metabolic Competition: Ethanol/Fomepizole (Competes for Alcohol Dehydrogenase in Methanol poisoning).
    • Chelating Agents: Direct chemical binding of heavy metals into stable ring complexes excreted in urine (e.g., BAL/Dimercaprol, Ca-Na2 EDTA, Penicillamine, Desferrioxamine).

8. Statutory Frameworks & Medical Duties

Duties of a Medical Practitioner in Suspected Poisoning

Clinical & Therapeutic Obligations Legal & Statutory Duties
• Primary obligation: Immediate resuscitation and life preservation regardless of legal circumstances.
• Document detailed history, neurological state, and serial vital signs.
• Collect and preserve all biological samples (gastric contents, vomitus, blood, urine) in clean containers for toxicological profiling. 		• Government Medical Officers: Mandatory legal duty to notify local police authorities of all poisoning cases (accidental, suicidal, or homicidal).
• Private Practitioners: Obligated to report confirmed or strongly suspected homicidal poisoning cases to police/magistrate; not legally bound to report purely accidental or suicidal cases unless requested by authorities.
• Record a Dying Declaration with a Magistrate if the patient's condition is deteriorating rapidly; if impossible, record it in the presence of two independent witnesses.
• In the event of death, withhold standard death certification and transfer the body to legal authorities for forensic autopsy.
