Inorganic Metallic Irritant Poisons

FAST TRACK CLINICAL SCIENCE MBBS - 3rd YEAR (pp. 538–554)

Classification

  1. Metallic poisons: Arsenic, Mercury, Lead, Copper.
  2. Non-metallic poisons: Phosphorus, Iodine, Chlorine.

ARSENIC

A normal constituent of all animal tissues, in minute amount. Metallic arsenic (black in color) is not poisonous, as it is not absorbed from the GIT.

Note: Among metals we study, only lead is poisonous in its metallic form. Others are not poisonous in their metallic forms; they are poisonous only in their metallic salt form.

Compounds of Arsenic

  1. Arsenious oxide or arsenic trioxide: (sankhya, somalkhar, white arsenic or arsenic) — Most toxic form.
  2. Copper arsenite: (Scheele's green).
  3. Copper acetoarsenite: (Paris green or emerald green).

Sources of Arsenic

  1. Natural sources: Soil, water and some sea fish (mussels, prawns).
    • High arsenic content of soil and subsoil water of some places is the cause of endemic toxicity (from shallow tubewells inserted for drinking water).
  2. Tobacco smoke, particularly cigars also contain arsenic, and in some beers as impurities.

Uses

  • For fair complexion, aphrodisiac.
  • Used in fruit sprays, sheepdips, weed-killers, insecticides, rat poisons, flypapers, calico-printing, wallpapers, artificial flowers and as mordant in dyeing.
  • Depilatory, coloring pigment.
  • Flypaper.

Arsenic Poisoning

Mechanism of Action

Cellular Effects

  • Binds to sulfhydryl (-SH) groups of mitochondrial enzymes.
  • Major enzymes affected:
    • Pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH)
    • Certain phosphatases
  • Uncouples mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation.
  • Results in:
    • ↓ Pyruvate → Acetyl-CoA conversion
    • ↓ Citric (Krebs) cycle activity
    • ↓ ATP production (cellular energy failure)

Metabolic Effects

  • ↓ Cellular glucose uptake
  • ↓ Gluconeogenesis
  • ↓ Fatty acid oxidation
  • ↓ Acetyl-CoA formation

Local & Systemic Effects

  • Local: Irritation of mucous membranes.
  • Systemic: Depression of the nervous system.
Mnemonic: SH → PDH → ATP↓
Sulfhydryl binding → PDH inhibition → ATP depletion.

Absorption & Excretion

Absorption

  • Gastrointestinal tract (oral).
  • Skin.
  • Lungs (arsine gas).
  • Parenteral route.

Excretion

  • Major route: Kidneys.
  • Also excreted through:
    • Feces
    • Bile
    • Sweat
    • Breast milk
    • Hair
    • Nails
Important Forensic Points
  • Appears in hair and nails within a few hours after ingestion.
  • Chronic intermittent exposure produces successive deposits in hair and nails.
  • Secreted into the stomach and intestines even after non-oral exposure.

Fulminant Type

  • Occurs after ingestion of >3 g arsenic.
  • GIT symptoms absent.
  • Rapid shock.
  • Peripheral vascular failure.
  • Death within 1–3 hours.

Narcotic Type

  • Giddiness.
  • Formication.
  • Muscle tenderness.
  • Delirium.
  • Coma.
  • CNS depression.
  • Hemolysis.
  • Liver and kidney damage.
  • Hemoglobinuria.
  • Renal failure.
  • Complete paralysis (rare).
  • Black urine.
  • Anemia.
  • Convulsions.
  • Death usually preceded by anuria.

Important Facts

  • Characteristic findings:
    • Garlic odor of breath
    • Rice-water stools
    • Severe dehydration
    • Shock
    • Peripheral neuropathy
    • Black urine (hemoglobinuria)
  • Fatal dose:
    • Adults: 120–200 mg arsenic trioxide
    • Children: 2 mg/kg
  • Fatal period: 1–2 days.

Treatment

1. ABC & Supportive Care

  • Hemodynamic stabilization is the priority.
  • Large-volume IV crystalloids.
  • Correct dehydration and shock.

2. Gastric Decontamination

  • Repeated gastric lavage with warm water or milk.
  • Whole bowel irrigation with polyethylene glycol.
  • Purgatives:
    • Castor oil
    • Magnesium sulfate
  • Avoid:
    • Activated charcoal (poor adsorption).
    • Oral alkalis (increase arsenic solubility).

3. Demulcents

  • Butter.
  • Greasy substances.
  • Reduce arsenic absorption.

4. Chelation Therapy (Antidotes)

  • BAL (Dimercaprol)
    • 3–5 mg/kg IM.
    • Every 4 hours × 2 days.
    • Every 6 hours × 1 day.
    • Every 12 hours × 10 days.
  • Succimer (DMSA)
    • Oral.
    • 10 mg/kg every 8 hours for 10 days.
  • DMPS (Dimerval)
    • Drug of choice for most heavy metal poisoning.
    • 200 mg IV every 4 hours.
    • Then oral 100 mg TDS/QID.

5. Other Measures

  • Glucose-saline with sodium bicarbonate.
  • Hemodialysis.
  • Exchange transfusion.

Chronic Arsenic Poisoning (Arsenicosis)

Causes

  • Recovery from acute arsenic poisoning.
  • Repeated accidental exposure in workers handling arsenic.
  • Repeated ingestion of food or water contaminated with arsenic (may occasionally be homicidal).

Tolerance

  • Arsenophagists (individuals who consume arsenic regularly as a tonic or aphrodisiac) may tolerate 250–300 mg or more in a single dose.

Clinical Features

Progresses through four stages (Mnemonic: ECRP)

  1. E – GIT disturbances with emaciation
  2. C – Catarrhal changes
  3. R – Skin rashes
  4. P – Peripheral nervous system disturbances
  • Chronic exposure may also cause:
    • Diabetes mellitus
    • Peripheral neuropathy
    • Peripheral vascular insufficiency
    • Vasospasm

Important Forensic Points
  • Hair and nail samples are the preferred specimens for confirming chronic arsenic poisoning.
  • Tissue samples may also be collected for toxicological analysis.

Medico-legal Importance

Arsenic poisoning can be homicidal, suicidal, accidental, occupational or unintentional.

  1. Homicide: Arsenic was a popular homicidal poison because of the following:
    • Onset of symptoms is gradual
    • Cheap
    • Symptoms simulate those of cholera
    • Colorless, Tasteless, Odorless
    • Small quantity is required to cause death
    • Easily obtainable
    • Can be administered easily with food, drink or betel leaf (paan)
    • Chronic cases causing debility resemble certain diseases
  2. Suicide: Rare (it causes too much of pain).
  3. Accidental death: May be due to admixture with articles of food or from its improper medicinal use. Chronic poisoning results from drinking well water containing arsenic.
  4. Occupational poisoning: In those working in metal foundry, mining, glass production or in the semiconductor industry.
  5. Ingested or applied locally in the form of a paste or ointment on abortion sticks to procure abortion.
  6. Cattle poison.
Note — Disadvantages of arsenic as homicidal poison:
• It retards putrefaction.
• It can be detected in decomposed/buried bodies.
• Arsenic can be found in bones, hair and nails for several years.
• It can be detected in charred bones or ashes.

COPPER

  • Not poisonous as a metal.
  • Human body copper content: 100-150 mg.
  • Present as an integral and functional moiety of proteins and enzyme systems including catalase, cytochrome C oxidase, dopamine β-hydroxylase and serum ceruloplasmin (However, the body cannot synthesize copper).

Compounds of Copper

  1. Copper sulphate
  2. Copper subacetate (verdigris)
  3. Copper carbonate

Uses

  • Algicide, molluscicide and plant fungicide.
  • Mordant in electroplating.
  • Agent for leather tanning and hide preservation.
  • Emetic.

Mechanism of Action

  • High affinity for ligands containing nitrogen and sulfur donors (as in other heavy metals).
  • Toxicity of copper is exerted on enzymes whose activities depend on sulfhydryl and amino groups.
  • May also affect nucleic acid.

Absorption and Excretion

  • Ingestion or inhalation of copper dust and fumes occurs in industrial settings and in miners.
  • After ingestion, maximum absorption of copper occurs in the stomach and jejunum. Absorbed copper is initially bound to albumin and is transported from the GIT to the liver where it is transferred to ceruloplasmin.
  • Eliminated mostly through the feces after excretion into the bile.
  • Urinary excretion of copper is low in humans. Adults have urinary excretion of 25 µg/24 h.

Clinical Features

Acute Ingestion

Symptoms appear in 15-30 min after swallowing.

System Signs and Symptoms
GIT - Metallic taste, ptyalism (increased salivation), burning pain in stomach, thirst, colicky abdominal pain, nausea, eructation and repeated vomiting. Vomited matter is blue or green.
- Diarrhea with much straining. Motions are liquid brown, rarely bloody.
Renal Oliguria, uremia, albuminuria, hematuria, hemoglobinuria
CVS Breathing difficulty, cold perspiration, hypotension and symptoms of circulatory collapse
Hepatic Jaundice is common in severe cases
MS Cramps or spasms of legs, paralysis of limbs
CNS Frontal headache, drowsiness, insensibility, irreversible coma and death occurs
Note: Individuals with G-6-phosphate deficiency may be at increased risk of hematologic effects of copper. Multi-organ dysfunction syndrome may occur and death due to hepatic or renal failure or both occurs after a few days.

Acute Inhalation

  • Upper respiratory tract irritation resulting in sore throat and cough.
  • Conjunctivitis, palpebral edema and sinus irritation may occur.
  • Nasal mucous membrane may show atrophy with perforation.

Skin Exposure

  • Irritant contact dermatitis.
  • Severe exposure may cause a greenish-blue discoloration of skin.

Fatal Dose: Copper subacetate: 15 g. Copper sulphate: 20 g (0.15-0.3 g/kg).
Fatal period: 18-24 h, but it may extend to 1-3 days.

Treatment

  1. No need to use emetics as vomiting occurs in 5-10 min after ingestion.
  2. Gastric lavage with 1% potassium ferrocyanide which acts as antidote by forming cupric ferrocyanide (insoluble).
  3. Demulcents: Egg white or milk (form insoluble albuminate of copper).
  4. Castor oil is given to remove poison from the intestines.
  5. D-penicillamine given in usual doses is very effective.
  6. EDTA or BAL in usual doses.
  7. Allay pain by injecting morphine and use diuretics, if urine is suppressed.
  8. Symptomatic treatment to maintain electrolyte and fluid balance.
  9. For severe cases associated with anorexia and hematuria, cortisone 50-100 mg IM thrice daily is recommended.
Note: Tetrathiomolybdate is suggested to be useful as chelating agent in case of acute copper poisoning as urinary excretion is enhanced by increased molybdenum intake.

Postmortem Findings

  1. Bluish line on gums.
  2. Skin may be yellow due to jaundice.
  3. Greenish-blue froth from the mouth and nostrils.
  4. Mucous membrane of the mouth and tongue may have bluish or greenish-blue tinge.
  5. Internally, some discoloration is present in the mucous membrane of the esophagus and stomach.
  6. Stomach: Gastric mucosa is congested with desquamation and hemorrhage at places.
  7. Small intestine: Mucosa (upper part) shows signs of moderate irritation.
  8. Liver: Soft and fatty. Shows centrilobular necrosis and biliary stasis.
  9. Kidneys: Show degenerative changes in proximal tubules and hemoglobin casts may be seen. Tubular necrosis, edema of medulla and appearance of eosinophilic cast.
  10. Hemolysis.

Chronic Copper Poisoning

Causes

  • Occurs among workers using copper and its salts due to inhalation of copper dust or fumes (Welders may develop metal fume fever).
  • Food being contaminated with verdigris from dirty copper vessels.

Clinical Features

  • Green or purple line on the gums.
  • A constant metallic taste, nausea, dyspepsia, vomiting and diarrhea with colicky pain.
  • Giddiness and headache.
  • Laryngitis and bronchitis.
  • Renal damage.
  • General signs of progressive emaciation, viz. anemia, malaise and debility.
  • Peripheral neuritis with wrist drop or foot drop and atrophy of muscles.
  • Copper dust may cause inflammation of the conjunctiva and ulceration of the cornea.
  • Skin becomes jaundiced. Urine and perspiration become green.
  • Bronzed diabetes may be present.
Note — Chalcosis oculi:
• Chronic ophthalmic exposure to particulate elemental copper or one of its alloys may result in its deposition in the cornea, lens, vitreous and retina.
• Copper deposits in the cornea (chalcosis corneae) appear as golden brown, ruby red or green pigment ring in the peripheral Descemet's membrane (Kayser-Fleischer ring).
• Lens opacities (chalcosis lentis) occur in the form of anterior subcapsular cataract ('sunflower' cataract and typically greenish in color).

Vineyard sprayer's lung: Occupational disease seen due to chronic exposure to Bordeaux solution (1-2% copper sulfate solution neutralized with lime). The patients develop interstitial pulmonary fibrosis and histiocytic granulomas containing copper. The radiographic picture resembles that of silicosis.

Treatment

  1. After removing the cause, patient should be given a massage and a warm bath. Patient should be exposed to fresh air.
  2. Attention should be paid to his diet and dyspepsia.
  3. Symptomatic treatment.

Postmortem Findings

  • Liver: Fatty degeneration.
  • Kidneys: Degeneration of the epithelial cells.

Medico-legal Aspects

  • Suicidal cases: Common.
  • Accidental poisoning: Food contaminated with verdigris. Toxicity may develop from copper absorbed systemically from IUCD wire or hemodialysis tubing.
  • Homicide: Rare because of its color and taste.
  • Children may swallow copper sulphate crystals attracted by its color.
  • Cattle poison (Rare).

THALLIUM

Soft and pliable metal with tin-white color that tarnishes on air exposure due to formation of black thallous oxide. Thallium sulfate is odorless and tasteless.

Toxic Compounds of Thallium

  1. Thallium sulfate
  2. Thallium acetate
  3. Thallium iodide
  4. Thallium nitrate
  5. Thallium carbonate

Uses

Dye industry, Optical glass, Imitation jewelry, Rodenticide, Depilatory agent, Fireworks.

Mechanism

  • Exact mechanism is unclear but postulated that thallium results in ligand formation with protein sulfhydryl group of enzymes and inhibits cellular respiration.
  • It disrupts calcium homeostasis.
  • Interaction with riboflavin and riboflavin based cofactors.

Clinical Features

Acute Poisoning

Pain in abdomen, Features of gastroenteritis, Hematemesis, Hematochezia, Headache, Confusion, Disorientation, Paraesthesia, Hallucinations, Convulsions, Retrobulbar neuritis, Ophthalmoplegia, Peripheral neuropathy.

Chronic Poisoning

  • Alopecia: falling of hairs from head, eyebrows and axilla are common.
  • Skin rash, Acneform eruptions, Dystrophy of nails, Ascending sensorimotor neuropathy, Ataxia, Optic neuropathy, Encephalopathy, Ptosis, Nystagmus.
Thallium Triad: Combination of alopecia, skin rash, and painful peripheral neuropathy and mental confusion with lethargy.

Management

  • Gastric lavage with ferric ferrocyanide (Prussian blue). Prussian blue binds thallium in the intestine and enhances its fecal excretion.
  • Hemodialysis, Forced diuresis, Supportive measures.

Autopsy Findings

Alopecia, Stomatitis, Fatty degeneration of liver and heart, Tubular necrosis, Pulmonary edema, Cerebral edema.

Fatal dose: 12 mg/kg body weight.
Fatal period: 24 to 30 hours.

Medicolegal Importance

  1. Popular as ideal homicidal agent.
  2. Also consumed as suicidal agent.

LEAD

  • Heavy steel-grey metal.
  • Metallic lead and all its salts are poisonous.
  • Metallic lead is absorbed through GIT, being soluble in gastric juice.
  • It is a cumulative poison (gets deposited in tissues, mostly in the bones (90%), liver and kidney in chronic exposure).
  • Greatest source of lead in lead poisoning is gasoline or petrol or auto mobile exhaust.

Toxic Compounds of Lead

  1. Lead acetate (sugar of lead)
  2. Lead carbonate (safeda)
  3. Lead chromate
  4. Lead monoxide (litharge)
  5. Lead tetroxide (red lead, vermilion, sindur)
  6. Lead sulphide (least toxic)

Uses

Earlier used as an astringent and local sedative for sprains, Antiknock for petrol, Used as Sindur, For manufacture of paints.

Mechanism of Action

Combines with sulfhydryl groups and interferes with mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, ATPases, calcium-dependent messengers and enhances oxidation and cell apoptosis. This causes defective heme synthesis, proximal renal tubular and osteoblast dysfunction. In the CNS, it has deleterious effects on the nerve cells and myelin sheaths and also causes cerebral edema.

Absorption and Excretion

  • Absorbed through the GIT, respiratory tract (dust and fumes) and skin (lead tetraoxide).
  • 95-99% of lead is sequestered in RBCs.
  • GIT lead absorption is increased by iron deficiency and low dietary calcium and decreased by co-ingestion with food.
  • Mainly excreted through the urine (70%), also via feces and scant amounts via the hair, nails and sweat.

Clinical Features

Acute Poisoning

System Features
GIT Astringent and metallic taste, Dry throat, thirst, Vomiting, nausea, Burning abdominal pain (colic), Bloodstained diarrhea leading to circulatory collapse.
CNS Headache, Lethargy, Arthralgia, Myalgia, Anorexia, Insomnia, Paresthesia, Depression, Coma and death.

Fatal dose: Lead carbonate: 40 g; Lead acetate: 20 g.
Fatal period: 1-2 days.

Treatment

  • Gastric lavage with 1% solution of sodium or magnesium sulphate (forms insoluble lead sulphate); also given in the purgative dose.
  • Demulcents, whole bowel irrigation and repeated cathartics.
  • Calcium chloride 5 mg as 10% solution IV or calcium gluconate 10 ml of 10% solution IV causes deposition of lead in bones from blood (to combat acute crisis).
  • Edetate calcium disodium (CaNa2EDTA), 1500 mg/m²/kg/day (50 mg/kg/day) in 4-6 divided doses or as a continuous infusion for 5 days.
  • Some add BAL, 4-5 mg/kg IM every 4 h for 5 days.
  • Vitamin C (weak, but natural chelating agent) may be given.
  • Peritoneal or hemodialysis in anuric patients with chronic renal failure.
  • Symptomatic treatment.

Post-mortem Findings

Signs of acute gastroenteritis, Stomach mucosa thickened/softened with gray-white deposit, Pale skin, Emaciation, Lead line on X-ray, Elevated delta-Aminolaevulinic (ALA) levels in postmortem blood.

Chronic Lead Poisoning (Plumbism / Saturnism)

Causes

  1. Inhalation of lead dust and fumes by makers of white lead and users of lead paints, smelters, plumbers, printers, etc.
  2. Continuous absorption of minute amounts from drinking water stored in lead cisterns, tinned food, hair dyes, cosmetics.
  3. Absorption through raw or intact skin.
  4. Use of ghee stored in brass/copper vessels lined with tin.
  5. Absorption of vermilion applied to the scalp.
  6. Children chewing/licking toys or furniture painted with lead-based compound.

Clinical Features @ABCDEFGh

  1. Facial Pallor (F=Facial pallor): Particularly about the mouth (earliest and most consistent sign due to vasospasm).
  2. Anemia (A=Anemia): Early stages: polycythemia with polychromatophilia. Later stages: Anemia with karyorrhexis, Dyserythropoiesis (punctate basophilia, reticulocytosis, poikilocytosis, anisocytosis), Nucleated red cells, RBC count down to 3.5 million/dl and Hb to 6.5 g%.
  3. Burton's / Burtonian line (B=Burtonian line): Stippled blue line on gingival surface in 50-70% cases due to lead sulphide formation.
  4. Colic and constipation (C=Colic, Constipation): Intestinal colic in 85% cases, severe night pain.
  5. Lead palsy (D=1 Drop i.e. wrist drop and foot drop): Late symptom (< 10%), caused by nerve degeneration and muscle atrophy.
  6. Lead encephalopathy (E=Encephalopathy): Common in children, reversible or permanent brain damage/death in 25%.
  7. Cardiovascular system (H=Hypertension): Vascular constriction leading to hypertension.
  8. Kidney: Chronic arteriosclerotic/interstitial nephritis.
  9. Reproductive system: Amenorrhea, abortions.
  10. Lead osteopathy: Radiopaque metaphysis bands in children long bones.
  11. Retinal stippling: Grayish glistening particles in early phase.

Diagnosis & Management

  • Diagnosis: History, clinical signs, metaphysis X-ray bands, Erythrocyte protoporphyrin (EP / ZPP), basophilic stippling, GI radiopaque material.
  • Management: Chelation with CaNa2EDTA, Diazepam for convulsions, Thiamine, Calcium gluconate.

Medico-legal Importance

  • Accidental chronic poisoning common in industrial workers.
  • Diachylon paste (lead oleate) used as local application for abortion.
  • Lead missiles remaining in tissues produce poisoning in 12-48 days.

MERCURY

Bright silvery liquid, volatile at room temperature. Liquid metallic mercury is not poisonous if swallowed (poorly absorbed from GIT). Not a constituent of the human body.

Toxic Compounds of Mercury

Inorganic salts: Mercuric chloride (corrosive sublimate), Mercurous chloride (calomel), Mercuric sulphide (cinnabar/vermilion), Mercuric cyanide/oxide/iodide.
Organic salts (More toxic): Methyl mercury, Dimethyl mercury, Ethyl mercury, Phenyl mercury.

Mechanism of Action

Binds with sulfhydryl groups resulting in enzyme inhibition. Elemental and methyl mercury are CNS toxic. Mercury vapor is a pulmonary irritant. Inorganic salts are corrosive to skin, eyes, GIT and nephrotoxic.

Clinical Features

Acute Poisoning

  • Inhalation: Chemical pneumonitis, pulmonary edema, gingivostomatitis, CNS symptoms (ataxia, visual restriction, delirium).
  • Ingestion (Inorganic): Acrid metallic taste, grayish-white corroded mucosa, burning pain, bloody vomitus and diarrhea, oliguria, renal failure, circulatory collapse. Second phase brings glossitis, renal tubular necrosis, membranous colitis.
  • Organic Salts: Ataxia, dysarthria, paresthesia, mental deterioration, chorea.

Fatal dose: 1-4 g mercuric chloride; 10-60 mg/kg methyl mercury.
Fatal period: 3-5 days.

Treatment

Supplemental oxygen for inhalation. Gastric lavage with 5% sodium formaldehyde sulphoxylate. BAL is chelator of choice (3-5 mg/kg IM). DMSA / Succimer or D-penicillamine as alternatives.

Chronic Mercury Poisoning (Hydrargyrism)

Mnemonic — Blue MEATS:
Blue line, Mercuria lentis, Erethism, Acrodynia, Tremors, Salivation and gingivitis.
  1. Danbury tremors / Hatter's shakes: Coarse tremors starting in hands, progressing to tongue/limbs.
  2. Mercurial erethism: Neuropsychiatric changes (insomnia, anxiety, shyness, blushing, explosive irritability).
  3. Mercuria lentis: Brownish lens capsule reflex on slit-lamp exam.
  4. Acrodynia (Pink disease): Idiosyncratic reaction with pinkish acral rashes, desquamation of palms/soles (5-P: Pink, Puffy, Paresthesia, Painful, Peeling).
  5. Minamata disease: Caused by eating methylmercury contaminated fish (ataxia, visual restriction, clouding of consciousness).

ZINC

Normally present in body. Toxic compounds: Zinc chloride, Zinc phosphide, Zinc sulphate (white vitriol), Zinc oxide, Zinc stearate.

Clinical Features: Metallic taste, abdominal pain, blood-stained vomitus/stool, mucosal ulceration, shock collapse.

Fatal Dose: Zinc chloride/phosphide: 5 g; Zinc sulphate: 15 g.
Treatment: Alkaline gastric lavage, oral sodium bicarbonate, demulcents.

Note on Specific Zinc Reactions:
• Zinc Phosphide: Garlicky vomitus smell, dyspnea, pulmonary edema, metabolic acidosis.
• Zinc Oxide: Metal fume fever or zinc shakes.
• Zinc Stearate: Pneumonitis.

IRON

Poisonous salts: Ferrous sulphate (green vitriol), Ferric chloride.

Mechanism & Stages

Concentrated form acts as corrosive; produces ROS free radicals causing cellular/mitochondrial toxicity and hepatic necrosis.

  • Stage I (GI Toxicity): Metallic taste, vomiting, hematemesis, melena, shock.
  • Stage II (Quiescent Phase): Apparent stabilization, lethargy, confusion.
  • Stage III (Mitochondrial / Hepatic Toxicity): Acute liver failure, MODS, hypoglycemia, coma.
  • Stage IV (Recovery / Gastric Scarring): Pyloric stenosis/obstruction.

Treatment: Polyethylene glycol WBI, Chelation with IV Desferrioxamine (15 mg/kg/hr).

ANTIMONY

Compounds: Tartar emetic, Antimony trioxide, Butter of antimony, Surma.
Clinical Features: Vomiting, abdominal pain, hematemesis, dermatitis, hepatic/renal failure.
Treatment: Gastric lavage, chelation with BAL, hemodialysis.
