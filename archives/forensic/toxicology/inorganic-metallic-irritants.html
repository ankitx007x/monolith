Inorganic Metallic Irritant Poisons

FAST TRACK CLINICAL SCIENCE MBBS - 3rd YEAR (pp. 538–554)

Classification

  1. Metallic poisons: Arsenic, Mercury, Lead, Copper.
  2. Non-metallic poisons: Phosphorus, Iodine, Chlorine.

ARSENIC

A normal constituent of all animal tissues, in minute amount. Metallic arsenic (black in color) is not poisonous, as it is not absorbed from the GIT.

Note: Among metals we study, only lead is poisonous in its metallic form. Others are not poisonous in their metallic forms; they are poisonous only in their metallic salt form.

Compounds of Arsenic

  1. Arsenious oxide or arsenic trioxide: (sankhya, somalkhar, white arsenic or arsenic) — Most toxic form.
  2. Copper arsenite: (Scheele's green).
  3. Copper acetoarsenite: (Paris green or emerald green).

Sources of Arsenic

  1. Natural sources: Soil, water and some sea fish (mussels, prawns).
    • High arsenic content of soil and subsoil water of some places is the cause of endemic toxicity (from shallow tubewells inserted for drinking water).
  2. Tobacco smoke, particularly cigars also contain arsenic, and in some beers as impurities.

Uses

  • For fair complexion, aphrodisiac.
  • Used in fruit sprays, sheepdips, weed-killers, insecticides, rat poisons, flypapers, calico-printing, wallpapers, artificial flowers and as mordant in dyeing.
  • Depilatory, coloring pigment.
  • Flypaper.

Arsenic Poisoning

Mechanism of Action

Cellular Effects

  • Binds to sulfhydryl (-SH) groups of mitochondrial enzymes.
  • Major enzymes affected:
    • Pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH)
    • Certain phosphatases
  • Uncouples mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation.
  • Results in:
    • ↓ Pyruvate → Acetyl-CoA conversion
    • ↓ Citric (Krebs) cycle activity
    • ↓ ATP production (cellular energy failure)

Metabolic Effects

  • ↓ Cellular glucose uptake
  • ↓ Gluconeogenesis
  • ↓ Fatty acid oxidation
  • ↓ Acetyl-CoA formation

Local & Systemic Effects

  • Local: Irritation of mucous membranes.
  • Systemic: Depression of the nervous system.
Mnemonic: SH → PDH → ATP↓
Sulfhydryl binding → PDH inhibition → ATP depletion.

Absorption & Excretion

Absorption

  • Gastrointestinal tract (oral).
  • Skin.
  • Lungs (arsine gas).
  • Parenteral route.

Excretion

  • Major route: Kidneys.
  • Also excreted through:
    • Feces
    • Bile
    • Sweat
    • Breast milk
    • Hair
    • Nails
Important Forensic Points
  • Appears in hair and nails within a few hours after ingestion.
  • Chronic intermittent exposure produces successive deposits in hair and nails.
  • Secreted into the stomach and intestines even after non-oral exposure.

Fulminant Type

  • Occurs after ingestion of >3 g arsenic.
  • GIT symptoms absent.
  • Rapid shock.
  • Peripheral vascular failure.
  • Death within 1–3 hours.

Narcotic Type

  • Giddiness.
  • Formication.
  • Muscle tenderness.
  • Delirium.
  • Coma.
  • CNS depression.
  • Hemolysis.
  • Liver and kidney damage.
  • Hemoglobinuria.
  • Renal failure.
  • Complete paralysis (rare).
  • Black urine.
  • Anemia.
  • Convulsions.
  • Death usually preceded by anuria.

Important Facts

  • Characteristic findings:
    • Garlic odor of breath
    • Rice-water stools
    • Severe dehydration
    • Shock
    • Peripheral neuropathy
    • Black urine (hemoglobinuria)
  • Fatal dose:
    • Adults: 120–200 mg arsenic trioxide
    • Children: 2 mg/kg
  • Fatal period: 1–2 days.

Treatment

1. ABC & Supportive Care

  • Hemodynamic stabilization is the priority.
  • Large-volume IV crystalloids.
  • Correct dehydration and shock.

2. Gastric Decontamination

  • Repeated gastric lavage with warm water or milk.
  • Whole bowel irrigation with polyethylene glycol.
  • Purgatives:
    • Castor oil
    • Magnesium sulfate
  • Avoid:
    • Activated charcoal (poor adsorption).
    • Oral alkalis (increase arsenic solubility).

3. Demulcents

  • Butter.
  • Greasy substances.
  • Reduce arsenic absorption.

4. Chelation Therapy (Antidotes)

  • BAL (Dimercaprol)
    • 3–5 mg/kg IM.
    • Every 4 hours × 2 days.
    • Every 6 hours × 1 day.
    • Every 12 hours × 10 days.
  • Succimer (DMSA)
    • Oral.
    • 10 mg/kg every 8 hours for 10 days.
  • DMPS (Dimerval)
    • Drug of choice for most heavy metal poisoning.
    • 200 mg IV every 4 hours.
    • Then oral 100 mg TDS/QID.

5. Other Measures

  • Glucose-saline with sodium bicarbonate.
  • Hemodialysis.
  • Exchange transfusion.

Chronic Arsenic Poisoning (Arsenicosis)

Causes

  • Recovery from acute arsenic poisoning.
  • Repeated accidental exposure in workers handling arsenic.
  • Repeated ingestion of food or water contaminated with arsenic (may occasionally be homicidal).

Tolerance

  • Arsenophagists (individuals who consume arsenic regularly as a tonic or aphrodisiac) may tolerate 250–300 mg or more in a single dose.

Clinical Features

Progresses through four stages (Mnemonic: ECRP)

  1. E – GIT disturbances with emaciation
  2. C – Catarrhal changes
  3. R – Skin rashes
  4. P – Peripheral nervous system disturbances
  • Chronic exposure may also cause:
    • Diabetes mellitus
    • Peripheral neuropathy
    • Peripheral vascular insufficiency
    • Vasospasm

  • Hair and nail samples are the preferred specimens for confirming chronic arsenic poisoning.
  • Tissue samples may also be collected for toxicological analysis.

Medico-Legal Importance

  • Arsenic poisoning may be:
    • Homicidal
    • Suicidal
    • Accidental
    • Occupational
    • Unintentional

1. Homicidal Poisoning

Historically known as the "King of Poisons" because it possesses several characteristics favorable for homicide.

  • Symptoms develop gradually.
  • Cheap and readily available.
  • Colorless, tasteless and odorless.
  • Only a small quantity is required to produce death.
  • Easily mixed with food, drinks or betel leaf (paan).
  • Acute poisoning closely resembles cholera.
  • Chronic poisoning mimics many debilitating diseases.

2. Suicidal Poisoning

  • Rare.
  • Uncommon because poisoning is extremely painful.

3. Accidental Poisoning

  • Accidental contamination of food.
  • Improper medicinal use.
  • Consumption of arsenic-contaminated well water (chronic arsenicosis).

4. Occupational Poisoning

  • Mining industry.
  • Metal foundries.
  • Glass manufacturing.
  • Semiconductor industry.

5. Other Medico-Legal Uses

  • Applied locally (paste or ointment on abortion sticks) to procure abortion.
  • Used as cattle poison.
Why Was Arsenic a Popular Homicidal Poison?
  • Colorless, tasteless and odorless.
  • Easy to administer with food or drinks.
  • Small fatal dose.
  • Symptoms mimic cholera.
  • Chronic poisoning resembles natural illness.
Disadvantages as a Homicidal Poison
  • Retards putrefaction.
  • Can be detected even in decomposed or buried bodies.
  • Persists in bones, hair and nails for many years.
  • Can be detected in charred bones and ashes.

Copper Poisoning

  • Copper metal itself is not poisonous.
  • Total body copper content: 100–150 mg.
  • Essential trace element present in several enzymes and proteins.
  • The body cannot synthesize copper; it must be obtained from the diet.

Important Copper Compounds

  • Copper sulphate
  • Copper subacetate (Verdigris)
  • Copper carbonate

Uses

  • Algicide.
  • Molluscicide.
  • Plant fungicide.
  • Mordant in electroplating.
  • Leather tanning and hide preservation.
  • Emetic.

Mechanism of Action

  • High affinity for nitrogen- and sulfur-containing ligands.
  • Inhibits enzymes dependent on:
    • Sulfhydryl (-SH) groups
    • Amino groups
  • May also damage nucleic acids.
Mechanism
Copper → SH & NH2 enzyme inhibition → Cellular toxicity

Absorption & Excretion

Absorption

  • Exposure occurs by:
    • Ingestion
    • Inhalation of copper dust or fumes (industrial workers/miners)
  • Maximum gastrointestinal absorption occurs in:
    • Stomach
    • Jejunum
  • After absorption:
    • Initially binds to albumin.
    • Transported to the liver.
    • Incorporated into ceruloplasmin.

Excretion

  • Main route: Bile → Feces.
  • Urinary excretion is minimal (~25 μg/24 hours in adults).

Important Points
  • Patients with G6PD deficiency are more prone to hematologic complications.
  • Severe poisoning may progress to multi-organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS).
  • Death usually results from hepatic failure, renal failure or both.

Acute Inhalation

  • Upper respiratory tract irritation.
  • Sore throat.
  • Cough.
  • Conjunctivitis.
  • Palpebral edema.
  • Sinus irritation.
  • Chronic exposure may cause:
    • Nasal mucosal atrophy.
    • Nasal septal perforation.

Skin Exposure

  • Irritant contact dermatitis.
  • Severe exposure may produce greenish-blue discoloration of the skin.

Important Facts

  • Fatal Dose
    • Copper subacetate: 15 g
    • Copper sulphate: 20 g (0.15–0.3 g/kg)
  • Fatal Period: Usually 18–24 hours (may extend to 1–3 days).

Treatment

  • Do NOT induce emesis (vomiting usually begins spontaneously within 5–10 minutes).
  • Gastric lavage with 1% potassium ferrocyanide.
    • Forms insoluble cupric ferrocyanide.
  • Demulcents:
    • Egg white
    • Milk
    • Form insoluble copper albuminate.
  • Castor oil to eliminate unabsorbed poison.
  • Chelation Therapy
    • D-Penicillamine (very effective).
    • EDTA.
    • BAL (Dimercaprol).
    • Tetrathiomolybdate (alternative chelator).
  • Pain relief with morphine.
  • Diuretics if urine output is suppressed.
  • Maintain electrolyte and fluid balance.
  • Severe cases with anorexia and hematuria:
    • Cortisone 50–100 mg IM three times daily.
High-Yield Viva Points
  • Characteristic blue-green vomitus.
  • Main excretion is through bile and feces.
  • Major transport protein: Ceruloplasmin.
  • Most effective chelator: D-Penicillamine.
  • Tetrathiomolybdate enhances urinary copper excretion by increasing molybdenum intake.

Postmortem Findings

  1. Bluish line on gums.
  2. Skin may be yellow due to jaundice.
  3. Greenish-blue froth from the mouth and nostrils.
  4. Mucous membrane of the mouth and tongue may have bluish or greenish-blue tinge.
  5. Internally, some discoloration is present in the mucous membrane of the esophagus and stomach.
  6. Stomach: Gastric mucosa is congested with desquamation and hemorrhage at places.
  7. Small intestine: Mucosa (upper part) shows signs of moderate irritation.
  8. Liver: Soft and fatty. Shows centrilobular necrosis and biliary stasis.
  9. Kidneys: Show degenerative changes in proximal tubules and hemoglobin casts may be seen. Tubular necrosis, edema of medulla and appearance of eosinophilic cast.
  10. Hemolysis.

Special Conditions

Chalcosis Oculi
  • Occurs due to chronic ocular exposure to copper or copper alloys.
  • Copper deposits may occur in:
    • Cornea
    • Lens
    • Vitreous
    • Retina
  • Cornea (Chalcosis corneae)
    • Golden-brown, ruby-red or green pigment ring.
    • Located in the peripheral Descemet's membrane.
    • Known as the Kayser-Fleischer ring.
  • Lens (Chalcosis lentis)
    • Anterior subcapsular cataract.
    • Typical Sunflower cataract.
    • Usually greenish in color.
Vineyard Sprayer's Lung
  • Occupational disease caused by chronic exposure to Bordeaux solution.
  • Bordeaux solution = 1–2% copper sulphate neutralized with lime.
  • Produces:
    • Interstitial pulmonary fibrosis.
    • Histiocytic granulomas containing copper.
    • X-ray appearance resembling silicosis.
High-Yield Eye Findings
  • Kayser-Fleischer ring → Peripheral Descemet's membrane.
  • Sunflower cataract → Chalcosis lentis.

Treatment

  • Remove the patient from further copper exposure.
  • Fresh air exposure.
  • Warm bath and massage.
  • Correct nutritional deficiencies and dyspepsia.
  • Symptomatic and supportive treatment.

Post-mortem Findings

Organ Findings
Liver Fatty degeneration.
Kidneys Degeneration of tubular epithelial cells.

Medico-Legal Importance

  • Suicidal poisoning: Relatively common.
  • Accidental poisoning:
    • Food contaminated with verdigris.
    • Systemic absorption from copper IUCDs.
    • Copper-containing hemodialysis tubing.
    • Children swallowing attractive blue copper sulphate crystals.
  • Homicidal poisoning: Rare because of its characteristic color and taste.
  • Occasionally used as a cattle poison.
High-Yield Revision
  • Green or purple gum line.
  • Metal fume fever in welders.
  • Kayser-Fleischer ring (chalcosis corneae).
  • Sunflower cataract (chalcosis lentis).
  • Vineyard sprayer's lung → Bordeaux solution exposure.
  • Homicide is rare; accidental and occupational poisoning are more common.

Thallium Poisoning

  • Soft, pliable, tin-white metal.
  • Tarnishes on exposure to air due to formation of black thallous oxide.
  • Thallium sulfate is colorless, odorless and tasteless.

Toxic Compounds

  • Thallium sulfate
  • Thallium acetate
  • Thallium iodide
  • Thallium nitrate
  • Thallium carbonate

Uses

  • Dye industry.
  • Optical glass manufacturing.
  • Imitation jewelry.
  • Rodenticide.
  • Depilatory agent.
  • Fireworks.

Mechanism of Action

  • Binds to sulfhydryl (-SH) groups of cellular enzymes.
  • Inhibits cellular respiration.
  • Disrupts calcium homeostasis.
  • Interferes with riboflavin and riboflavin-dependent cofactors.

Lead Poisoning (Plumbism / Saturnism)

  • Heavy steel-grey metal.
  • Metallic lead and all lead salts are poisonous.
  • Metallic lead is absorbed through the GIT after dissolving in gastric juice.
  • Cumulative poison:
    • ~90% deposited in bones.
    • Remaining deposited mainly in liver and kidneys.
  • Major environmental source: Gasoline (petrol) and automobile exhaust.

Toxic Compounds of Lead

  • Lead acetate (Sugar of Lead)
  • Lead carbonate (Safeda)
  • Lead chromate
  • Lead monoxide (Litharge)
  • Lead tetroxide (Red lead / Vermilion / Sindur)
  • Lead sulphide (Least toxic)

Uses

  • Previously used as an astringent and local sedative.
  • Anti-knock additive in petrol.
  • Used in Sindur (red lead).
  • Paint manufacturing.

Mechanism of Action

  • Binds to sulfhydryl (-SH) groups of enzymes.
  • Interferes with:
    • Mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation.
    • ATPases.
    • Calcium-dependent messenger systems.
  • Increases oxidative stress and apoptosis.
  • Produces:
    • Defective heme synthesis.
    • Proximal renal tubular dysfunction.
    • Osteoblast dysfunction.
    • CNS toxicity with neuronal damage, demyelination and cerebral edema.
Mechanism
SH binding → ↓ Heme synthesis + CNS toxicity + Renal tubular damage

Absorption & Excretion

Absorption

  • Gastrointestinal tract.
  • Respiratory tract (lead dust and fumes).
  • Skin (especially organic lead compounds such as tetraethyl lead).
  • 95–99% of absorbed lead is bound to RBCs.
  • Absorption increases with:
    • Iron deficiency.
    • Low dietary calcium.
  • Absorption decreases when taken with food.

Excretion

  • Main route: Urine (~70%).
  • Also excreted through:
    • Feces.
    • Hair.
    • Nails.
    • Sweat (small amounts).

Treatment

  • Gastric lavage with 1% sodium or magnesium sulfate.
    • Forms insoluble lead sulfate.
  • Demulcents.
  • Whole bowel irrigation.
  • Repeated cathartics.
  • Calcium therapy

    • OR Calcium gluconate 10 mL IV (10%).

  • Chelation Therapy
    • CaNa2EDTA (Drug of choice).
    • BAL (Dimercaprol).
  • Vitamin C (weak natural chelator).
  • Hemodialysis/peritoneal dialysis in anuric patients with chronic renal failure.
  • Symptomatic and supportive care.

  • Features of acute gastroenteritis.
  • Stomach:
    • Thickened or softened mucosa.
    • Gray-white deposits.
  • Pale skin.
  • Emaciation.
  • Radiopaque lead line on X-ray.
  • Raised post-mortem blood δ-ALA (delta-aminolevulinic acid).

Chronic Lead Poisoning (Plumbism / Saturnism)

Causes

  • Occupational exposure:
    • Lead paint workers.
    • Smelters.
    • Plumbers.
    • Printers.
    • Workers exposed to lead dust and fumes.
  • Drinking water stored in lead pipes or cisterns.
  • Tinned food.
  • Hair dyes and cosmetics.
  • Absorption through intact skin.
  • Consumption of ghee stored in improperly lined brass/copper vessels.
  • Use of vermilion (Sindur).
  • Children chewing lead-painted toys or furniture.

Mercury Poisoning

  • Bright, silvery liquid metal.
  • Volatile at room temperature.
  • Metallic mercury is poorly absorbed from the GIT; therefore swallowing elemental mercury is usually not toxic.
  • Not a normal constituent of the human body.

Toxic Compounds

Inorganic Salts

  • Mercuric chloride (Corrosive sublimate)
  • Mercurous chloride (Calomel)
  • Mercuric sulphide (Cinnabar / Vermilion)
  • Mercuric cyanide
  • Mercuric oxide
  • Mercuric iodide

Organic Compounds (More Toxic)

  • Methyl mercury
  • Dimethyl mercury
  • Ethyl mercury
  • Phenyl mercury

Mechanism of Action

  • Binds to sulfhydryl (-SH) groups, causing enzyme inhibition.
  • Elemental and methyl mercury primarily affect the CNS.
  • Mercury vapor is a potent pulmonary irritant.
  • Inorganic salts are:
    • Corrosive to skin, eyes and GIT.
    • Nephrotoxic.
Mechanism
SH binding → Enzyme inhibition → CNS + Kidney toxicity

Zinc Poisoning

  • Zinc is an essential trace element.

Toxic Compounds

  • Zinc chloride
  • Zinc phosphide
  • Zinc sulphate (White vitriol)
  • Zinc oxide
  • Zinc stearate

Clinical Features

  • Metallic taste.
  • Abdominal pain.
  • Blood-stained vomiting.
  • Blood-stained stools.
  • Mucosal ulceration.
  • Shock and circulatory collapse.

Important Facts

  • Fatal dose
    • Zinc chloride / phosphide: 5 g
    • Zinc sulphate: 15 g

Treatment

  • Alkaline gastric lavage.
  • Oral sodium bicarbonate.
  • Demulcents.
  • Supportive care.
High-Yield Zinc Reactions
  • Zinc phosphide: Garlic odor, pulmonary edema, metabolic acidosis.
  • Zinc oxide: Metal fume fever (Zinc shakes).
  • Zinc stearate: Pneumonitis.

Iron Poisoning

Poisonous Salts

  • Ferrous sulphate (Green vitriol)
  • Ferric chloride

Mechanism of Action

  • Concentrated iron acts as a corrosive.
  • Generates reactive oxygen species (ROS).
  • Causes mitochondrial toxicity.
  • Produces hepatic necrosis.

Clinical Stages

Stage Clinical Features

  • Metallic taste
  • Vomiting
  • Hematemesis

  • Shock

  • Apparent improvement

III Mitochondrial/Hepatic toxicity:
  • Acute liver failure
  • MODS
  • Hypoglycemia
  • Coma
IV Recovery:
  • Pyloric stenosis
  • Gastric outlet obstruction

Treatment

  • Whole bowel irrigation with polyethylene glycol.
  • IV Desferrioxamine (15 mg/kg/hour).
  • Supportive care.
