1. Conceptual Definitions & Taxonomy

Toxicology: It is the branch of the medical science that deals with poison in relation to their sources, properties, action, clinical features, fatal dose, diagnosis, treatment, autopsy findings and medico-legal aspects. The word toxicology is derived from the Greek word 'Toxon' that refers to a bow used for shooting arrows.

Toxicology deals with all aspects of poison. The overall investigations of a medico-legal case includes three basic constituents namely the investigation of circumstance of the death including medical history, a thorough autopsy and lab investigations including toxicology. So without toxicology a forensic investigation is never complete.

Classification of Toxicology

  • Forensic Toxicology: It is the branch of toxicology that deals with establishment of evidences related to poisoning for the purpose of legal treatment and hence justice.
  • Clinical Toxicology: It is a branch of toxicology that deals with causation, diagnosis and management of poisoning in humans during clinical practice.
  • Environmental Toxicology
  • Industrial Toxicology
  • Toxinology: It is the study of toxins (a toxin is a poisonous substance produced during the metabolism and growth of certain microorganisms and some higher plant and animal species). Eg. venom of snake, bee etc.
Key Legal & Clinical Definitions:
• Poison: It is any substance if taken in any account by any route produces harmful effect (namely disease, deformity or death) over the body. [2003, 2008, 1999, 2001, 2007, 2006, 2011]
• Drug: It is any substance or product that is used or intended to be used to modify or explore physiological systems or pathological states for the benefit of the recipient.
• Parasuicide (attempted suicide or pseudocide): A supposed suicide attempt that was not intended to succeed. It is a conscious, often impulsive, manipulative act, undertaken to get rid of an intolerable situation. Drug ingestion is the commonest form.

Temporal Typology of Poisoning Exposures

  • Fulminant poisoning: Massive dose results rapid death eg. Oxalic acid poisoning if dose > 15g.
  • Acute poisoning: Single episode of high dose poison intake or multiple episodes over a short period resulting in acute worsening type of clinical features eg Snake bite.
  • Subacute poisoning: Showing features of both acute and chronic poisoning when small doses of poison are taken on daily basis for months or years eg Arsenic poisoning.
  • Chronic poisoning: Showing progressive deteriorating type of clinical features when small doses of poison are taken on daily basis for months or years eg arsenic poisoning by using well water in Bangladesh. Eg Anemia, Burtonian lines, Colic, etc in Lead poisoning.

2. General Considerations & Classification

A. Based on Nature (MOA) of Poison (@CISM)

  1. Corrosives:
    • Acids: Inorganic acids - like sulphuric acid, Hydrochloric acid, Nitric acid. Organic acids - like carbolic acid (phenol), Oxalic acid, Acetic acid.
    • Alkalis: Eg. Sodium hydroxide, Caustic Potash, Carbonates.
    • Metallic salts: Like mercuric chloride, ferric chloride, Zinc chloride.
  2. Irritants:
    • Inorganic Nonmetallic: Iodine, phosphorous, Chlorine, Bromine.
    • Inorganic Metallic: Arsenic, lead, Mercury, Antimony, Bismuth, Copper.
    • Organic Animal: Snake, wasp, Scorpion, Cantharides.
    • Organic Plant: Castor, calotropis, Croton tiglium, Marking nut, Abrus precatorius.
    • Mechanical irritants: Powdered glass, chopped hairs, diamond dust.
  3. Systemic Poisons (@Remember the systems covered in 1st and 2nd yr):
    • Cerebral Somniferous: Opium, Morphine, Codeine, Heroin.
    • Cerebral Inebriant: Alcohol, Methanol, Ether, Chloroform.
    • Cerebral Stimulants: Amphetamine, Cocaine, Caffeine.
    • Cerebral Deliriant: Dhatura, Cannabis sativa, Hyoscyamus niger, Atropa belladonna.
    • Cerebral Depressant: Barbiturate, Benzodiazepines, Chloral hydrate.
    • Spinal: Strychnine, gelsemium.
    • Peripheral nerve: Curare, conium.
    • Myotoxic: Sea snake poison, OP.
    • Cardiac: Nicotine, aconite (also called as Bikh), Digoxin, Oleander.
    • Asphyxiants or Respiratory: Carbon monoxide, Hydrogen sulphide, OP, Cyanide, Carbon dioxide.
    • Nephrotoxic: Mercury, Oxalates.
    • Hepatotoxic: Phosphorous, Paracetamol, Carbon tetrachloride.
    • Gastrointestinal: Aloe barbadensis.
  4. Miscellaneous: Food poisoning, war gases etc.

B. Based on Manner of Use

(@The following points are to be considered in each poison while writing medicolegal importance of poisons):

  • Homicidal poisons: Arsenic, Thallium, Aconite, Oleander, Insulin.
  • Suicidal poisons: OP, Aluminium phosphide (Celphos), KCN, HCN, Opium, Barbiturates.
  • Accidental poisons: Lead poisoning, Mercury poisoning, Contamination of food and drinks, Mistaken for drugs, Wrong medication.
  • Abortifacient: Castor, Croton, Lead pills.
  • Stupefying (making unconscious or causing stupor): Alcohol, Dhatura, used with the intent to commit crime.
  • Cattle: Calotropis, killed for hide.
  • Arrow: Curare.
  • Aphrodisiac: Cocaine, cantharides (Spanish fly poison).
  • Used to produce fabricated injury: Plumbago, Marking nut.
  • Used to cause injury: Acids (Vitriolage).
  • Used in torture: Eg. Injection of addictive drugs to produce physical dependence first and then causing withdrawal reactions later not giving the drugs. The process is an example of pharmacological torture and other drugs like muscle relaxants, and LSD are also used so as to induce pain or cause anxiety/psychological disturbances.
  • Adulteration of food and drinks.

  • Microbial poisons eg. food poisoning
  • Animal poisons
  • Plant poisons
  • Pharmaceutical poisons

Action & Pharmacokinetics of Poison

  • Local Action: Is at the site of application eg. burns due to acid on the face.
  • Systemic Action: Is at a place far from site of application through blood (or body fluids) or sometimes by reflex neurogenic shock. Eg. Aconite causes cardiac toxicity and death.
  • Both Local and Systemic Action: Is both at the site of application and also at a site far from the local site through blood or reflex shock. Eg. Phenol corrodes skin while at the same time affects CNS causing headache, convulsions and coma.

Note: There are no poisons that have only systemic actions. Systemic poisons have major harm through systemic action and their local action is minor. Local poisons are presumed to have no systemic action.

Factors Modifying the Action of Poisons [2001, 2004, 2008]

  1. Type of poison (e.g. lead has cumulative action)
  2. Amount or dose or concentration of poison
  3. Routes of application or administration
  4. Condition of the site of absorption (diseases eg. GI fibrosis reduces absorption of alcohol; state of sleep/intoxication delays action)
  5. Physical state of poison
  6. Tolerance
  7. Idiosyncrasy (inherent hypersensitivity resulting in dyspnea, rigors, fever, diarrhea, bowel hemorrhage, albuminuria e.g., penicillin, aspirin, cocaine, sulfonamides, sera, mushrooms, eggs, shellfish, heroin)
  8. Diseases of metabolizing organs (e.g., liver disease increases toxicity)
  9. Route of elimination
  10. Age
  11. Sex
  12. Chemical state of poison
Routes Summary:
• Administration Routes: Local (dermal), Systemic (oral, sublingual, rectal, vaginal, dermal, inhalational, nasal, parenteral). Rectal, vaginal, and nasal are considered systemic in toxicology due to significant absorption.
• Elimination Routes: Urine, Feces, Exhaled air, Saliva, Sweat, Milk, Bile.
• Commonest poisoning in Nepal is Organophosphorus poisoning.
• Love philter: A drug supposed to increase love between giver and taker (e.g., arsenic, alcohol, opium, cocaine, cannabis).

Toxicity Rating Standard

Toxicity rating is directly proportional to potency. Lower the fatal dose, higher the toxicity.

@Mnemonic: Super super toxic (< 5 mg/kg), Extra extremely toxic (5-50 mg/kg), Very very toxic (51-500 mg/kg), Modern moderately toxic (501 mg/kg - 5 g/kg), Slice of slightly toxic (5.1-15 g/kg), Poison = practically non-toxic (> 15 g/kg).

3. Diagnosis of Poisoning

A. Diagnosis in Living

Based upon History, Symptoms, Signs, and Laboratory examination.

  • History & Symptoms: Sudden onset of vomiting/diarrhea/abdominal pain; onset in multiple persons eating together; history of consumption; uniform and rapidly progressing symptoms. Non-specific symptoms include persistent vomiting/diarrhea, abdominal pain, altered sensorium/coma, breathlessness/choking, convulsions, palpitations/arrhythmias, hypothermia, paralysis.
  • Signs (Clinical Examination): Peculiar odor, taste, temperature changes, ocular changes, oral manifestations, CNS (coma grading, convulsions, tremors, neuropathy, gait, delirium), Respiratory (dyspnea, pulmonary edema), GI (vomiting, diarrhea, melena, distension, ileus, thirst, dysphagia), GU (urine color, albuminuria, hematuria, oliguria), MSK (myopathy, rhabdomyolysis, fasciculations), Blood (anemia, stippling, methemoglobinemia), Dermal (rashes, ulcers), Ear (rhinorrhea, deafness, vertigo, tinnitus).
  • Lab Investigations: Specific assays (e.g. plasma cholinesterase for OP, silver-impregnated paper mask test for Celphos, LFT for Paracetamol, urine analysis for Amphetamine).

B. Diagnosis in Dead

  • Autopsy Findings (External): Soiled clothes, bottle/container/label in pocket, suicide note, vitriolage, trickling/stigmata of poison on skin, injuries, body smell, early/delayed rigor mortis, postmortem lividity coloration, altered decomposition.
  • Autopsy Findings (Internal):
    • Cranial cavity: Odor, cerebral edema, hemorrhages.
    • Chest cavity: Pleural/pericardial effusion, state of lungs/heart, pulmonary edema.
    • Abdominal cavity: Gastric contents color, mucosal erosions, ulcers, hemorrhagic gastritis, mucosa staining; state of intestines, liver, spleen, kidneys, pancreas, bladder, esophagus.
  • Laboratory Examination: Viscera/fluid extraction and analysis:
    • Qualitative Assays: Thin layer chromatography (TLC) — simple and inexpensive.
    • Quantitative Assays: Gas chromatography (GC), High performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), Mass spectrometry (MS), Radio-immunoassay (RIA), Atomic absorption spectrophotometry, Neutron activation analysis.

Preservation Guidelines

  • Containers: Clean glass container, filled to 2/3 capacity (1/3rd empty), airtight.
  • Separate Containers: Stomach + intestine in one; Liver + kidneys in one; Other viscera in separate; Blood/urine/skin in separate glass containers; Bone/hair/nails/clothes in polythene packets. A sample of pure preservative must always be sent separately in a clean container.
  • Container Labeling: Name, age, sex, police station, reference case, mortuary name, PM serial number, date of PM, preservative used, signature and designation of autopsy surgeon.
Preservative Rules:
1. Rectified Spirit / Absolute Alcohol: Standard preservative for routine cases.
CONTRAINDICATED IN (@PAPPA): Phosphorus, Alcohol, Phenol (carbolic acid), Paraldehyde, Acetic acid.

2. Saturated Solution of Common Salt (NaCl): Routinely used because economical.
CONTRAINDICATED IN: Inorganic acid (mineral acid) poisoning and Vegetable/Plant poisoning (@IV).

3. Special Preservatives:
• Rabies (Brain): 50% glycerol solution for Negri bodies.
• Histology: 10% formal saline solution.
• Urine: 20 ml in sterile container without preservative (or boric acid, formaldehyde, thymol).
• Blood: Drawn from subclavian vein with 13-gauge needle (50 ml total: two 20 ml tubes with NaF; one 10 ml tube with NaF + Potassium Oxalate). NaF prevents bacterial decomposition.
• Vitreous Humor: All available volume, sent in frozen condition (reflects blood level 1–2 hours antemortem).

Failure to Detect Poison Post-Mortem

  1. Elimination by pre-mortem vomiting and diarrhea (irritants).
  2. Evaporation or oxidation of volatile poisons from lungs.
  3. Detoxification, conjugation, and elimination after absorption.
  4. Alkaloidal poisons not detectable by routine chemical methods.
  5. Rapid metabolism making extraction difficult.
  6. Biological toxins and venoms (protein in nature) cannot be separated from body tissues.
  7. Organic poisons (alkaloids, glucosides) detoxified by oxidation during life, faulty preservation, or decomposition.
  8. Delayed death in slow-acting poisons allows complete excretion after producing irreversible changes.
  9. Small quantities of drugs requiring massive viscera volume for detection.
  10. Wrong or insufficient material sent for analysis.

5. Management of Poisoning

Principles of Management

  1. Stabilization and Evaluation
  2. Decontamination (Eye, Skin, Gut)
  3. Poison Elimination
  4. Antidote Administration
  5. Nursing Care
  6. Psychiatric Care

1. Stabilization & Evaluation

  • A - Maintenance of airway
  • B - Maintenance of breathing
  • C - Maintenance of circulation
  • D - Disability (neurologic status evaluation)
  • E - Elimination of poison
AVPU / Coma Grading Levels:
• Level 0: Fully conscious (Alert)
• Level 1: Drowsy but responding to verbal command
• Level 2: Maximum response to minimal painful stimuli
• Level 3: Minimal response to maximum painful stimuli
• Level 4: Unresponsive to painful stimuli, loss of all reflexes (pharyngeal, laryngeal, corneal)

2. Decontamination Protocols

  • Ocular: Irrigate with copious water for 15–20 minutes.
  • Dermal: Wash skin thoroughly with plain water, remove clothes, copiously irrigate.
  • Gut Decontamination:
    • Emesis: Syrup of ipecac, apomorphine, or pharyngeal stimulation. Indications: Conscious patient within 4–6 hours. Contraindications (@4Cs & V): Coma/convulsions/unconscious, impaired gag reflex, corrosive/volatile ingestion, pregnancy. Complications: Aspiration pneumonia, Mallory-Weiss tears, laryngospasm.
    • Gastric Lavage (Stomach Wash): Undertaken if life-threatening ingestion within 1 hour.
      Indications: Conscious patient within 1–2 hours.
      Contraindications (@4Cs & V): Coma/convulsions, impaired gag reflex, corrosive/volatile ingestion, marked hypothermia, esophageal varices, electrolyte imbalance, hydrocarbon ingestion, cardiac arrhythmia.
      Complications: Aspiration pneumonia, laryngospasm, Mallory-Weiss tears, GI perforation, hypothermia, bradycardia, electrolyte imbalance.
      Equipment: Ewald or Boas tube (14 mm diameter in adults), Ryle's tube (children), mouth gag, rubber tube, funnel, suction bulb, cuffed ET tube, bucket, jug.
      Procedure: Left lateral decubitus position (head lower than larynx); remove dentures; lubricate and insert tube to 50 cm mark; confirm placement by forcing air while auscultating over epigastrium (bubbling sound = stomach; hissing = trachea); instill 200–250 mL lavage fluid, lower funnel to siphon out; repeat until clear/odorless; leave antidote/activated charcoal in stomach at end.
    • Activated Charcoal: Fine black powder that adsorbs stomach poisons. Dose: 1 g/kg body weight mixed in water. Disadvantages: Unpleasant taste, vomiting, constipation/diarrhea. Contraindications: Paralytic ileus, intestinal obstruction, GI perforation.

6. Antidotes & Pharmacological Therapeutics

Antidotes [2078]: Substances used to counteract or neutralize the effects of poison.

Classification of Antidotes

  1. Physical / Mechanical / Non-Specific: Retards absorption mechanically. E.g., Activated charcoal (adsorbs poison), demulcents (milk, starch, egg white/albumin form a protective coating).
  2. Chemical Antidote: Neutralizes poison or forms harmless/insoluble compounds. E.g., weak vegetable acids (citric acid, lemon juice) for corrosive alkalis.
  3. Physiological / Pharmacological Antidote: Counteracts systemic effects on tissues via:
    • Reducing toxic conversion: Ethanol for methanol (competes for alcohol dehydrogenase).
    • Receptor competition: Naloxone antagonizes opiates at opioid receptors.
    • Receptor blocking: Atropine blocks muscarinic receptors in OP poisoning.
    • Chelating action: Forms inert ring complexes with heavy metals for urinary excretion (BAL, Succimer, Disodium edetate, Penicillamine, Desferrioxamine, Ca-Na2 EDTA).

Used when nature of poison is unknown. Mnemonic: @CoMmenT

3. Poison Elimination Techniques

Indications: Severe poisoning, progressive deterioration despite support, high risk of morbidity/mortality, impaired normal excretion, delayed serious toxicity.

Methods: Hemodialysis, Hemoperfusion, Peritoneal dialysis, Plasma perfusion, Alkaline & Acid diuresis, Diaphoretics (Pilocarpine, Salicylates).

7. Prevention & Statutory Frameworks

Prevention Measures

  1. Education
  2. Locking drugs and poisons in cabinets
  3. Separating food and poisons
  4. Keeping products in original containers with caps closed
  5. Keeping out of reach of children
  6. Reading labels before use; never using in the dark
  7. Proper disposal of expired products
  8. Child-proof packaging
  9. Teaching children not to eat wild plants/berries
  10. Kitchen ventilation
  11. Care for distressed/mental health patients
  12. Air/water pollution control & occupational safety

Legal Aspects & Statutory Laws (Nepal)

  • Protocol for prohibition of chemical/biological gases (1925)
  • Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs (1961, amended 1972)
  • Drug Advisory Council Rules (1970)
  • Narcotic Drug Control Act (1976)
  • Drug Act (1978)
  • Drug Registration Rules (1981)
  • Drug Category Rules (1986)
  • UN Convention against Illicit Traffic in Narcotic Drugs (1988)
  • Nepal Pharmacy Control Act (2000) & Rules (2002)
  • Drug Control Strategy (2010)

Punishments & Legal Provisions

  • Mens rea (intent) is essential. Murder by poison is treated legally as murder.
  • Act of omission/commission with poison endangering human life: imprisonment up to 6 months or fine.
  • Administering poison with intent to hurt: imprisonment up to 10 years and fine.
  • Adulteration of drugs: imprisonment up to 6 months.
  • Criminal uses of poison: Murder, Injury, Stupefying (Dhatura/railroad poison in robbery), Criminal abortion, Torture, Careless use.

Duties of a Medical Practitioner in Suspected Poisoning

Medical Obligations Legal Obligations
• Primary duty: Immediate care and treatment to save life regardless of manner of poisoning.
• Note preliminary particulars (name, age, sex, occupation, address, date/time, history).
• Collect and preserve all evidences (containers, soiled clothes, food, vomitus, feces, gastric wash) and hand over to Investigating Officer sealed.
• Observe symptoms relative to food/drink changes and affect on others.
• Consult a senior practitioner in confidence. 		• Government Medical Officers: Required to report ALL suspected cases (accidental, suicidal, homicidal) to police.
• Private Practitioners: Bound to inform police/magistrate in confirmed homicidal poisoning; not bound in purely suicidal or accidental cases unless summoned by IO.
• Arrange Dying Declaration if patient is serious.
• In case of death without clear cause, inform police and DO NOT issue a death certificate.
• Notify Public Health Authorities in food poisoning cases.
• Preserve indoor patient records for up to 3 years (provide within 72 hours if requested).
Drug Automatism: Hypothesis where a patient enters a state of toxic delirium after taking one or several doses of a depressant drug (e.g., alcohol/barbiturates), and subsequently ingests additional doses repeatedly without realizing it.

8. Memory Booster (Quick Summary)

Classification Summary:
• Corrosive: Strong acids (H2SO4, HCl, carbolic, oxalic), Strong alkalis (NaOH), Metals (HgCl2, FeCl3).
• Irritants: Inorganic Non-metal (P, I), Metal (As, Pb), Organic Animal (snake, scorpion), Plant (castor, calotropis), Mechanical (chopped hair, glass).

• Miscellaneous: Food poisoning.
