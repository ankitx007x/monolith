Forensic Medicine: Spinal Poisons

FAST TRACK CLINICAL SCIENCE MBBS - 3rd YEAR (pp. 599–600)

1. Strychnos Nux Vomica

Classification: Neurotoxic (Spinal) Poison.

Derived from the plant Strychnos nux-vomica. The active principle is a powerful alkaloid present in the plant's seeds. It is extremely bitter in taste.

Active Principles

  • Strychnine: Present in the seeds. Powerful spinal convulsant.
  • Brucine: Present in the bark.
  • Loganine

Commercial / Domestic Uses

Used as a rodenticide and occasionally employed to kill stray dogs (known as "dog buttons").

Mechanism of Action

  • Competitively blocks postganglionic motor neuron receptor sites in the ventral horn of the spinal cord and brainstem.
  • Prevents the action of glycine (the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the spinal cord), leading to uninhibited reflex muscular contractions and spastic muscle paralysis.
  • GABA activity is unaffected.
Lethal Parameters:
• Fatal Dose: 50 to 100 mg (or 1 to 2 crushed seeds).
• Fatal Period: 1 to 2 hours.

2. Clinical Features & Management

Signs & Symptoms

Initial features include anxiety, nausea, mydriasis (dilated pupils), and muscle twitching. This rapidly progresses to violent, generalized tonic convulsions.

Classic Triad & Spasm Postures (@ROPE):
• Whole-body muscle convulsions with characteristic relaxation gaps between fits.
• Risus sardonicus: Spasm of facial muscles producing a characteristic grinning grimace.
• Opisthotonus: Spasmodic hyperextension of the spine (arching backward).
• Pleurothotonus: Spasmodic lateral bending of the body.
• Emprosthotonus: Spasmodic forward bending of the body.
• Trismus (lock jaw) and mydriasis are also prominent.

Crucial Note: Consciousness remains completely intact until the very end, making the death agonizingly painful.

Systemic Complications

  • Hyperthermia
  • Rhabdomyolysis
  • Myoglobinuria
  • Acute renal failure
  • Respiratory failure (primary cause of death)

Treatment Protocol

  1. Control Convulsions: Administer Diazepam IV initially, followed by Phenobarbital. If unmanaged, consider general anesthesia or neuromuscular blockers (Succinylcholine, Curare, Gallamine, or Pancuronium bromide).
  2. Environment: Keep the patient in a dark, quiet room completely free from noise, light, or tactile disturbances (any sensory stimulus can trigger severe spasms).
  3. Specific Antidote: Short-acting barbiturates (Phenobarbital or Sodium Amytal).
  4. Decontamination & Elimination: Gastric lavage with warm water and dilute solution of Potassium Permanganate (KMnO4); administer Activated Charcoal to adsorb remaining alkaloid. Acidify the urine to promote renal elimination of Strychnine.

3. Differential Diagnosis: Strychnine vs. Tetanus

Primary clinical differentials include Tetanus, Rabies, and Meningitis. Below is the detailed comparative analysis between Strychnine Poisoning and Tetanus:

Feature Strychnine Poisoning Tetanus
History No history of injury; potential history of poison ingestion. History of trauma or dirty wound present.
Onset Sudden. Gradual.
Fever Not usual initially (hyperthermia may develop late). Usual.
Convulsions All muscles of the body are affected simultaneously. Muscles affected sequentially; does not involve all muscles at once.
Lower Jaw (Lock Jaw) Does not start in jaw; jaw affected along with body. Usually starts in and specifically affects lower jaw (trismus).
Muscular State Between Fits Muscles are completely relaxed between convulsions. Muscles remain continuously stiff and rigid between fits.
Fatal Period 1 to 2 hours. More than 24 hours.
Laboratory Findings Chemical test positive for Strychnine. Microbiological isolation of Clostridium tetani.

4. Postmortem Findings & Medico-Legal Aspects

Postmortem Findings

  • Postmortem Caloricity: Early postmortem temperature rise instead of the usual cooling process.
  • Rigor Mortis: Early onset and rapid disappearance of rigor mortis due to intense muscle spasm prior to death.
  • Asphyxial Signs: Generalized organ congestion, cyanosis, and froth at mouth and nostrils.
  • Gastrointestinal & Muscular Changes: Congestion of the GI tract; serosal hemorrhages; occasional muscle tearing/hemorrhages.
  • Spinal Cord Pathology: Congested spinal cord. Histology shows multiple capillary ring hemorrhages in anterior and posterior horns, along with neuronal chromatolysis.
Viscera Preservation Requirement:
In addition to standard viscera (stomach, liver, kidney), the entire spinal cord, one half of the brain, and the whole heart must be preserved and submitted for chemical analysis.

Medico-Legal Importance

  • Homicide: Administered as pure alkaloid or crushed Nux vomica seeds, despite its intensely bitter taste.
  • Suicide: Rare due to the agonizing, extremely painful nature of death.
  • Accident: Common due to medicinal overdose, accidental substitution, or children ingesting plant seeds.
  • Other Uses: Employed as arrow poison, cattle poison, and occasionally taken illicitly as an aphrodisiac.

5. Memory Booster (Quick Summary)

Strychnine Summary:
• Source: Strychnos nux-vomica seeds.
• MOA: Blocks glycine receptors in spinal cord ventral horn.
• Triad / Features (@ROPE): Risus sardonicus, Opisthotonus, Pleurothotonus, Emprosthotonus + relaxation between spasms + retained consciousness.
• Key Differential: Tetanus (continuous muscle rigidity vs. complete relaxation in strychnine).
• PM Key Signs: Postmortem caloricity, rapid onset/disappearance of rigor mortis, anterior/posterior horn spinal hemorrhages.
• Treatment: Diazepam/Barbiturates + quiet dark room + urinary acidification.
