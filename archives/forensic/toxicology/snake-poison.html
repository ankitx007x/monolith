Toxicology: Snakes & Animal Irritants

FAST TRACK CLINICAL SCIENCE MBBS - 3rd YEAR (pp. 566–569)

1. Overview & Taxonomy

Animal Irritants

  • Include snakes, scorpions, and cantharides (blister beetles).

Snakes

  • Ectothermic (cold-blooded), limbless vertebrates.
  • Belong to Class Reptilia.
  • More than 3,500 species exist worldwide.
  • Approximately 250 species are venomous.
  • WHO:
    • >2.5 million venomous snake bites occur annually.
    • >125,000 deaths occur each year.

Exam Pearls

  • Venomous species: ~250
  • Snake bites/year: >2.5 million
  • Deaths/year: >125,000

Exam Pearls

3. Snake Venom

Definition

  • Snake venom is a poisonous secretion (modified saliva).
  • Produced by the modified parotid (venom) gland.
  • Fresh venom is clear and amber coloured.
  • Becomes slightly turbid on exposure to sunlight.
  • Dried venom retains the same potency as fresh venom.

Important Exam Point

  • Venom is poisonous only when injected or absorbed through tissues.
  • It is not poisonous when swallowed because digestive enzymes break it down into amino acids.
  • Venom is excreted mainly through the kidneys, and also in milk and saliva/mucous secretions.

Composition of Snake Venom

1. Toxins

  • Low molecular weight proteins and peptides
  • Neurotoxins
    • α-Bungarotoxin → Post-synaptic blockade (Cobra)
    • β-Bungarotoxin → Pre-synaptic blockade (Krait)
  • Cardiotoxins
  • Haemolysins
  • Cytolysins
  • Agglutinins

2. Enzymes

  • Protease
  • Proteinases
  • Phospholipase A
  • Phosphatidases
  • Lecithinase
  • Hyaluronidase (spreading factor)
  • Cholinesterase
  • Anticholinesterase
  • ATPase
  • Transaminase
  • RNase & DNase
  • Ophioxidase

3. Toxalbumins

  • Haemorrhagin
  • Haemolysin
  • Fibrinolysin
  • Proteolysin
  • Thromboplastin

Types of Snake Venom

A. Neurotoxic Venom

  • Seen in: Elapidae (Cobra, Krait)
  • Mechanism
    • Acts like curare
    • Blocks neuromuscular transmission
    • Causes progressive muscular paralysis
  • Order of paralysis
    • Muscles of mouth
    • Muscles of throat
    • Respiratory muscles
  • Local effects
    • Burning pain
    • Rapid oedema
    • Inflammation
    • Serous oozing
  • Systemic effects (15 min–2 hrs)
    • Weakness
    • Giddiness
    • Lethargy
    • Progressive paralysis
    • Bulbar palsy
    • Respiratory failure
    • Coma
    • Death
    • Convulsions (especially Cobra)

B. Haemotoxic Venom

  • Seen in: Viperidae (Russell's viper, Pit viper, Saw-scaled viper, Bamboo pit viper)
  • Mechanism
    • Damages vascular endothelium
    • Haemolysis
    • Coagulation disorders
  • Local effects
    • Severe pain
    • Swelling
    • Ecchymosis
    • Cellulitis
    • Bleeding
  • Systemic effects
    • Shock / cardiovascular collapse
    • Bleeding from mucous membranes
    • Gangrene
    • Renal tubular necrosis
    • Intracerebral haemorrhage
    • Convulsions

C. Myotoxic Venom

  • Seen in: Sea snakes (Hydrophiidae)
  • Local effects
    • Pain
    • Swelling
  • Systemic effects
    • Generalised muscle pain
    • Muscle stiffness
    • Myoglobinuria
    • Renal tubular necrosis

High-Yield Values

  • Fatal dose (dried venom)
    • Cobra → 12 mg
    • Russell's viper → 15 mg
    • Echis → 8 mg
    • Krait → 6 mg
  • Average venom yield
    • Cobra → 170–325 mg
    • Russell's viper → 130–250 mg
    • Krait → 20 mg
    • Echis → 20–35 mg

4. Diagnosis of Snake Bite

  1. Detection of snake venom antigen in:
    • Wound swab
    • Aspirate
    • Biopsy
    • Serum
    • CSF
    • Urine
    • Other body fluids
  2. Radioimmunoassay (RIA)
    • Most sensitive and specific test
    • Detects as low as 0.4 μg/L
  3. ELISA / Enzyme Immunoassay (EIA)
    • Simple and routine investigation
    • Detects ≥ 0.4 μg/L
  4. Detection of cholinesterase and thromboplastin activity.
  5. Bioassay
    • Inject wound extract into frog to demonstrate toxicity.

5. Management of Snake Bite

RIGHT Principle (First Aid)

  • R → Reassure the patient
  • I → Immobilise the affected limb
  • GH → Go to Hospital immediately
  • T → Tell the doctor about systemic symptoms

A. Immediate First Aid

  • Reassure
    • Not every snake is venomous.
    • Even venomous snakes may give a dry bite.
  • Identify snake only if safe.
  • Pressure immobilisation
    • Recommended for Elapid and Sea snake bites.
    • Apply broad firm bandage (50–70 mmHg).
    • Do NOT use arterial tourniquet.
  • Immobilise the limb with a splint.
  • Do NOT:
    • Incise wound
    • Suck venom
    • Cauterise
    • Apply cryotherapy
    • Excise tissue
  • Clean wound with soap & water or iodine.
  • Keep patient in left lateral recovery position.
  • Observe mild cases for 24 hours.
  • Monitor:
    • Pulse
    • Blood pressure
    • SpO₂
    • ECG
  • Give Tetanus toxoid before discharge.

B. Anti-Snake Venom (ASV/PAV)

  • Types
    • Monovalent (species-specific)
    • Polyvalent ASV (PAV)
  • Polyvalent ASV covers:
    • Cobra
    • Krait
    • Russell's viper
    • Echis carinatus
  • Best given: Within 4 hours.
  • Limited benefit after 8 hours.
  • Doubtful value after 24 hours.
  • One vial neutralises approximately 6–8 mg venom.
  • Half-life ≈ 90 hours.

PAV Dose According to Severity

Severity Clinical Features PAV Dose
Minimal Local swelling only; no systemic toxicity 5 vials
Moderate Progressive swelling + systemic symptoms 10 vials
Severe Marked local reaction + severe systemic toxicity 15 vials

Last-Minute Viva Pearls

  • Cobra/Krait → Neurotoxic
  • Viper → Haemotoxic
  • Sea snake → Myotoxic
  • RIGHT = Reassure • Immobilise • Go Hospital • Tell symptoms
  • Best ASV timing → Within 4 hours
  • No incision, suction, cautery or tight tourniquet.

Administration of Anti-Snake Venom (ASV/PAV)

  • Preparation
    • Reconstitute lyophilized ASV in 500 mL Normal Saline.
    • Administer by IV infusion over 30–60 minutes.
    • Alternative: Slow IV injection (<2 mL/min).
  • Repeat Dose (Neurotoxic Snake Bite)
    • If neurological signs persist, repeat 10 vials after 1 hour.
  • Repeat Dose (Haemotoxic Snake Bite)
    • Wait at least 6 hours before repeating ASV.
    • Repeat if:
      • Blood remains incoagulable after 6 hours.
      • Active bleeding continues after 1–2 hours.
  • Standard Initial Dose (India/Nepal)
    • 100 mL (10 vials) Polyvalent ASV.

C. Adverse Reactions to ASV & Supportive Care

Management of Anaphylaxis

Signs: Urticaria, chills, hypotension, bronchospasm, respiratory distress.

  1. Stop ASV infusion immediately.
  2. Give Adrenaline (Epinephrine)
    • Adults → 0.5 mg IM (1:1000)
    • Children → 0.01 mg/kg IM
  3. Give:
    • IV Hydrocortisone
    • Antihistamine
  4. Repeat adrenaline after 10–15 minutes if required.
  5. Restart ASV once the patient is stable.

Management of Neuroparalysis

  • Neostigmine 1.5 mg IM
  • Atropine 0.6 mg IM/IV
  • Repeat up to 2 more doses at 10-minute intervals.

Supportive Treatment

  • Mechanical ventilation for respiratory paralysis.
  • Blood transfusion if indicated.
  • Hemodialysis for acute kidney injury.
  • Broad-spectrum antibiotics when indicated.
  • Sedatives/analgesics for pain and anxiety (especially viper bite).
  • Heparin (1000–5000 IU) for coagulation defects (only when indicated).

ASV Reaction – Viva Sequence

  1. Stop ASV
  2. Adrenaline IM
  3. Hydrocortisone + Antihistamine
  4. Repeat adrenaline if needed
  5. Restart ASV after stabilization

6. Autopsy Findings

External Findings

  • Venomous snake
    • Usually 1–2 fang puncture marks.
    • Depth:
      • Colubrine → ~1.5 cm
      • Viperine → ~2.5 cm
  • Non-venomous snake
    • Multiple teeth marks arranged in a semicircle.
  • Colubrine bite
    • Fluid blood
    • Haemolysed blood
    • Vascular staining
  • Viperine bite
    • Marked swelling
    • Discoloration
    • Cellulitis

Internal Findings

Colubrine (Cobra/Krait)

  • Features of asphyxia
  • Pulmonary oedema
  • Dark fluid blood
  • Venous congestion

Viperine

  • Multiple haemorrhages
  • Lung haemorrhage
  • Pleural haemorrhage
  • Pericardial haemorrhage
  • Acute renal tubular necrosis
  • Cloudy swelling of renal epithelium
  • Desquamation of renal tubular epithelium

7. Medico-Legal Importance

  • Accidental
    • Most common manner of poisoning.
  • Suicidal
    • Very rare.
    • Classical example: Queen Cleopatra.
  • Homicidal
    • Extremely rare.
  • Cattle poisoning
    • Sometimes practiced by hide traders.
  • Forensic identification of venom
    • Colubrine bite → Cholinesterase activity
    • Viperine bite → Thromboplastin activity

Last-Minute Revision

  • Neurotoxic → Repeat ASV after 1 hour.
  • Haemotoxic → Repeat ASV after 6 hours.
  • ASV reaction → Stop ASV → Adrenaline IM → Hydrocortisone → Antihistamine.
  • Neostigmine + Atropine for neuroparalysis.
  • Colubrine → Asphyxia.
  • Viper → Haemorrhage + Acute renal tubular necrosis.
  • Most snake bites are accidental.
