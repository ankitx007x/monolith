Overview & Key Concepts
  • Cholera is an acute, infectious, diarrhoeal disease of the small intestine, caused by the bacteriae Vibrio cholerae O1 (classic or El Tor) transmitted usually by the fecal contamination of food and water.
  • Cholera is a disease of inequity—an ancient illness that today sickens and kills only the poorest and most vulnerable people.
  • The map of cholera is essentially the same as a map of poverty. Every death from cholera is preventable with the tools we have today, putting the goal of ending its public health impact within our reach.
  • Cholera can be controlled with a multi-sector approach—including basic water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) services, and oral cholera vaccines (OCV).
Problem Statement

World

While safe drinking water and advanced sanitation systems have made Europe and North America cholera free for decades, the disease still affects at least 47 countries across the globe, resulting in an estimated 2.9 million cases and 95,000 deaths per year worldwide.

In 2017, cholera continues to hit communities already made vulnerable by tragedies such as conflicts and famines. Yemen currently faces the world's largest cholera outbreak, with over 600,000 suspected cases and more than 2,000 deaths reported since April 2017. Over 800 people have died of cholera in Somalia since the beginning of the year, and over 500 in the DRC. Haiti has now reported over 1 million cases with over 10,000 deaths since the beginning of the 2010 outbreak.

Nepal (FY 2079/80)

In FY 2079/80, 44 cases of cholera have been reported in EWARS (Source: EoMS/EDCD/DOHS). There were 42 cases of Cholera reported from Bagmati Province while Madhesh, Gandaki, Lumbini, and Karnali Provinces reported zero cases.

Reactive Oral Cholera Vaccination (OCV) was done among 44.64% of the target population (n=92,815) in two phases of campaigning. Nine V. cholerae isolates were reported for bacterial diarrhoea. Two isolates of V. cholerae were resistant to ampicillin and one to tetracycline.

Epidemiology

Agent Factors

  • Causative organism: Vibrio cholerae
  • Two serogroups:
    1. V. cholerae O1: Two biotypes (Classic type and El Tor).
    2. V. cholerae O139 (confined to South East Asia).
  • Classical and El Tor varieties can be further classified into serotypes: Inaba, Ogawa (most common in our region), and Hikojima.
  • Morphology & Characteristics:
    • Comma shaped, minute bacillus, size (1–5 mm) × (0.3–0.4 mm).
    • Gram-negative, aerobic, non-spore forming, actively motile (Darting or Scintillating movement), and flagellated bacillus.
  • Resistance:
    • Killed within 30 minutes by heating at 56°C or within a few seconds by boiling.
    • Destroyed by coal tar disinfectants (cresol).
  • Toxin Production:
    • Endotoxin: Lipopolysaccharide O antigen (cell wall).
    • Exotoxin (Enterotoxin): Contains two subunits A and B:
      • B unit: Adheres organism to mucosal membrane of jejunal epithelial cells.
      • A unit: Enzyme moiety consisting of A1 and A2. Subunit A1 activates adenylate cyclase via cAMP pathway, resulting in secretion of water and electrolytes into the intestinal lumen, causing massive purging and watery diarrhea. Subunit A2 links A1 and B.
  • Reservoir of infection: Human being is the only known reservoir (Cases and carriers).
  • Infective materials: Stools and vomit of case and carrier.
  • Infective dose: 1011 organisms required to produce clinical disease in a normal person.
  • Period of communicability: 7–10 days for cases; 2–3 weeks for convalescent carriers; up to 10 years or more for chronic carriers.

Cholera Carriers (4 Types)

  1. Preclinical carrier: Short duration (1–5 days).
  2. Convalescent carrier: 2–3 weeks (those who have not received effective antibiotic treatment).
  3. Contact or healthy carriers: Subclinical infection, gallbladder not infected, duration < 10 days.
  4. Chronic carriers: Have infected gallbladder; antibody titre against V. cholerae O1 remains positive as long as person harbors organism.

Host Factors

  • Gastric acidity: Destroys V. cholerae at pH < 5; increased risk of infection in achlorhydric persons and those taking antacids.
  • Population mobility: Increases exposure to infection.
  • Economic status: Highest incidence in low socio-economic groups.
  • Immunity: Conferred by natural infection and vaccination.

Environmental Factors

  • Season: High in May and June months.
  • Climate: Cholera tends to occur only when the absolute humidity exceeds 10 mmHg vapor pressure.
  • Sanitation & Hygiene: Poor environmental sanitation, contaminated water and food, and high fly population.

Modes of Transmission

Feco-oral route, not only through water, but also through contaminated food, fruits, and vegetables. House flies act as mechanical vectors. Contaminated fingers and fomites also transmit the disease.

Predisposing Factors

Poverty, illiteracy, ignorance, poor standard of living with lack of sanitation.

Incubation Period & Nature of Stool

  • Incubation Period: Varies from a few hours to a few days (Average 1–2 days).
  • Nature of Stool: "Rice-watery" stools caused by classical vibrios; watery stools by El Tor vibrios.
Clinical Features

The majority of cases are mild or asymptomatic. Typical severe features present in three distinct stages:

  1. Stage of Evacuation: Sudden onset of painless, profuse, effortless, watery diarrhea followed by vomiting. Rice water stools passed as many as 40 times per day.
  2. Stage of Collapse: Rapid onset of dehydration and collapse characterized by sunken eyes, hollow cheeks, scaphoid abdomen, hypotension, hypothermia, rapid, feeble, and thready pulse, loss of skin elasticity, and hurried respirations.
    • Decreased output of urine indicates anuria and renal failure.
    • Kussmaul's type of respiration indicates acidosis.
    • Dehydration leads to acidosis, renal failure, and death.
  3. Stage of Recovery: Patient shows signs of clinical improvement as fluid balance is restored.
Laboratory Investigations

Consists mainly of examination of stools of a cholera patient, water sample of the suspected source, vomitus, and food samples.

Methods of Collection of Stool

  1. Rubber catheter method (Best method): A sterile rubber catheter (no. 26 to 28) is lubricated with liquid paraffin and passed into the rectum for about 5 cm. Stool is collected into a sterile container / McCartney bottle (30 mL) containing holding/transport media (Venkataraman-Ramakrishnan medium or Alkaline Peptone Water).
  2. Rectal swab method: A sterile rectal swab is dipped into holding medium, inserted into the rectum, swabbed, placed in a sterile plastic bag, tightly sealed, and sent to the lab.

Collection of Water Samples

About 1 to 3 liters of water collected from suspected source mixed with 10% alkaline-peptone (A-P) water in a ratio of 9:1 (9 volumes water to 1 volume A-P water), sealed, and sent by quickest transport.

Culture & Identification

Stool specimen is inoculated into enrichment medium (potassium tellurite) and incubated for 6 hours, then inoculated onto Bile Salt Agar (BSA) or Meat Extract Agar and incubated for 24–48 hours. (Cary-Blair media is used if collected via rectal swab).

Colonies appear as translucent, moist, smooth, raised, and easily emulsifiable colonies (~1 mm diameter).

  • Gram Staining: Gram-negative curved rods.
  • Hanging Drop Preparation: Characteristic scintillating/darting movements.
  • Dark Field Illumination: Look like "shooting stars in a dark sky".
  • Serological Tests: Agglutination with specific antisera.
  • Biochemical Examination: Fermentation with acid production in sucrose and mannose, but NOT arabinose.
  • Other Tests: Hemolytic test, Voges-Proskauer (V-P) test, Polymyxin B sensitivity test.
Prevention and Control

1. Elimination of Reservoirs

  • Cases: Isolation in hospital until 2 to 3 consecutive stool cultures are negative. Concurrent and terminal disinfection of stools and vomitus using cresol. Chemotherapy: Erythromycin and azithromycin are drugs of choice (Tetracycline and Ciprofloxacin used in susceptible areas). Rehydration therapy (Oral/IV depending on degree of dehydration).
  • Carriers: Detected by stool culture and treated accordingly.

3. Protection of Susceptibles (Immunization)

a. Parenteral Vaccine

  • Heat killed, phenol preserved vaccine.
  • Each 0.5 mL dose contains 3000 million killed organisms of Inaba and Ogawa serotypes (classical V. cholerae O1), providing cross-immunity against El Tor.
  • Schedule: 2 doses deep IM, 0.5 mL each, 4–6 weeks apart. (Half dose for children < 10 yrs; infants not immunized).
  • Immunity develops after ~15 days and lasts only 5–6 months.
  • Limitations: Outbreak response usage is ineffective due to 15-day delay in antibody production relative to short incubation period.

b. Oral Vaccines (OCV)

  1. Killed whole cell V. cholerae O1 + recombinant B-subunit:
    • For ≥ 6 years old: 2 oral doses ≥ 7 days apart.
    • For < 6 years old: 3 doses given.
    • Confers 50–60% protection lasting up to 3 years.
  2. Live attenuated CVD 103-HgR vaccine:
    • Single dose vaccine conferring 80% protection.
    • Avoid oral antibiotics 1 week before and after administration.
    • Note: No longer produced. Mass prophylaxis not recommended.

4. Health Education

Most effective prophylactic measure. Key educational messages:

  • Routes of transmission and consequences of dehydration.
  • Avoiding open-air defecation and using sanitary latrines.
  • Drinking boiled and cooled water.
  • Immediate initiation of ORT at diarrhea onset.
  • Prompt medical treatment for severe diarrhea.
  • Hand hygiene with soap and water before handling food and after toilet use.
  • Fly control through clean domestic surroundings.

Cholera Control & Global Elimination Roadmap

Cholera control requires both emergency response during outbreaks and long-term development strategies in endemic areas. Key integrated approaches include:

  • Enhanced surveillance: Epidemiological and laboratory surveillance to identify endemic areas and respond rapidly.
  • Universal safe WASH: Universal access to safe drinking water and basic sanitation.
  • Community engagement: Behavioral change communication and improved hygiene practices.
  • Quick access to treatment: Oral Rehydration Solution (ORS), IV fluids, and antibiotics for severe cases.
  • Vaccination: Oral Cholera Vaccines (OCV) in target groups.

Cholera Elimination Definition

Any country that reports no confirmed cases with evidence of local transmission for at least three consecutive years and maintains a well-functioning epidemiologic and laboratory surveillance system capable of detecting and confirming cases.

Ending Cholera: A Global Roadmap to 2030

  1. Early detection and quick response to contain outbreaks: Focuses on rapid containment via community engagement, early warning surveillance, laboratory capacity building, health system readiness, and rapid response teams (RRTs).
  2. Targeted multi-sectoral approach to prevent cholera recurrence: Focuses on "cholera hotspots"—small geographic areas with ongoing/seasonal transmission (40 to 80 million people live in cholera hotspots in Africa alone). Stopped via WASH and OCV interventions.
  3. Effective mechanism of coordination: Supported by the Global Task Force on Cholera Control (GTFCC) to coordinate technical assistance, advocacy, resource mobilization, and cross-sectoral partnerships.

Implementing the Global Roadmap can save up to 50% in response costs while accelerating progress toward the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).

Cholera Control Context in Nepal
  • Establishment of anti-microbial sentinel surveillance systems nationwide to identify cholera and deploy WHO stockpile vaccines.
  • Strengthening outbreak preparedness via:
    • Early Warning and Reporting System (EWARS) and rapid response teams.
    • Expanded community health education.
    • Adequate stockpiling and distribution of ORS.
    • Implementation of OCV in high-risk communities (e.g., DOVE: Delivering Oral Cholera Vaccine Effectively).

WASH Strategy in Disaster / Flood Settings

In waterborne diseases (cholera, typhoid), the WASH strategy (Water, Sanitation, Hygiene, and Health Education) is critical during floods and emergencies. Key interventions include water supply disinfection, water quality monitoring, and safe excreta disposal in homes and communities.
