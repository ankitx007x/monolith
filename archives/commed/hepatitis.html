Applied Epidemiology: Viral Hepatitis (A, B, C, D, E)

FAST TRACK CLINICAL SCIENCE MBBS - 3rd YEAR

Hepatitis A

Synonyms: Acute infectious hepatitis; Epidemic jaundice; Botkin's disease; MS1 hepatitis; Australia antigen negative hepatitis[cite: 2]

It is an acute, communicable, inflammatory disease of the liver caused by Hepatitis A virus (HAV)[cite: 2].

Problem Statement of Hepatitis in FY 2079/80

(Source: DoHS 2079/80)[cite: 2]

  • Hepatitis A is endemic disease in all the developing countries, because of lack of sanitation and poor hygiene, often giving rise to epidemic[cite: 2].
  • 173 cases of Hepatitis Acute jaundice has been observed in FY 2079/80 which is higher than recorded in previous year (136 cases)[cite: 2]. Highest cases have been reported in Lumbini province (127 cases) followed by Bagmati (22 cases) and Koshi (14 cases)[cite: 2]. Least cases were from Madhesh and Karnali (1 case in each)[cite: 2].

Epidemiology

Agent Factors

  • Causative agent: Hepatitis A virus (HAV), Enterovirus, Family: Picornaviridae[cite: 2]
  • Non-enveloped single stranded RNA virus[cite: 2]
  • Only one serotype known[cite: 2]
  • Resistance:
    • Can withstand heat at 60°C[cite: 2]
    • Destroyed at 100°C within a few minutes[cite: 2]
    • Formalin and 0.5% sodium hypochlorite are effective disinfectants[cite: 2]
  • Reservoir of infection: Human being is only known reservoir[cite: 2]
  • Infective material: Feces of the case or the carrier[cite: 2]
  • Period of infectivity: Individual is highly infectious during last 2 weeks of incubation period[cite: 2]

Host Factor

  • Age: Incidence is high among children of 5-15 years age[cite: 2]
  • Sex: Equal in both the sexes[cite: 2]
  • Immunity: One attack confers lifelong immunity[cite: 2]
  • Pregnancy:
    • Hepatitis A during pregnancy carries a high mortality[cite: 2]
    • No transplacental transmission[cite: 2]

Environmental factors

  • Poor standard of living with lack of sanitation and overcrowding[cite: 2]
  • Mode of transmission: Mainly by feco-oral route, i.e. by fecal contamination of water, milk, food, fruits and vegetables[cite: 2]

Feco-oral route of transmission

Feces (of reservoir)
↓
Fluids (water, milk) / Food / Fruits and vegetables / Fomites (utensils, spoons, etc.) / Fingers / Flies
↓
Mouth (of susceptibles)
Note: 6Fs
Feces → Fluids, Food, Fruits and vegetables, Fomites, Fingers, Flies → Mouth[cite: 2]
  • Carrier: House flies act as mechanical carrier[cite: 2]
  • Incubation period: 10 to 50 days (usually 25 to 30 days)[cite: 2]

Clinical Features

i. Pre-icteric stage (Prodromal stage)

  • Characterised by sudden onset of fever, associated with chills, fatigue, malaise, headache and body ache[cite: 2]
  • In 1-2 days: Gastrointestinal symptoms (anorexia, nausea, vomiting)[cite: 2]
  • In 3-5 days: High colored urine, light colored stools[cite: 2]

ii. Icteric stage

  • Characterised by onset of jaundice (yellow coloration of sclera), high colored urine, hepatomegaly[cite: 2]
  • Lasts for 4-6 weeks[cite: 2]

iii. Convalescent stage (Stage of recovery)

Starts after about 6 to 8 weeks and lasts for 2 to 6 weeks[cite: 2]

Investigations

  • Increase in serum bilirubin level[cite: 2]
  • Demonstration of viral particles in the stools, by electron microscopy[cite: 2]
  • Demonstration of a rise in HAV antibody titer[cite: 2]
  • Demonstration of bile salts and bile pigments in the urine[cite: 2]

Prevention and Control

Elimination of Reservoirs:

This is difficult because of the following reasons[cite: 2]:

  • There is no treatment[cite: 2].
  • The individual will have already spread the disease before clinical diagnosis is made (because the cases will be shedding the virus in last 2 weeks of incubation period and first week of illness)[cite: 2].
  • There will be existence of large number of subclinical cases acting as carriers. Difficult to identify them[cite: 2].
  • Infectivity comes down with the onset of jaundice[cite: 2].
  • Isolation of the cases also does not help because of above mentioned reasons[cite: 2].

However, concurrent disinfection of patient's excreta by using 0.5 percent sodium hypochlorite must be carried out[cite: 2]. Usually this disease occurs among the people of low socio-economic profile[cite: 2].

Management

  • Absolute bed-rest is traditional. This prevents the patient from getting fatigue, exhaustion, etc[cite: 2]
  • Avoiding fatty and oily foods will prevent the liver from secreting more of bile juice, thereby liver gets rest[cite: 2]
  • Energy requirements, therefore, have to be made up with carbohydrate rich diet[cite: 2]
  • Symptomatic treatment is given with antipyretics and anti-emetics[cite: 2]
  • Antibiotics play no role. However, neomycin can be given in advanced cases[cite: 2]
  • Steroids play no role, except that they give symptomatic relief, they do not alter the course of the disease. They are indicated only in advanced cases[cite: 2]
  • Fluids and electrolyte balance will have to be maintained[cite: 2]

Breaking the channel of transmission

  • This is the most important measure in the control of an outbreak of VHA[cite: 2].
  • Since the disease is transmitted by feco-oral route, it can be broken by construction of 'sanitation barrier', which prevents the access of the pathogens from feces of the cases to the mouths of susceptible through 6 Fs (Feces, Fluids, Food, Fruits and vegetables, Fomites, Fingers, Flies)[cite: 2].

Protection of susceptible

Immunization:[cite: 2]

A. Active immunization:
Two types of HAV vaccines are now available, namely Inactivated vaccines and live attenuated vaccine[cite: 2].

1. Inactivated vaccine:[cite: 2]

  • It is a cell-culture liquid vaccine[cite: 2].
  • Each 1 ml contains not less than 720 Elisa-units of viral antigens...[cite: 2]
  • Dose: 1 ml for adults and 0.5 ml for children (Infants are not given)[cite: 2]
  • Route: Intramuscular in deltoid region[cite: 2]
  • Primary course consists of 2 doses with 4 to 6 weeks interval, followed by a booster dose after 6 to 12 months[cite: 2]
  • Storage temperature: 2 to 8°C[cite: 2]
  • A combination vaccine (Hepatitis-A + Hepatitis-B) is given as 3 dose series, at 0, 1, 6 months schedule[cite: 2]
  • Immunity lasts for 15-20 years[cite: 2]

2. Live vaccine:[cite: 2]

  • A live, attenuated freeze dried vaccine, containing H2 strain of Hepatitis A virus, cultured on human diploid cells[cite: 2]
  • Dose: single dose of 0.5 ml[cite: 2]
  • Route: subcutaneous, in the deltoid region[cite: 2]
  • No booster dose is required[cite: 2]
  • Storage temperature: +2°C to 8°C[cite: 2]
  • Immunity lasts for 15 years[cite: 2]

B. Passive immunization: Not in use[cite: 2]

Hepatitis B

Synonyms: Serum hepatitis; Australia antigen hepatitis; Hippy hepatitis; Homologous serum jaundice; MS2 hepatitis; Tattoo jaundice[cite: 2]

It is an acute systemic infection with major pathology in the liver caused by hepatitis B virus (HBV) and transmitted usually by the parenteral route[cite: 2].

Problem Statement

  • Hepatitis B is endemic throughout the world, especially in tropical and developing countries and in some regions of Europe[cite: 2]
  • The virus causes 60-80% of all primary liver cancer which is one of the three top causes of cancer death in east and SEAR[cite: 2]
  • Based on the different HBsAg carrier rates, country can be divided into 3 categories:[cite: 2]
    • i. High endemicity (>8%): Type 3 countries: Bangladesh, Korea, Myanmar, Thailand[cite: 2]
    • ii. Intermediate (2-8%): Type 2 countries: Bhutan, India, Indonesia and Maldives[cite: 2]
    • iii. Low endemicity (<2%): Type 1 countries: Nepal and Sri Lanka[cite: 2]

Epidemiological Determinants

Agent Factors

  • a. Agent: Causative agent: Hepatitis B virus (HBV) - Hepadnavirus[cite: 2].
    • HBV is a complex, double shelled DNA virus, originally known as the "Dane particle"[cite: 2]
    • It replicates in the liver cells[cite: 2]
    • Occurs in 3 morphological forms in the serum of patient:[cite: 2]
      • i. Small spherical particles (antigenic and stimulate production of surface antibodies): 22-nm particle[cite: 2]
      • ii. Tubules of varying length and diameter[cite: 2]
      • iii. Dane particle which corresponds morphologically to hepatitis B virus → Infectious...[cite: 2]
  • b. Reservoir of infection: Man is only reservoir (carriers or case)[cite: 2]
  • c. Carriers:[cite: 2]
    • i. Super carrier: With high HBeAg are super carriers. Contains high HBsAg titre, DNA polymerase, HBV[cite: 2]
    • ii. Simple carrier: No HBeAg but with low titre of HBsAg. HBV and DNA polymerase absent[cite: 2]
  • d. Infective material:[cite: 2]
    • Contaminated blood (main source of infection)[cite: 2]
    • Body secretions like saliva, vaginal secretions and semen[cite: 2]
  • e. Resistance: Stable and capable of surviving for days on environmental surfaces[cite: 2]
  • f. Period of communicability: Months to years[cite: 2]

Host Factors

  • a. Age:
    • Perinatal: Approximately 1% occurrence of HBV but highest chance to develop chronic HBV (70% approx.)[cite: 2]
    • 1-5 years: 10%[cite: 2]
    • >5 years: 30%[cite: 2]
  • b. High risk group: Surgeons, dentists, nurses, lab-technicians and blood bank workers. High incidence among homosexuals, sex workers, IV drug abusers and infants of HBV carrier mothers[cite: 2].
  • c. Humoral and cellular response:
    • Hepatitis B virus has 3 distinct antigens: Surface antigen/Australia antigen (HBsAg), Core antigen (HBcAg) and e-antigen (HBeAg)[cite: 2].
    • These antigens stimulate the production of corresponding antibodies e.g. Surface antibody (anti-HBs), core antibody (anti-HBc) and e antibody (anti-HBe)[cite: 2].
    • Antibodies are produced about 10 days after onset of jaundice[cite: 2].
    • Order of antibodies production: Core-antibody, e-antibody and lastly surface antibody[cite: 2].
    • These antibodies and their antigens constitute very useful markers of HBV infection[cite: 2].

Mode of Transmission

  • i. Parenteral route: Commonest route of transmission. Disease is transmitted through contaminated blood and blood-products through transfusion, dialysis, use of contaminated needles, syringes, drip sets, etc.[cite: 2]
  • ii. Percutaneous route: Accidental inoculations can occur during surgery, dental procedures, tattooing, ear piercing, ritual circumcision, acupuncture[cite: 2]
  • iii. Direct contact: Deep kissing, sexual intercourse[cite: 2]
  • iv. Vertical transmission (perinatal transmission): From carrier mother to their babies[cite: 2]
  • v. Horizontal transmission: Child to child through physical contact with skin conditions like scabies and impetigo. This was observed in Nepal[cite: 2]

Incubation Period: 50-150 days (average = 100 days)[cite: 2]

Clinical Features & Clinical Course

Clinical course of Hepatitis B on Exposure:[cite: 2]

  • Acute hepatitis B infection: 33%[cite: 2]
    • Fulminant: 0.5%[cite: 2]
    • Clinical infection (Jaundice): 33% → Recovery / Immunity[cite: 2]
    • Sub-clinical infection (asymptomatic / flu-like): 66%[cite: 2]
  • Chronic carrier / Chronic liver disease: 5-10% (Adults), 70-90% (Perinatal)[cite: 2]
    • Minimal disease / Chronic hepatitis / Reactivation → Cirrhosis → Primary liver cancer → Death[cite: 2]

Laboratory Diagnosis

Serum to be examined for useful markers:[cite: 2]

Markers of Hepatitis B infection (in order of appearance in serum):[cite: 2]

Antigens:

  • HBsAg (Hepatitis B surface antigen): Also known as Australia antigen. First antigen to appear in serum — first evidence of infection. Epidemiological marker of Hepatitis B infection[cite: 2].
  • HBcAg (Hepatitis B core antigen): Alone does not appear in serum. HBcAg remains in hepatocytes for assembly of complete virions. It cannot be detected in blood but can be demonstrated in nuclei of hepatocytes in carrier state and in chronic hepatitis but not during acute stage[cite: 2].
  • HBeAg (Hepatitis B envelope antigen): Is a secretory form of HBcAg. 'Indicates active viral replication'. 'Is a marker of infectivity for Hepatitis B'[cite: 2].
Note: Persistence of HBeAg beyond 3 months: Increased likelihood of chronic Hepatitis B[cite: 2].

Antibodies:

  • Anti-HBc (Antibody to Hepatitis B core antigen): First antibody to appear in serum. IgM Anti-HBc indicates a diagnosis of acute Hepatitis B. IgG Anti-HBc persists indefinitely. It is indicative of hepatocyte destruction[cite: 2].
  • Anti-HBe (Antibody to Hepatitis B envelope antigen): Starts to appear after peak acute infection. Signals 'stoppage of active viral replication'. Indicates 'end of period of infectivity'[cite: 2].
  • Anti-HBs (Antibody to Hepatitis B surface antigen): Last antibody to appear in serum. Signals 'recovery, end of period of communicability'[cite: 2].
Note on Window Period:
Anti-HBs is usually not detectable for a few weeks after disappearance of HBsAg. This period is called window period. During this period, only IgM anti-HBc is detectable[cite: 2].

Prevention and Control

Elimination of reservoir

This is not possible because there is no treatment[cite: 2].

Blocking the channel of transmission

This is possible in some cases by the following measures:[cite: 2]

  • Sterilization of syringes, needles, catguts, surgical instruments, etc.[cite: 2]
  • Avoiding sharing of toothbrush, razors, syringes among drug-abusers, etc.[cite: 2]
  • Screening of blood donors for HBsAg[cite: 2]
  • Avoiding homosexuality and multiple sexual partners[cite: 2]
  • Instructions to the carriers — not to donate blood, not to share their razors, bath brush, tooth-brush, etc. with others, and to use condoms while having sex[cite: 2]
  • Sterilization of instruments (simple heating over a flame) before adopting procedures like nose piercing, ear pricking, acupuncture and tattooing[cite: 2]

Protection of susceptibles

A. Active immunization:[cite: 2]

1. Hepatitis B vaccine:[cite: 2]

  • a. Plasma derived vaccine: It is a formalin inactivated sub-unit viral vaccine for i.m. injection. Based on surface antigen (HBsAg). Vaccine has no effect on HBsAg carriers and is unnecessary in persons with surface antibody from previous infection[cite: 2].
  • b. Recombinant DNA-yeast derived vaccine: It is a recombinant DNA vaccine elaborated from cultures of yeast cloned with HBsAg gene. It is immunogenic, safe and effective as the plasma derived vaccine[cite: 2].
    • Dose for adults is 10-20 μg at 0, 1, and 6 months[cite: 2]
    • Newborn and pediatric dose is half the adult dose[cite: 2]
    • Booster dose is usually not required[cite: 2]
    • Each 1.0 ml dose of vaccine contains 20 μg of HBsAg[cite: 2]
    • An effective antibody response is generally attained after 3 doses in 95% of vaccinees[cite: 2]
    • Provides immunity for 3-5 years[cite: 2]
    • Applicable for both pre-exposure and post-exposure prophylaxis[cite: 2]

2. Hepatitis B immunoglobulin (HBIG):[cite: 2]

  • Used for immediate protection[cite: 2]
  • For those acutely exposed to HBsAg positive blood like surgeons, laboratory workers, newborn infant of carrier mother[cite: 2]
  • Should be given as soon as possible ideally within 6 hours and not later than 48 hours[cite: 2]
  • 2 doses given 30 days apart[cite: 2]
  • Provides short-term passive protection which lasts for approx. 3 months[cite: 2]

B. Passive-active immunization:
Simultaneous administration of HBIG & Hepatitis B vaccine is more efficacious than HBIG alone[cite: 2].

Other Measures

  • All blood donors should be screened for HBV infection[cite: 2]
  • Adequate sterilization of all instruments and adoption of simple hygienic measures[cite: 2]
  • Carriers should be told not to share razors or toothbrushes and use barrier methods of contraception and they should not donate blood[cite: 2]

Hepatitis C

Synonyms: Parenterally transmitted non-A non-B hepatitis[cite: 2]

It is caused by Hepatitis C virus (HCV)[cite: 2].

Problem Statement

WHO estimates that 3% of world population is infected with HCV and around 170 million individuals are chronic carriers at risk of developing liver cirrhosis and liver cancer[cite: 2].

Epidemiology

  • Agent: Hepatitis C virus is single-stranded RNA virus belonging to flavivirus group[cite: 2]. It bears no genomic resemblance to hepatitis B or D[cite: 2].
  • Mode of Transmission:
    • Transfusion of blood and blood products[cite: 2]
    • Intravenous drug users (50%)[cite: 2]
    • Transmitted sexually and perinatally (low rate)[cite: 2]
    • As occupational hazards among health care workers, etc.[cite: 2]
  • Incubation period: 6-8 weeks[cite: 2]

Clinical Features

  • Characterised by mild illness with insidious onset of jaundice and malaise, milder than that of hepatitis B[cite: 2]
  • 50% cases are asymptomatic[cite: 2]
  • Remaining 50% of cases may develop chronic liver diseases such as cirrhosis of liver or even hepatocellular carcinoma[cite: 2]

Diagnosis

  • By exclusion of type A and type B[cite: 2]
  • Serological test for anti-HCV[cite: 2]

Prevention

  • No vaccine, so prevention is difficult[cite: 2]
  • Health care workers should stick to universal precautions as in Hepatitis B prevention[cite: 2]
  • Health education programs[cite: 2]
  • Testing donated blood for HCV[cite: 2]
  • Treatment: By Interferon[cite: 2]

Hepatitis E

Caused by hepatitis E virus and is essentially a water-borne disease[cite: 2].

  • Agent: HEV RNA virus[cite: 2]
  • Transmission: Occurs through consumption of water and food materials contaminated by feces[cite: 2]
  • Incubation period: 2-9 weeks[cite: 2]
  • A self-limiting viral hepatitis lasting for several weeks, which is followed by recovery[cite: 2]
  • No chronic cases have been reported[cite: 2]
  • HEV has propensity to induce fulminating form of acute disease particularly in pregnant women, up to 20% of whom may develop fulminating hepatitis E, with a mortality of 80% of such cases[cite: 2].
  • Hepatitis E infection during pregnancy is responsible for abortions, intrauterine death and high perinatal morbidity and mortality[cite: 2].
  • Diagnosis: Made by anti-HEV antibodies in the serum[cite: 2].
  • General precautions: As outlined for hepatitis A[cite: 2].
  • Treatment: There is no specific treatment for hepatitis E, only symptomatic measures are required[cite: 2].
