Definition & Overview

Diarrhoea is defined as the passage of loose, liquid or watery stool usually more than three times a day.

However, important is the recent change in consistency and character of stools rather than the number of stools.

Clinical Types of Diarrhoeal Disease

  • i. Acute watery diarrhea
    • Lasts several hours to days
    • Dangers: Dehydration, weight loss
    • Pathogens: Caused by V. cholerae or E. coli bacteria as well as rotavirus
  • ii. Acute bloody diarrhea
    • Also called dysentery
    • Dangers: Damage of intestinal mucosa, sepsis, malnutrition, dehydration
    • Causative pathogen: Shigella
  • iii. Persistent diarrhoea
    • Lasts for 14 days or longer
    • Dangers: Malnutrition, non-intestinal infection, dehydration
    • Patients with other illness such as AIDS are more likely to develop persistent diarrhoea.
  • iv. Diarrhoea with severe malnutrition (marasmus and kwashiorkor)
    • Dangers: Severe systemic infection, dehydration, heart failure, vitamin and mineral deficiency

Problem Statement

Global

  • Diarrheal disease is a major public health problem in all the developing countries like Asia, Africa, and Latin America.
  • It is estimated that nearly 750 million children below 5 years of age suffer from diarrheal diseases every year.
  • Nearly 5 million children under-fives die from diarrheal disease every year, at the rate of 10 per minute.
  • 9% of the global mortality rate under 5 is accounted to diarrhoea and 9 million hospitalizations occur annually.

Nepal (Source: DoHS 2079/80)

  • In FY 2079/80, incidence of diarrhoea was 115.1 per 1,000 under 5 children with a threefold decrease from previous fiscal years.
  • Karnali Province had the highest incidence (216), followed by Lumbini Province (161.6), and the lowest incidence was reported in Bagmati Province (78.8).
  • There were four diarrhoeal deaths: one death each was recorded in Bagmati, Madhesh, Karnali, and Sudurpaschim Provinces, with no reported diarrhoeal deaths in Lumbini, Koshi, and Gandaki Provinces.
  • The proportion of diarrhoeal cases treated with ORS and Zinc as per IMNCI national protocol at national level was 93.6%, which was similar to the previous year trend, with more than 90% of diarrheal cases treated with Zinc and ORS in all provinces.
  • There were 0.6% of diarrheal cases treated with intravenous (IV) fluid.

Epidemiological Determinants

1. Agent Factors

i. Viruses

  • Rotaviruses (Leading cause of severe dehydrating diarrhea in children aged <5 years; reinfection is common)
  • Astroviruses
  • Adenoviruses
  • Calciviruses
  • Coronaviruses
  • Norwalk group viruses
  • Enteroviruses
  • Cytomegaloviruses

ii. Bacteria

  • Campylobacter jejuni
  • Enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC)
  • Shigella
  • Vibrio cholerae
  • Vibrio parahaemolyticus
  • Bacillus cereus
  • Staphylococcus aureus
  • Clostridium perfringens
  • Clostridium difficile
  • Aeromonas, Yersinia, Chlamydia, N. gonorrhoeae
Note:
- Enterotoxigenic E. coli is the most common cause of traveler's diarrhea. It produces Heat-labile (LT) and Heat-stable (ST) toxins.
- Campylobacter is the commonest cause of enteritis.
- Salmonella causes inflammation of bowel epithelium.
- Heat-labile toxin (LT) of Enterotoxigenic E. coli resembles cholera toxin. However, cholera toxin is 100 times more powerful.

iii. Others

  • E. histolytica
  • Giardia intestinalis
  • Trichuriasis
  • Cryptosporidium spp.
  • Intestinal worms
  • Malnutrition leads to kwashiorkor, sprue, celiac disease, and pellagra—all associated with diarrhoea.

Reservoir of Infection

  • Man: Principal reservoir in ETEC, Shigella spp., V. cholerae, Giardia lamblia, E. histolytica.
  • Animals: Important reservoir in Campylobacter jejuni, Salmonella spp., and Yersinia enterocolitica.

Host Factors

  • Common in children between 6 months and 2 years. Highest incidence in age group 6–11 months.
  • Common in babies under 6 months of age fed on cow's milk or commercial formulas.
  • Contributing factors: Poverty, prematurity, reduced gastric acidity, immunodeficiency, lack of personal and domestic hygiene, and incorrect feeding practices.

Environmental Factors

  • Temperate climate: Bacterial diarrhea common during warm season; viral diarrhoea common during winter.
  • Tropical area: Bacterial diarrhoea peaks during warmer, rainy season; Rotavirus diarrhoea occurs throughout the year.

Mode of Transmission

  • Faeco-oral route
  • Water-borne
  • Food-borne
  • Direct transmission

Incubation period: Varies from a few hours to a few days.

Clinical Features

Clinical features are mainly due to dehydration:

  • Early features: Child is irritable, thirsty, and drinks water eagerly.
  • Next phase: Child becomes more irritable, develops a pinched look due to dry and sunken eyes, anterior fontanelle is depressed in an infant, tongue becomes dry, abdomen becomes scaphoid, passes urine at longer intervals.
  • Progressive stage: Child develops shock characterized by all features of peripheral circulatory failure (hypothermia, rapid and thready pulse, drowsiness or even unconsciousness, hypotension).
  • Kussmaul type of respiration and anuria.

Degree of Dehydration

Necessary for management purposes. Done by looking and feeling for signs of dehydration, including three key signs (*):

  1. General condition of child
  2. Thirst
  3. Skin pinch

Management of Diarrhoeal Diseases

General Principle: Give extra fluid for diarrhoea and continue feeding.

PLAN A: Treat Diarrhoea at Home

Counsel the mother on the 4 Rules of Home Treatment:

  1. Give Extra Fluid
  2. Give Zinc Supplements (age 2 months up to 5 years)
  3. Continue Feeding
  4. When to Return

1. Give Extra Fluid (as much as the child will take)

Tell the mother:

  • Breastfeed frequently and for longer at each feed.
  • If exclusively breastfed, give ORS or clean water in addition to breast milk.
  • If not exclusively breastfed, give one or more of: ORS solution, food-based fluids (soup, rice water, yoghurt drinks), or clean water.

Especially important to give ORS at home when:

  • Child has been treated with Plan B or Plan C during this visit.
  • Child cannot return to a clinic if diarrhoea gets worse.
  • Teach mother how to mix and give ORS. Give her 2 packets of ORS to use at home.

Fluid volume in addition to usual intake:

  • Up to 2 years: 50 to 100 ml after each loose stool
  • 2 years or more: 100 to 200 ml after each loose stool

Instructions for administration:

  • Give frequent small sips from a cup.
  • If child vomits, wait 10 minutes, then continue more slowly.
  • Continue giving extra fluid until diarrhoea stops.

2. Give Zinc (age 2 months up to 5 years)

Tell the mother how much zinc to give (20 mg tablet):

  • 2 months up to 6 months: ½ tablet (10 mg) daily for 14 days
  • 6 months or more: 1 tablet (20 mg) daily for 14 days

How to give:

  • Infants: Dissolve tablet in small amount of expressed breast milk, ORS, or clean water in a cup.
  • Older children: Tablets can be chewed or dissolved in a small amount of water.

3. Continue Feeding

Exclusive breastfeeding if age is less than 6 months.

4. When to Return

PLAN B: Treat Some Dehydration with ORS

In the clinic, give recommended amount of ORS over a 4-hour period.

Determine Amount of ORS to Give During First 4 Hours

Weight <6 kg 6 to <10 kg 10 to 12 kg 12 to 19 kg

*Use child's age only when weight is unknown. Approximate ORS volume (ml) = Weight (kg) × 75.

  • If the child wants more ORS than shown, give more.
  • For non-breastfed infants <6 months using standard ORS, give 100–200 ml clean water during this period (not needed with low osmolarity ORS).

Show Mother How to Give ORS Solution

  • Give frequent small sips from a cup.
  • If child vomits, wait 10 minutes, then continue more slowly.
  • Continue breastfeeding whenever child wants.

After 4 Hours

  • Reassess child and classify for dehydration.
  • Select appropriate plan (A, B, or C) to continue treatment.
  • Begin feeding child in clinic.

If Mother Must Leave Before Completing Treatment

  • Show her how to prepare ORS solution at home.
  • Show her how much ORS to give to finish 4-hour treatment.
  • Give enough ORS packets to complete rehydration + 2 additional packets (Plan A).
  • Explain 4 Rules of Home Treatment.

PLAN C: Treat Severe Dehydration Quickly

Follow the algorithm decision tree:

START HERE: Can you give intravenous (IV) fluid immediately?

IF YES:

Start IV fluid immediately. If child can drink, give ORS by mouth while drip is set up.
Give 100 ml/kg Ringer's Lactate Solution (or Normal Saline), divided as follows:
Age First give 30 ml/kg in: Then give 70 ml/kg in:
Infants (<12 months) 1 hour* 5 hours
Children (12 months to 5 years) 30 minutes* 2 ½ hours

*Repeat once if radial pulse is still very weak or not detectable.

  • Reassess child every 1–2 hours. If hydration status is not improving, give IV drip more rapidly.
  • Also give ORS (about 5 ml/kg/hour) as soon as child can drink (usually after 3–4 hours in infants, 1–2 hours in children).
  • Reassess infant after 6 hours and child after 3 hours. Classify dehydration and choose Plan A, B, or C.

IF NO IV IMMEDIATELY: Is IV treatment available nearby (within 30 minutes)?

  • YES: Refer URGENTLY to hospital for IV treatment. If child can drink, provide mother with ORS solution to give frequent sips during transport.
  • NO: Check if trained to use a naso-gastric (NG) tube for rehydration.

IF NG TUBE AVAILABLE:

Start rehydration by tube or mouth with ORS solution: give 20 ml/kg/hour for 6 hours (total 120 ml/kg).
Reassess child every 1–2 hours while waiting for transfer.
If repeated vomiting or abdominal distension occurs, give fluid more slowly.
If hydration status is not improving after 3 hours, send child for IV therapy.
After 6 hours, reassess child and classify dehydration to select plan (A, B, or C).

IF CANNOT DRINK & NO NG/IV AVAILABLE:

Refer URGENTLY to hospital for IV or NG treatment.
Note: If child is not referred to hospital, observe child at least 6 hours after rehydration to ensure mother can maintain hydration using ORS by mouth.

Prevention and Control

  1. Elimination of reservoirs:
    • Prevention of dehydration (Treatment Plan A)
    • Correction of moderate and severe dehydration (Treatment Plans B and C)
  2. Sanitation:
    • Safe water supply
    • Excreta disposal
    • Food sanitation
    • Disinfection
    • Health education
    • Fly control
  3. Chemoprophylaxis:
    • Tetracycline BID × 3 days
    • Not to give: Neomycin, purgatives, opium, steroids, charcoal, atropine, etc.
  4. Vaccination:
    • For Cholera: Killed vaccine, 2 doses s/c
    • Rotavirus vaccine:
      • i. Rotarix: Live, oral, monovalent, human.
        3 doses: 1st dose at 6–12 weeks; 2nd dose after 4 weeks (10th week); 3rd dose at 14th week.
      • ii. RotaTeq: Live, oral, bovine-human, pentavalent.
        3 doses: 1st dose at 2 months; 2nd dose at 4 months; 3rd dose at 6 months.
      • Vaccination should not be initiated for infants aged >12 weeks.
  5. Appropriate feeding

Diarrheal Disease Control Program in Nepal [2015]

Integral part of Primary Health Care (PHC) program.

Objectives

  • To reduce morbidity and mortality due to diarrhea and dehydration.
  • To reduce morbidity from 3.3 episodes/child/year to minimum.

Targets

  • To reduce <5 mortality rate by 50%.
  • To reduce <5 morbidity by 20%.
  • To increase accessibility of the population to ORS.
  • To raise public awareness about ORS to 100%.
  • To increase number of providers of low osmolarity ORS.

Strategies

  • To establish functioning ORT corners.
  • To increase access through FCHV, PHC, HPs, SHPs.
  • To raise public awareness.
  • Apply IMCI package including EPI, Nutrition, ARI, Malaria control programs.
  • Promote specific preventive measures: Breastfeeding, proper weaning.

Achievements

  • Low Osmolarity ORS and Zinc tablets treatment now covers 27 and 37 districts of Nepal.
  • 100% ORS supply during 2007–2008.
  • 100% Zinc tablets purchase.
    • Dose: Daily 10 mg for <6 months; Daily 20 mg for >6 months for 10–14 days.

Water-borne Epidemics VS Milk-borne Epidemics

Feature Water-borne Epidemics Milk-borne Epidemics
Outbreak Explosive only if contamination is heavy Sudden in onset
Common source Continuous exposure epidemic Single exposure epidemic
Tracing of source May not be traced if too many cases are present Common source of milk is traceable
Isolation of disease agent May not always be isolated Relatively easy to isolate
Age involved Affects people of all ages Children and elderly are more affected
Epidemic subsides After purification of affected water supply After treatment of affected persons and animals
Limitation of epidemics Epidemics not limited to primary cases only Epidemics limited to only those who drink the particular contaminated milk
Place of distribution Distribution may be large if river water is involved Distribution is restricted to the area of distribution of contaminated milk
