Influenza, Avian Flu & Swine Flu

Fast Track Clinical Science MBBS - 3rd Year | Communicable Diseases

Influenza

Introduction

It is an acute respiratory tract infection caused by influenza virus[cite: 1].

  • Types of Influenza virus: A, B and C[cite: 1]
  • All known pandemics were caused by influenza A strains[cite: 1].
  • Characterized by: Sudden onset of Chills, Malaise, Fever, Muscular pain and Cough[cite: 1].

Problem Statement

  • It occurs in all the countries and affects millions of people every year[cite: 1].
  • It may smoulder in a community without clinical recognition, being manifest only by serological surveys[cite: 1].
  • It may occur in pandemics every 10–40 years due to major antigenic changes, as occurred in 1918 (Spanish influenza), 1957 (Asian influenza) and 1968 (Hong Kong influenza)[cite: 1].
  • The periodicity is not regular because several strains of the virus may be in simultaneous circulation, which means there may be outbreaks almost every year or more[cite: 1].
  • Once an epidemic begins, the picture is quite characteristic:
    • Preceded by a few early cases, there is a sudden outburst of the disease[cite: 1].
    • Increased hospitalization of cases and sickness-absenteeism in schools and places of work[cite: 1].

Unique features of influenza epidemic:[cite: 1]

  1. Suddenness with which they arise[cite: 1]
  2. The speed and ease with which they spread[cite: 1]
  3. The short incubation period[cite: 1]
  4. Large number of subclinical cases[cite: 1]
  5. High proportion of susceptible population[cite: 1]
  6. Short duration of immunity and absence of cross immunity[cite: 1]
  • At present three types of Influenza viruses are circulating in the world: A(H1N1), A(H3N2) and B viruses[cite: 1].
  • In 1997, influenza A(H5N1) was identified in Hong Kong[cite: 1].
  • In 2009, influenza A(H1N1) virus of swine emerged in Mexico[cite: 1].

Nepal Scenario

Influenza like illness (ILI) is reported by EWARS as the top priority disease[cite: 1]. 2,207 cases of ILI were reported in FY 2079/80 which is lower as compared to 2 previous years[cite: 1]. At provincial level, Influenza-like illness was predominantly reported in the Karnali Province (n=1,560)[cite: 1].

Status of Influenza A and Influenza B in Nepal as per DoHS 2079/80

  • Influenza H1N1 Positive: 85 cases[cite: 1]
  • Influenza A/H3 Positive: 208 cases[cite: 1]
  • Influenza B (Victoria) Positive: 89 cases[cite: 1]
  • Influenza positivity rate: 5.1%[cite: 1]

Epidemiological Determinants

1. Agent Factors

a. Agent:

  • Family: Orthomyxoviridae[cite: 1]
  • Subtypes: Type A, Type B, Type C (There is no cross immunity between them)[cite: 1].
  • Influenza A and B viruses are responsible for epidemics of disease throughout the world[cite: 1].
  • Surface antigens of Influenza A virus:[cite: 1]
    • Haemagglutinin (H) antigen: Initiation of infection[cite: 1].
    • Neuraminidase (N) antigen: Severity and transmission of infection[cite: 1].
  • Influenza virus A exhibits:[cite: 1]
    • Antigenic Shift: Sudden complete or major change[cite: 1]. Due to genetic recombination of human with animal or avian virus[cite: 1]. Causes a major epidemic or pandemic[cite: 1].
    • Antigenic Drift: Gradual change over period of time[cite: 1]. Due to 'point mutation'[cite: 1].
  • Type C virus appears antigenically stable (causes sporadic cases)[cite: 1].

b. Reservoir of infection:

  • Major reservoir of influenza virus exists in animals and birds[cite: 1].
  • There is increasing evidence that the animal reservoirs provide new strains of influenza virus by recombination between the influenza viruses of man, animals and birds[cite: 1].

c. Source of infection:

  • Usually a case or subclinical case[cite: 1].
  • The secretions of the respiratory tract are infective[cite: 1].

d. Period of infectivity:

  • Virus is present in the nasopharynx from 1–2 days before and 1–2 days after onset of symptoms[cite: 1].

2. Host Factors

  • Age and Sex: Affects all age groups and both sexes[cite: 1]. High-risk groups include old people (>65 years), children (<18 years), persons with diabetes or chronic heart disease, kidney and respiratory ailments[cite: 1].
  • Human Mobility: An important factor in the transmission of the disease[cite: 1].
  • Immunity:[cite: 1]
    • Immunity to influenza is subtype specific[cite: 1].
    • Antibodies against HA: Resistance to initiation of infection[cite: 1].
    • Antibodies against NA: Decreased severity and decreased ability to transmit virus to contacts[cite: 1].
    • Antibodies against ribonucleoprotein are type specific and are useful in typing viral isolates as in influenza A and B[cite: 1].
    • Protection correlates with both serum antibodies and secretory IgA antibodies in nasal secretions[cite: 1].
    • Serum antibodies: Persist for many months[cite: 1].
    • Secretory IgA antibodies: Only a few months, important in preventing infections[cite: 1].
    • The three types of influenza viruses are antigenically unrelated and therefore induce no cross-protection[cite: 1].

3. Environmental Factors

  • Season: Winter months in the Northern hemisphere; winter or rainy season in the Southern hemisphere[cite: 1].
  • Overcrowding: Enhances transmission[cite: 1]. The attack rates are high in closed population groups e.g. schools, institutions, ships, etc[cite: 1].

Mode of Transmission

  • By droplet infection or droplet nuclei created by sneezing or coughing[cite: 1].
  • Portal of entry: Respiratory tract[cite: 1].

Incubation Period

18 to 72 hours[cite: 1].

Pathogenesis

Virus enters the respiratory tract → Inflammation of superficial epithelium of tracheal and bronchial mucosa → Secondary bacterial invasion[cite: 1].

Clinical Features

  • Fever (lasts 1–5 days)[cite: 1]
  • Chills[cite: 1]
  • Aches and pains[cite: 1]
  • Coughing[cite: 1]
  • Generalized weakness[cite: 1]

Complications

  • Acute sinusitis[cite: 1]
  • Otitis media[cite: 1]
  • Purulent bronchitis[cite: 1]
  • Pneumonia (most dreadful)[cite: 1]
  • Reye Syndrome i.e., hepatic failure and encephalopathy (in children, caused by influenza B)[cite: 1]

Lab Diagnosis

a. Virus isolation:

  • Sample: Nasopharyngeal secretions[cite: 1].
  • Method: Indirect fluorescent antibody technique[cite: 1].

b. Serology:

  • Sample: Acute and convalescent sera[cite: 1].
  • Routine tests: Haemagglutination inhibition (HI) and ELISA[cite: 1].
  • Four-fold or more increase in titer must occur to indicate influenza infection[cite: 1].
  • Human sera often contain non-specific mucoprotein inhibitors that must be destroyed before testing by HI[cite: 1].

Prevention of Influenza

  1. Good ventilation of public buildings[cite: 1]
  2. Avoidance of crowded places during epidemics[cite: 1]
  3. Encouraging sufferers to cover their mouth and nose with a handkerchief when coughing and sneezing[cite: 1]
  4. To stay at home at the first sign of influenza[cite: 1]
  5. Immunization:[cite: 1]
    • In theory, it offers the best prospect of controlling influenza at present times[cite: 1].
    • Due to antigenic variation of virus, new vaccines are constantly required[cite: 1].
    • To be effective, the vaccine must be administered at least two weeks before the onset of epidemic or preferably 2–3 months before influenza is expected[cite: 1].
    • Since the epidemic is unpredictable, immunization prevents least[cite: 1].
    • Recommended only in certain selected population groups e.g., in industry to reduce absenteeism, in public servants like police, fire protection, transport and medical care[cite: 1].

Influenza Vaccine

a. Killed vaccine:

  • The recommended vaccine strain is grown in the allantoic cavity of developing chick embryos, harvested, purified, and killed by formalin or beta-propiolactone[cite: 1].
  • One dose of vaccine contains approximately 15 μg of HA[cite: 1].
  • Route: Subcutaneous or intramuscular[cite: 1].
  • Immunity lasts for 6–12 months[cite: 1].
  • Single inoculation (0.5 ml for adults and children above 3 years, 0.25 ml for children from 6 to 36 months)[cite: 1].
  • Side effects: Fever, local inflammation at site of inoculation and very rarely Guillain-Barré syndrome[cite: 1].

b. Live attenuated vaccine:

  • A trivalent, live-attenuated influenza vaccine administered as a single dose intranasal spray[cite: 1].
  • Indicated for healthy individuals of 2 to 49 years[cite: 1].
  • It should not be used in the household members of immunocompromised individuals[cite: 1].

Antiviral Drugs

Used for the prophylaxis and therapy of influenza A and B:[cite: 1]

  • Zanamivir: By inhaler (10 mg). Twice daily for therapy and once daily for prophylaxis[cite: 1].
  • Oseltamivir: For prophylaxis: 75 mg per day. For therapy: 75 mg twice a day[cite: 1].

Avian Influenza

Past Questions: Write short note on avian influenza. [2064][cite: 1]

Introduction

  • It refers to infection caused by avian/bird influenza virus (influenza virus A)[cite: 1].
  • "Birds" means almost any type of domestic or wild birds, but most bird flu infections occur in bird populations that permit easy bird to bird transmission (birds that flock together or domestic chicken farms)[cite: 1].
  • The strain H5N1 infected humans in 1997[cite: 1].
  • While its most highly pathogenic strain H5N1 had been spreading throughout Asia since 2003, avian influenza reached Europe in 2005, and the Middle East, as well as Africa, the following year[cite: 1].
  • Recent outbreak was in China in the year 2013[cite: 1].

Epidemiological Determinants

1. Agent Factors

  • Agent: There are many subtypes of avian influenza viruses, but only some strains have been highly pathogenic in humans[cite: 1]. These are types H5N1, H7N3, H7N7, H7N9, and H9N2[cite: 1].
  • Reservoir of infection: Migratory wild birds are a natural reservoir of avian influenza viruses[cite: 1]. Domestic poultry can be infected by wild aquatic birds, including migratory birds which fly long distances[cite: 1].

2. Host Factors

  • Age: Infants and children (<2 years) and older people (>65 years)[cite: 1].
  • Sex: Both sexes[cite: 1].
  • Persons with chronic diseases[cite: 1].
  • The greatest risks are for people who encounter infected birds, their droppings, and surfaces that have been contaminated by sick or infected birds or their feathers, saliva, or dead birds[cite: 1].

3. Environmental Factors

  • Winter months[cite: 1]
  • Overcrowding[cite: 1]

Mode of Transmission

  • Portal of entry: Respiratory tract[cite: 1].
  • Farm to Farm: Bird droppings, contaminated dust, soils, equipment, vehicles, feed, cages or clothing[cite: 1].
  • Country to Country: Bird trade or migration of migratory birds infected with the virus[cite: 1].

Incubation Period

2 to 3 days[cite: 1].

Clinical Features

  • Fatigue[cite: 1]
  • Fever (>38°C)[cite: 1]
  • Conjunctivitis[cite: 1]
  • Sore throat[cite: 1]
  • Cough[cite: 1]
  • Muscle aches[cite: 1]
  • Diarrhea[cite: 1]
  • Vomiting[cite: 1]
  • Abdominal pain[cite: 1]
  • Chest pain[cite: 1]
  • Bleeding from nose and gums[cite: 1]
  • When untreated: Rapid deterioration, viral pneumonia leading to respiratory distress, kidney failure, multi-organ failure, and death[cite: 1].

Treatment

  • Antiviral drugs, notably Oseltamivir (75 mg BD for 5 days), can reduce the duration of viral replication and improve prospects of survival[cite: 1].
  • In suspected cases, Oseltamivir should be prescribed as soon as possible i.e., within 48 hours[cite: 1].

Pandemic Influenza (Swine Flu)

Introduction

  • It is an infection caused by any one of several types of swine influenza viruses[cite: 1].
  • In March 2009, it emerged and spread rapidly throughout the world[cite: 1].
  • WHO declared influenza pandemic on 11th June 2009[cite: 1].
  • In March 2015, it reappeared as Indian swine flu[cite: 1].
  • Based on knowledge about past epidemics, it is expected to continue to circulate as a seasonal virus for some years to come[cite: 1].
  • Swine influenza differs from seasonal influenza because:[cite: 1]
    1. Majority of the population has little or no immunity to the virus, so the impact of infection is wide[cite: 1].
    2. Virus infects the lower respiratory tract and can cause rapidly progressive pneumonia[cite: 1].

Epidemiological Determinants

1. Agent Factors

  • Agent: Influenza A virus (H1N1, H1N2, H2N3, H3N2, H3N1)[cite: 1]. Influenza C virus is rare[cite: 1].
  • Reservoir of infection: Major reservoir exists in infected pigs[cite: 1].

2. Host Factors

  • Age: Infants and children (<2 years) and older people (>65 years)[cite: 1].
  • Sex: Pregnant women[cite: 1].
  • Patients with COPD, Asthma[cite: 1].
  • Children receiving chronic aspirin therapy[cite: 1].
  • Chronic cardiac disease, metabolic disorders, chronic renal/hepatic/neurological/hemoglobinopathies/immunosuppression, and morbid obesity[cite: 1].

3. Environmental Factors

  • Winter months[cite: 1]
  • Overcrowding[cite: 1]

Mode of Transmission

  • Portal of entry: Respiratory tract[cite: 1].
  • People who work with swine, especially those with intense exposures, are at increased risk[cite: 1].

Incubation Period

2 to 3 days[cite: 1].

Clinical Features

a. Uncomplicated influenza:

  • Influenza-like illness: Fever, cough, sore throat, rhinorrhea, headache, muscle pain, malaise[cite: 1].
  • Gastrointestinal illness: Diarrhea, vomiting[cite: 1].

b. Complicated influenza:

  • Respiratory tract disease: Shortness of breath / dyspnea, radiological signs of lower respiratory tract infection[cite: 1].
  • Central nervous system: Encephalopathy, encephalitis[cite: 1].
  • Severe dehydration[cite: 1].
  • Exacerbation of underlying chronic disease[cite: 1].

Lab Diagnosis

  • Reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR): Most timely and sensitive[cite: 1]. Sample: Nasopharyngeal + throat swab (Tracheal/bronchial aspiration in lower respiratory tract infection cases)[cite: 1].
  • Point of care diagnostic test[cite: 1].

Infection Control

  1. Appropriate infection control measures: Strict adherence to hand hygiene with soap and water or an alcohol-based sanitizer; cover mouth and nose with tissue or handkerchief when coughing and sneezing; patient must wear mask when going to the community[cite: 1].
  2. Whenever performing high-risk aerosol-generating procedures, use a particulate respirator, eye protection, gown, and carry out all procedures in an adequately ventilated room[cite: 1].
  3. The duration of isolation precautions for hospitalized patients with influenza symptoms should be continued for 7 days after onset of illness or 24 hours after the resolution of fever and respiratory symptoms, whichever is longer[cite: 1].

Vaccination

  • Vaccine does not give 100% protection but reduces the risk of disease[cite: 1].
  • Vaccine becomes effective only after 14 days of vaccination[cite: 1].
  • WHO priority groups for vaccination:[cite: 1]
    1. Pregnant women[cite: 1]
    2. Children (>6 months) with one of several chronic diseases[cite: 1]
    3. Healthy young adults (15–49 years)[cite: 1]
    4. Healthy children[cite: 1]
    5. Healthy adults (49–65 years)[cite: 1]
    6. Healthy adults (>65 years)[cite: 1]

Types of Swine Flu Vaccine

a. Inactivated vaccine:

  • Monovalent vaccine containing antigen equivalent to A/California/7/2009 (H1N1) V-like strain[cite: 1].
  • 15 μg of haemagglutinin per 0.5 ml dose[cite: 1].
  • Vaccine preservative: Thiomersal[cite: 1].
  • Storage temperature: 2–8°C[cite: 1].
  • Route: Intramuscular[cite: 1].
  • Side effects: Soreness, redness and swelling at injection site, fever, muscle or joint ache, or headache[cite: 1].
  • Contraindications:[cite: 1]
    • People with history of anaphylactic reaction to any substance[cite: 1].
    • People with history of a severe reaction to influenza vaccination[cite: 1].
    • People who developed Guillain-Barré syndrome[cite: 1].
    • Children below 6 months of age[cite: 1].

b. Live attenuated vaccine:

  • Route: Nasal spray[cite: 1].
  • Side effects: Runny nose, nasal congestion, cough, sore throat, low grade fever, muscle ache, headache[cite: 1].

Treatment of Influenza

  1. Basic symptomatic care[cite: 1]
  2. Antiviral therapy:[cite: 1]
    • Antiviral treatment should be initiated immediately and without waiting for laboratory confirmation of diagnosis[cite: 1].
    • Severe/progressive clinical illness: Oseltamivir (if not available or resistance, use Zanamivir)[cite: 1].
    • High risk of severe/complicated illness: Oseltamivir OR Zanamivir[cite: 1].
    • Not high risk OR Uncomplicated confirmed/suspected illness: No need for treatment[cite: 1].
    • Dosage:[cite: 1]
      • Oseltamivir: 75 mg BD for 5 days[cite: 1]
      • Zanamivir: 2 Inhalations (2 × 5 mg) BD for 5 days[cite: 1]
  3. Supplemental oxygen therapy if necessary[cite: 1].
  4. Fluid replacement, antimicrobial therapy, and supportive care[cite: 1].
