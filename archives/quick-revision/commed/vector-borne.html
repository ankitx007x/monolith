VECTOR-BORNE DISEASES

MIN CRAM | Fast Track Clinical Science MBBS - 3rd Year | Revision Page

1. VECTOR

Definition

Vector: An arthropod capable of transmitting or spreading diseases.

Types according to mode of transmission

1. Mechanical vector
Arthropod that passively, directly transmits pathogens without biting.
Example: Housefly picks up pathogens from filthy substances deposits them on eatables contaminates food.

2. Biological vector

  • Arthropod transmits pathogen indirectly by biting a reservoir and sucking pathogen-containing blood.
  • Pathogen undergoes biological development inside vector for a specified period, after which vector can transmit disease.

Examples: Mosquito, rat flea, cyclops.

Biological vector types - VERY HIGH YIELD

Type What happens inside vector Example
Propagative Multiplication only Plague bacilli in rat flea; yellow fever virus in Aedes
Cyclopropagative Multiplication + cyclic development Malarial parasite in female Anopheles
Cyclodevelopmental Development only, no multiplication Wuchereria bancrofti microfilariae in female Culex; Guinea-worm embryo in Cyclops

Arthropods can also transmit disease by:

  • Invading tissues/skin: itch mite scabies
  • Inoculating poisonous substances: scorpion sting, spider bite

DENGUE

Definition

Acute mosquito-borne tropical disease caused by dengue virus.
Also called break-bone fever.

4. DENGUE - PROBLEM STATEMENT

  • Worldwide
    • Dengue virus has 4 serotypes: DEN-1, DEN-2, DEN-3, DEN-4.
    • Genus: Flavivirus
    • Family: Flaviviridae
    • Each serotype has distinct genotypes extensive genetic variability.
    • Asian genotypes of DEN-2 and DEN-3 are frequently associated with severe disease, especially with secondary infection.
    • Estimated 390 million infections/year → 95% credible interval: 284-528 million
    • Of these, around 96 million manifest clinically → 67-136 million.
    • 3.9 billion people are estimated to be at risk.
    • Risk exists in 129 countries.
    • 70% of actual burden is in Asia.
    • Reported cases appeared to decline in 2020-21, but:
      • data incomplete
      • COVID-19 probably reduced reporting.
    • Increase over previous two decades partly reflects improved national recording/reporting.
    • But also reflects increasing government recognition of dengue burden.

5. EPIDEMIOLOGY - DENGUE

  • Agent
    • Dengue virus = arbovirus / flavivirus
    • 40-50 nm
    • Spherical
    • Enveloped
    • SSRNA
    • 4 serotypes: DEN-1, 2, 3, 4
    • All serotypes produce the same illness.
    • Multiple infections with different serotypes → severe dengue → DHF/DSS.
  • Host
    • Age: all ages; children more affected, but usually milder than adults.
    • Sex: females more prone.
    • Unlike many infections more common in well-nourished children.
  • Environment
    • Climate: hot tropical climate particularly Terai.
    • Vectors:
      • Aedes aegypti principal vector; breeds in standing water
      • Aedes albopictus important vector, especially Southeast Asian setting
    • Setting: clustering in urban + semiurban areas.

6. TRANSMISSION

Vector transmission
Infected Aedes mosquito bites human.

Infective period of human → mosquito
Mosquito becomes infected by feeding on patient from:
1 day before onset 5th day of illness

In mosquito
Extrinsic incubation period = 8-10 days
Once infective remains infective for life
Transovarian transmission occurs.

Cycle
understand rather than memorize blindly

Infected mosquito bites human
↓
Virus enters WBCs + lymphatic tissues
↓
Virus reproduces inside cells and disseminates
↓
Virus released into blood chemical/biological mediators
↓
Plasma leakage + bleeding
↓
Second mosquito feeds on infected blood
↓
Virus replicates in mosquito midgut + other organs
↓
Infects salivary glands
↓
Mosquito transmits virus to another human.

Important
Virus replicates in mosquito salivary glands.
Sequential infection with two different serotypes can produce severe dengue, probably because of cross-reactive enhancing antibodies.
Major pathophysiological changes determining severity:

  1. Plasma leakage
  2. Bleeding manifestations

Incubation period in humans: 2-7 days

7. CLINICAL SPECTRUM

Dengue may be:
Asymptomatic OR Symptomatic

  1. Undifferentiated fever
  2. Dengue fever
    • without haemorrhage
    • with haemorrhage
  3. Dengue haemorrhagic fever
    • without shock
    • with shock → DSS
  4. Expanded dengue syndrome / isolated organopathy

8. CLINICAL FEATURES

1. Undifferentiated fever
Simple fever indistinguishable from other viral infections.

2. Classical dengue fever

  • Sudden onset high-grade fever
  • Chills + rigor
  • Bleeding
  • Thrombocytopenia
  • Plasma leakage
  • Intense headache
  • Muscle pain
  • Joint pain
  • Retro-orbital pain
  • Photophobia
  • Extreme weakness
  • Anorexia
  • Constipation
  • Colicky abdominal pain
  • Rash:
    • maculopapular OR
    • scarlatiniform
  • Biphasic fever curve

9. DHF

Basic concept
Infection with >1 dengue virus/serotype.
First infection sensitizes patient.
Second infection may cause an immunological catastrophe.

Clinical

  • Abrupt high-grade fever
  • Facial flushing
  • Headache
  • Haemorrhagic manifestations

Any of:

  • Petechiae
  • Purpura
  • Ecchymosis
  • Epistaxis
  • Gum bleeding
  • Haematemesis
  • Melena

Positive tourniquet test = most common haemorrhagic manifestation
Hepatomegaly
Shock absent in DHF before progression to DSS.

10. DENGUE SHOCK SYNDROME

DHF + shock
Shock features:

  • Rapid, weak pulse
  • Dangerously low BP
  • Cold, clammy skin
  • Restlessness

11. CLINICAL COURSE — 3 PHASES

1. FEBRILE - 2-7 days

  • High fever, potentially >40°C/104°F
  • Sudden fever
  • Generalized pain
  • Mouth/nose bleeding
  • Headache
  • Muscle/joint pain
  • Vomiting
  • Rash
  • Diarrhoea

2. CRITICAL — 1-2 days
Occurs as fever resolves.
Key event = plasma leakage.
→ Pleural effusion
→ Ascites
→ Reduced circulating volume
→ Reduced organ perfusion

Potential problems:

  • Shock
  • Dehydration
  • Bleeding
  • Fluid overload
  • Organ impairment

Symptoms:

  • Hypotension
  • Pleural effusion
  • Ascites
  • GI bleeding

3. RECOVERY - 2-3 days
Leaked fluid is reabsorbed into bloodstream.
Problems:

  • Reabsorption
  • Fluid overload

Symptoms:

  • Altered consciousness
  • Seizures
  • Itching
  • Slow heart rate
MEMORY
F C R
Fever → Critical leakage → Recovery/reabsorption

14. GROUP A - CAN GO HOME

Criteria

  • Uncomplicated disease
  • Can tolerate adequate oral fluids
  • No warning signs

Treatment

  • ORS
  • Fruit juice
  • Electrolyte + sugar-containing fluids
  • Paracetamol
  • NO aspirin, ibuprofen or other NSAIDs → aggravate gastritis/bleeding.
WARNING SIGNS
Think: ABDOMEN + BLEEDING + BRAIN + SHOCK + BREATHING
  • Severe abdominal pain / persistent vomiting
  • Red spots/patches
  • Nose/gum bleeding
  • Haematemesis
  • Black tarry stools
  • Drowsiness/irritability
  • Pale, cold, clammy skin
  • Difficulty breathing

15. GROUP B

Need admission/close observation; may have warning signs.

A. Dengue WITH warning signs
Fluid algorithm

First obtain reference haematocrit BEFORE fluids
↓
0.9% saline / isotonic crystalloid → 5-7 mL/kg/hr for 1-2 hr
↓
Reduce to 3-5 mL/kg/hr for 2-4 hr
↓
Then 2-3 mL/kg/hr or less, according to clinical response.

Then interpret Hct:

  • Hct same → continue 2-3 mL/kg/hr
  • Hct rapidly rises → increase fluids to 5-10 mL/kg/hr for 1-2 hr
  • Hct falls → gradually reduce IV fluids → transfuse fresh whole blood

B. Dengue WITHOUT warning signs

  • Encourage oral fluids.
  • If unable to tolerate: → 0.9% saline ± dextrose at maintenance rate.
  • After several hours IV fluids may be able to resume oral fluids.

16. GROUP C — SEVERE DENGUE

These patients:

  • Require emergency treatment + urgent referral.
  • Are in critical phase with:
    • severe plasma leakage dengue shock and/or fluid accumulation + respiratory distress
    • severe haemorrhage
    • severe organ impairment:
      • hepatic
      • renal
      • cardiomyopathy
      • encephalopathy
      • encephalitis
  • ICU + access to blood transfusion

IV fluids

  • Essential for restoring circulation/consciousness/life.
  • Rapid replacement of plasma loss with isotonic crystalloid.
  • In hypotensive shock colloid (dextran).
  • Continue replacement of plasma losses to maintain circulation for 24-48 hr.

Blood
Blood transfusion only for severe bleeding.

Principle
Large fluid volumes for limited periods + close monitoring avoid pulmonary oedema.

Goals of fluid resuscitation

  • Circulation
    • Improved central/peripheral circulation
    • Reduced tachycardia
    • Improving BP
  • Organ perfusion
    • Stable/improved consciousness
    • Urine output ≥0.5 mL/kg/hr
    • Improvement of metabolic acidosis

17. DENGUE SHOCK — MONITORING

Frequent monitoring until danger period is over.
Vital signs + peripheral perfusion + urine output:

  • every 15-30 min until out of shock

Monitor Hct before/after fluid boluses until stable.

Hct interpretation

  • High Hct → active plasma leakage → more fluid needed
  • Falling Hct → major haemorrhage → urgent blood transfusion

18. LABORATORY DIAGNOSIS

Diagnosis can be established by ≥1:

  1. Fourfold or greater change in reciprocal IgG/IgM titres to dengue antigens in paired serum samples.
  2. Dengue antigen demonstration:
    • autopsy tissue
    • immunohistochemistry/immunofluorescence
    • serum
    • enzyme immunoassay:
      • MAC-ELISA
      • IgG ELISA
      • NS1-ELISA
      • EIA
  3. Viral genomic sequences by RT-PCR in:
    • autopsy tissue
    • serum
    • CSF
  4. Less commonly virus isolation from:
    • serum
    • plasma
    • leukocytes
    • autopsy samples

Other laboratory tests

  • CBC
  • Metabolic panel
  • Serum protein + albumin
  • Liver panel
  • Coagulation profile
  • DIC panel

21. CROSS-BORDER COLLABORATION 2073 PAST QUESTION

Why important?

  1. Free movement of people across border makes unilateral control difficult.
  2. Outbreak in one country can rapidly spread to the other.
  3. Control requires collaborative efforts.
  4. Sharing dengue information allows timely sensing of the situation → better preparedness.
  5. Collaboration can provide:
    • laboratory facilities
    • technical support
    • financial support
Exam conclusion
Dengue cannot be effectively controlled by Nepal alone because of porous/free cross-border movement with India; surveillance, information sharing and technical/laboratory/financial collaboration are essential.

22. DENGUE PREVENTION

Vaccine
Dengvaxia

  • First dengue vaccine developed.
  • Live attenuated vaccine: CYD-TDV
  • Efficacious/safe in people with previous dengue infection (seropositive).
  • If vaccination is considered pre-vaccination screening recommended.
  • WHO: introduce vaccine only where epidemiological data indicate high disease burden.
  • Other tetravalent live attenuated vaccines were under development in Phase III in the original notes.

23. PREVENTION = ATTACK THE VECTOR

Main method currently = combat mosquito vectors.

Prevent breeding

  • Prevent mosquito access to egg-laying habitats through environmental management/modification.
  • Proper solid waste disposal.
  • Remove artificial water-holding habitats.
  • Cover, empty and clean domestic water-storage containers WEEKLY.
  • Appropriate insecticides in outdoor water-storage containers.

Personal protection
Use:

  • Window screens
  • Repellents
  • Insecticide-treated materials
  • Coils
  • Vaporizers

Must be used during daytime, both indoors and outdoors because Aedes bites throughout the day.
Wear clothing minimizing exposed skin.

Community engagement

  • Educate community regarding mosquito-borne disease risks.
  • Community participation + mobilization for sustained vector control.

Active surveillance

  • Monitor:
    • vector abundance
    • species composition
  • Determine effectiveness of interventions.
  • Prospectively monitor virus prevalence in mosquitoes through sentinel mosquito collections.
  • Combine vector surveillance with:
    • clinical surveillance
    • environmental surveillance.

IVM = WHO-recommended approach for sustainable, effective, locally adapted vector control.

24. DENGUE VECTOR CONTROL - 3 METHODS

1. ENVIRONMENTAL MANAGEMENT
Mainstay of dengue vector control.
Goal:
destroy / alter / remove / recycle non-essential containers providing egg/larval/pupal habitats.

Three types

A. Environmental modification

  • Long-lasting physical changes reducing larval habitats.
  • Example: reliable piped water supply, including household connections.

B. Environmental manipulation
Temporary changes involving management of essential containers:

  • Frequently empty + scrub water vessels
  • Clean flower vases
  • Clean desert room coolers
  • Clean gutters
  • Shelter stored tyres from rain
  • Recycle/properly dispose discarded containers + tyres
  • Manage plants near houses that collect water in leaf axils

C. Changes in human habitation/behaviour
Reduce human-vector contact:

  • mosquito screens on windows/doors/entry points
  • mosquito nets while sleeping during daytime

25. OTHER ENVIRONMENTAL MEASURES

Water supply/storage

  • Improve water supply = fundamental for Ae. aegypti control.
  • Piped household water preferred to:
    • wells
    • communal standpipes
    • rooftop catchments/rainwater harvesting
    • other storage systems.
  • Piped supply should be accompanied by communication discouraging traditional storage.

Mosquito-proof containers

  • Tight-fitting lids.
  • Rain-filled containers → tightly fitted mesh screens.
  • Replace removable covers after each water removal.
  • Maintain covers so mosquitoes cannot enter/exit.
  • Expanded polystyrene beads can prevent egg laying on water surface.
  • Applicable only where container has bottom-installed pipe for drawing water.

Solid waste

  • Target discarded/non-essential containers identified as important mosquito-producing containers.
  • Collect waste in plastic sacks.
  • Dispose regularly.
  • Recommended collection frequency for housefly/rodent control in warm climates = twice/week.
  • Integrate Ae. aegypti control with waste-management services.

Street cleansing
Regular system should:

  • remove discarded water-holding containers
  • clean drains
  • prevent stagnant water
  • reduce Ae. aegypti breeding
  • remove breeding sites for other urban pests.

Building structures
Planning/construction + legislation/regulation can reduce larval habitats of:

  • Ae. aegypti
  • Culex quinquefasciatus
  • An. stephensi

26. CHEMICAL CONTROL

Larviciding

  • Widely used against Ae. aegypti larvae.
  • Complementary to environmental management.
  • Except emergencies → restrict to containers that cannot otherwise be eliminated/managed.

Larvicides should:

  • have low toxicity to other species
  • not significantly alter water:
    • taste
    • odour
    • colour.

Adulticides
Target adult vectors to affect:

  • mosquito density
  • longevity
  • transmission parameters.

Applied as:

  • residual surface treatments
  • space treatments.

Space spraying
Use only in emergencies:

  • ongoing epidemic
  • prevent an incipient epidemic

Objective: → rapid, massive destruction of adult mosquitoes.
It may temporarily reduce transmission, but long-term epidemiological significance is uncertain.
If started early + sufficiently large scale, it can reduce transmission enough to allow longer-term measures such as:

  • larviciding
  • community source reduction

Thus, when surveillance detects epidemic early and resources permit: space spraying + intensified source reduction + larviciding can be initiated together.

MALARIA

1. Definition

Protozoal disease caused by Plasmodium
Transmitted by bite of infected female Anopheles mosquito

Species

  • P. vivax
  • P. falciparum
  • P. malariae
  • P. ovale
  • P. knowlesi

2. IMPORTANT DEFINITIONS

Hypnozoite

  • Dormant liver stage
  • Seen in P. vivax + P. ovale
  • Responsible for relapse

Relapse
Reappearance due to persistent liver hypnozoites → P. vivax, P. ovale

Recrudescence

  • Reappearance due to surviving blood-stage parasites
  • Due to incomplete clearance of parasitaemia

Control
Reduction of malaria burden until it is no longer a public health problem

Elimination ☆

  • Zero local mosquito-borne transmission in a defined geographical area
  • Continued measures required to prevent re-establishment

Eradication
Permanent reduction to zero worldwide incidence

WHO elimination certification
Transmission interrupted for ≥3 consecutive years with adequate evidence

7. HOST FACTORS

  • Age: all ages affected
  • Newborn → relative resistance to falciparum due to fetal Hb
  • Sex: males may have greater exposure
  • Sickle-cell trait → protection against severe falciparum
  • Duffy negative → resistance to vivax
  • Pregnancy ↑ risk
    • Primigravida particularly vulnerable
    • IUFD
    • Prematurity
    • Abortion
  • Immunity develops after repeated exposure
  • Migrants/travellers from non-endemic areas susceptible
  • Migration → imported malaria

LYMPHATIC FILARIASIS

Definition

  • Lymphatic filariasis (LF) is a disease caused by lymphatic-dwelling, thread-like nematode parasites.
  • Causative parasites:
    • Wuchereria bancrofti
    • Brugia malayi
    • Brugia timori
  • Wuchereria bancrofti is the most widespread species.
  • LF is also called Wuchereriasis / Bancroftian filariasis.
  • It is generally not fatal but causes considerable suffering, deformity and disability.

PROBLEM STATEMENT

Global

  • Affects about 120 million people
  • Present in >55 countries
  • About 1.1 billion people live in areas at risk of infection.
  • In South-East Asia Region:
    • About 600 million live in endemic areas
    • About 60 million are infected.

Nepal

  • Nepal is one of the countries endemic for LF.
  • 64 districts have been identified as endemic.
  • The vector was initially thought to occur up to 5,800 ft, but a 2070/71 study showed vector presence up to 6,890 ft.
  • The initial population at risk was approximately 25 million.

Updated morbidity figures
DoHS 2081/82

  • Total LF morbidity cases: 47,866
  • Hydrocele: 32,659
  • Lymphoedema: 14,776

These replace the older 2079/80 figures in the original notes.

EPIDEMIOLOGICAL DETERMINANTS

1. Agent

  • Wuchereria bancrofti
  • Brugia malayi
  • Brugia timori

Nepal: Wuchereria bancrofti is the recorded parasite.

2. Vector
Female mosquitoes are the vectors.

  • Culex quinquefasciatus
    • Main vector in Nepal
    • Recorded in endemic areas of Nepal
    • Breeds mainly in organically contaminated water
    • Breeding places:
      • Sewage
      • Sullage water
      • Septic tanks
      • Soakage pits
      • Drains
      • Pit latrines
    • Active mainly at night
    • Transmits Bancroftian filariasis
  • Mansonia
    • Breeds in water containing aquatic plants:
      • Pistia stratiotes
      • Eichhornia
      • Salvinia
    • Requires aquatic vegetation for breeding.
    • Transmits Brugian filariasis.

3. Environmental factors

  • Temperature
  • Relative humidity
  • Rainfall
  • Poor sanitation
  • Improper sewage drainage
  • Stagnant/contaminated water

Relative humidity of around 70% favours mosquito survival.

MODE OF TRANSMISSION

  • Transmitted by the bite of an infected female mosquito.
  • Infective larvae are deposited near the site of the mosquito bite.
  • Larvae penetrate the skin and enter the lymphatic system.

Infected mosquito → infective larvae → skin → lymphatics

INCUBATION PERIOD

8-16 months
Average: 1 year.

CLINICAL FEATURES

1. Lymphatic filariasis

A. Asymptomatic amicrofilaremia
No clinical symptoms.
Microfilariae are not demonstrable in blood.

B. Asymptomatic microfilaremia
No recognizable symptoms.
Night blood is positive for microfilariae.
These individuals act as carriers and sources of infection.

C. Acute filariasis
Recurrent attacks of:

  • Fever
  • Lymphangitis
  • Lymphadenitis

Mainly due to infection/inflammation of lymphatics by adult worms.

D. Chronic filariasis
Usually takes 10-15 years to develop.
Fibrosis and obstruction of lymphatics occur.
Produces permanent damage such as:

  • Lymphoedema
  • Elephantiasis of legs
  • Genital elephantiasis
  • Breast involvement
  • Hydrocele
  • Lymph varix
  • Chyluria

Chronic cases are generally not infectious to others.

OCCULT/CRYPTIC FILARIASIS

Results mainly from a hypersensitivity reaction to microfilariae.
Classically produces tropical pulmonary eosinophilia.

Features

  • Paroxysmal nocturnal cough
  • Breathlessness
  • Wheezing
  • Diffuse pulmonary infiltrates
  • Lymphadenopathy
  • Hepatosplenomegaly
  • Markedly increased eosinophil count
  • Microfilariae are usually absent from peripheral blood

Treatment
Diethylcarbamazine (DEC)

Complication if untreated
Interstitial pulmonary fibrosis → COPD → respiratory failure.

FILARIA SURVEY

Types

  • Routine survey: 5-7% of population
  • Evaluation survey: 20% of population

Components

  1. Blood survey
  2. Clinical survey
  3. Entomological survey
  4. Xenodiagnosis
  5. Skin and serological tests

FILARIAL INDICES

Three groups:

A. Clinical indices

  • Incidence of acute:
    • Lymphangitis
    • Lymphadenitis
    • Epididymo-orchitis
  • Prevalence of chronic:
    • Lymphoedema
    • Elephantiasis
    • Hydrocele
    • Chyluria

B. Parasitological indices

  • Microfilaria rate: Percentage of sampled population with blood smear positive for microfilariae.
  • Microfilarial density: Number of microfilariae per unit volume of blood.
  • Filarial disease rate: Percentage of population showing clinical manifestations.
  • Filarial endemicity rate: Percentage showing microfilariae or clinical manifestations or both.
  • Average infestation rate: Average number of microfilariae per positive slide.

CONTROL OF FILARIASIS

Two major approaches:

  1. Chemotherapy
  2. Vector control

Chemotherapy alone is insufficient to completely prevent transmission; it must be supported by appropriate vector-control measures.

CHEMOTHERAPY

Diethylcarbamazine - DEC

  • Safe and effective drug.
  • Classical treatment for Bancroftian filariasis: 6 mg/kg/day orally for 12 days
  • Mainly effective against microfilariae.

Adverse effects:

  • Due to drug itself:
    • Headache
    • Nausea
    • Vomiting
    • Dizziness
  • Due to destruction of parasites:
    • Fever
    • Local inflammation
    • Orchitis
    • Lymphadenitis
    • Transient lymphoedema
    • Hydrocele

COMMUNITY CHEMOTHERAPY

1. Mass therapy
Drug is given to the eligible population irrespective of whether they have microfilaraemia or symptoms.
Traditionally excludes:

  • Children <2 years
  • Pregnant women
  • Seriously ill patients

2. Selective treatment
DEC is given only to microfilaria-positive individuals.

3. DEC-medicated salt

  • Salt containing low-dose DEC is given for prolonged periods.
  • Used historically as a community-control strategy.

IVERMECTIN

  • Broad-spectrum antiparasitic drug.
  • Dose: 150-200 μg/kg
  • Acts mainly against microfilariae.
  • In filaraemic individuals, inflammatory reactions may occur due to dying microfilariae.

Triple-drug regimen IDA
Ivermectin + DEC + Albendazole
The IDA regimen is now an important part of Nepal's LF elimination strategy.

VECTOR CONTROL

A. Anti-larval measures

Environmental control:

  • Elimination of breeding places
  • Adequate sanitation
  • Underground wastewater disposal
  • Filling ditches and cesspools
  • Drainage of stagnant water
  • Proper maintenance of septic tanks and soakage pits

Chemical control:

  • Larvicidal oil
  • Pyrethrum preparations
  • Temephos
  • Fenthion

Mansonia control: Removal of aquatic vegetation such as Pistia.

B. Anti-adult measures
Historical insecticides include:

  • DDT
  • HCH
  • Dieldrin

Resistance has developed to these insecticides.

C. Personal protection

  • Avoid mosquito bites
  • Use mosquito nets
  • Screening of houses

D. Integrated vector control
A combination of vector-control methods is required for sustained control.

Goal

Objectives

  2. To reduce and prevent morbidity.

CURRENT PROGRAM STRATEGIES

1. Interruption of transmission through Mass Drug Administration (MDA)
Initially, two-drug regimens, Diethylcarbamazine (DEC) and Albendazole (ALB), known as DA MDA, were administered yearly for six years. Now, in newly endemic and Transmission Assessment Survey failure districts, a three-drug regimen-Ivermectin, DEC, and Albendazole, known as IDA MDA-is used annually for 2 years. Given that humans are the sole reservoir of this filaria, MDA stands out as the most effective strategy for elimination.

2. Morbidity Management and Disability Prevention (MMDP)
Morbidity management involves essential package of care (EPC) or self-care services supported by intensive yet simple, effective, and locally tailored hygiene techniques. This recommended essential package of care includes:

  • Treatment for episodes of adenolymphangitis (ADL) or acute attack
  • Management of lymphoedema to prevent both progression of disease and episodes of ADL (hygiene, skin and wound care, elevation, exercise, and suitable footwear).
  • Surgery for hydrocoele (in W. bancrofti endemic areas) MDA or individual treatment to destroy any remaining adult parasites and microfilaria.

LEISHMANIASIS

Community Medicine MBBS 3rd Year Notes

Definition

  • Leishmaniasis is a group of protozoal diseases caused by parasites of the genus Leishmania.
  • It is transmitted to humans mainly by the bite of an infected female phlebotomine sandfly.
  • Major clinical forms:
    • Visceral leishmaniasis (VL) / Kala-azar
    • Cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL)
    • Mucocutaneous leishmaniasis (MCL)
    • Anthroponotic cutaneous leishmaniasis (ACL)
    • Zoonotic cutaneous leishmaniasis (ZCL)
    • Post-kala-azar dermal leishmaniasis (PKDL)
  • Most forms are zoonotic and involve animal reservoirs such as rodents and dogs.
  • Indian kala-azar is considered non-zoonotic, with humans as the main reservoir.

CLASSIFICATION

Type Main causative species
Visceral leishmaniasis / Kala-azar Leishmania donovani
Cutaneous leishmaniasis Leishmania tropica
Mucocutaneous leishmaniasis Leishmania braziliensis
PKDL Leishmania donovani

Forms in the parasite life cycle

  • Amastigote → found in vertebrate host/macrophages
  • Promastigote found in sandfly

PROBLEM STATEMENT

Global

  • More than 90% of VL occurs in six countries:
    • India
    • Bangladesh
    • Brazil
    • Nepal
    • Ethiopia
    • Sudan
  • Approximately 200,000-400,000 cases of kala-azar occur globally each year.
  • About 147 million people are at risk in the South-East Asia Region.
  • VL may occur as an opportunistic infection in AIDS.
  • It is associated with:
    • Poverty
    • Malnutrition
    • Population displacement
    • Poor housing
    • Weak immunity
    • Lack of financial resources.

EPIDEMIOLOGICAL DETERMINANTS

1. Agent

  • Leishmania donovani → visceral leishmaniasis/Kala-azar and PKDL
  • Leishmania tropica → cutaneous leishmaniasis
  • Leishmania braziliensis → mucocutaneous leishmaniasis

The parasite is an intracellular protozoan, multiplying within macrophages.

2. RESERVOIR
Animal reservoirs may include:

  • Dogs
  • Jackals
  • Foxes
  • Rodents
  • Other mammals
Important
Indian kala-azar non-zoonotic humans are the main/sole reservoir.

3. HOST FACTORS

  • Age: Can occur at any age, including infants. Classical Indian peak age: 5-9 years.
  • Sex: Males affected approximately twice as often as females.
  • Population movement: Migrants, Labourers, Tourists. Movement between endemic and non-endemic areas can spread infection.
  • Socioeconomic status: Mainly affects poor populations. It is a debilitating disease.
  • Occupation: Higher risk in people involved in: Farming, Forestry, Mining, Fishing.
  • Immunity: Recovery gives long-lasting immunity. During active kala-azar, cell-mediated immunity is impaired. Leishmanin skin test is therefore usually negative during active disease.

4. ENVIRONMENTAL FACTORS

  • Altitude: Mainly confined to plains. Classical Nepal notes: usually not above 2,000 ft/ 600 m.
  • Season: Higher prevalence during and after the rainy season.
  • Rural environment: Traditionally associated mainly with rural areas where sandfly breeding conditions exist. Vector sandflies breed in:
    • Cracks and crevices in soil/buildings
    • Tree holes
    • Caves
    • Rodent burrows

Transmission is favoured by: Overcrowding, Poor ventilation, Accumulation of organic matter.
Habit: Nocturnal, Only female sandflies bite.

VECTOR

Kala-azar
Phlebotomus argentipes

Cutaneous leishmaniasis

  • P. papatasi
  • P. sergenti

DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS AS RISK FACTORS

The following may increase transmission:

  • Forest clearing
  • Cultivation projects
  • Large water-resource schemes
  • Colonization
  • Resettlement programmes

These activities may bring humans into areas with high vector/reservoir concentration.

MODE OF TRANSMISSION

Main route
Infected female sandfly → human
Transmission occurs mainly through the bite of an infected female phlebotomine sandfly.

Other possible routes:

  • Contamination of wounds
  • Crushing of insect during feeding
  • Blood transfusion
  • Contaminated needles and syringes

INCUBATION PERIOD

Extrinsic incubation period
6-9 days

Human incubation period
1-4 months
Range: 10 days-2 years.

CLINICAL FEATURES

1. VISCERAL LEISHMANIASIS / KALA-AZAR
Main features:

  • Fever
  • Splenomegaly
  • Hepatomegaly
  • Anemia
  • Weight loss
  • Darkening of skin, especially:
    • Face
    • Hands
    • Feet
    • Abdomen

Lymphadenopathy may occur but is considered an atypical feature.

Classical picture
Prolonged fever + massive splenomegaly + hepatomegaly + anemia + weight loss + darkening of skin
"Kala-azar" literally refers to black sickness.

2. POST-KALA-AZAR DERMAL LEISHMANIASIS — PKDL

  • Caused by Leishmania donovani
  • Appears 1 to several years after apparent cure of kala-azar.
  • Multiple nodular infiltrations of the skin.
  • Usually without ulceration.
  • Parasites are numerous in the lesions.

Importance
PKDL acts as an important parasite reservoir, so detection and treatment are important for elimination.

3. CUTANEOUS LEISHMANIASIS
Types include:

  • Anthroponotic/urban CL
  • Zoonotic/rural CL
  • Diffuse CL

Features:

  • Painful skin ulcers
  • Usually on exposed parts: Face, Arms, Legs
  • Can reduce ability to work.
  • Parasites are mainly restricted to the skin.
  • May be confused with leprosy.

4. MUCOCUTANEOUS LEISHMANIASIS

  • Ulcers around: Mouth, Nose
  • May cause severe facial mutilation.
  • Can result in social stigma/social isolation.

LABORATORY DIAGNOSIS

1. Parasitological diagnosis
Demonstration of Leishman-Donovan (LD) bodies in aspirates/smears from:

  • Spleen
  • Liver
  • Bone marrow
  • Lymph nodes
  • Skin lesions in CL

Demonstration of the parasite is the definitive parasitological confirmation.

2. ALDEHYDE TEST - NAPIER TEST

  • Nonspecific test.
  • Uses venous blood/serum.
  • Formalin is added to serum.
  • Positive → Jellification → milk-white opacity
  • Strongly positive → Reaction within 2-20 minutes.

Important

  • Usually becomes positive 2-3 months after onset.
  • May become negative around 6 months after cure.
  • Useful for surveillance, but not suitable as a definitive diagnostic test.
  • False positive may occur in chronic infections with reversed albumin-globulin ratio.

3. SEROLOGICAL TESTS

  • Direct agglutination test - DAT
  • rK39 dipstick
  • ELISA
  • IFAT

rK39 TEST

  • Rapid diagnostic test based on rK39 antigen.
  • Detects antibodies against a conserved antigen associated with visceral Leishmania.
  • Gives results rapidly.
  • Used widely for diagnosis of VL in endemic settings.

Important limitation in your notes
It should not be relied upon in:

  • Kala-azar relapse
  • Reinfection
  • Kala-azar-HIV coinfection

4. LEISHMANIN / MONTENEGRO TEST

  • Intradermal test.
  • Read after 48-72 hours.
  • Positive if induration is ≥5 mm.
  • Usually becomes positive 4-6 weeks after onset of CL/MCL.
  • Usually negative during active kala-azar.
  • Becomes positive in many patients after recovery.

Uses:

  • Determining endemicity/epidemicity
  • Identifying groups at risk

5. HAEMATOLOGICAL FINDINGS IN KALA-AZAR

  • Progressive leukopenia
  • Anemia
  • Reversed albumin-globulin ratio
  • Increased IgG
  • Increased ESR

CONTROL OF KALA-AZAR

Main approaches:

  1. Control of reservoir / case management
  2. Sandfly control
  3. Personal protection
  4. Disease surveillance

1. CONTROL OF RESERVOIR

  • Early detection of infected persons
  • Treatment of cases
  • Detection and treatment of PKDL
  • House-to-house visits
  • Active/mass surveys

Treatment
Your original notes list:

  • Oral Miltefosine
  • Liposomal Amphotericin B

2. SANDFLY CONTROL
Indoor residual spraying - IRS
Historically in the Nepal programme:

  • DDT was used as the first choice.
  • Spraying was done twice yearly.
  • Human dwellings, animal shelters and resting places were sprayed.

  • Fill cracks in walls
  • Remove rodent burrows
  • Properly manage firewood/bricks
  • Keep cattle sheds and poultry away from houses
  • Improve housing standards

NATIONAL KALA-AZAR ELIMINATION PROGRAMME — NEPAL

Goal
To contribute to poverty reduction in kala-azar endemic districts by reducing morbidity and mortality and promoting equitable health systems.

Target
Reduce kala-azar incidence to <1 case per 10,000 population at district level.
This remains the WHO definition of elimination as a public health problem for Nepal.

Current Nepal target
The current DoHS report describes the programme as aiming to maintain <1 case/10,000 population and to sustain elimination as a public health problem by 2026.

OBJECTIVES

  1. Reduce incidence of kala-azar in endemic communities, especially among poor, vulnerable and unreached populations.
  2. Reduce case fatality rate to zero.
  3. Detect and treat PKDL to reduce the parasite reservoir.
  4. Prevent and manage Kala-azar-HIV-TB co-infections.

INSECTICIDE RESISTANCE

Definition
Insecticide resistance is the development of the ability of a strain of insects to tolerate doses of insecticide that would normally kill most individuals of the same species.

Causes

  • Biochemical: Insect converts the toxicant into a non-toxic form through enzymes.
  • Genetic: Resistance is transmitted through genes.

TYPES OF ORGANOCHLORINE RESISTANCE

  1. Resistance to DDT and DDT analogues
  2. Resistance to HCH-dieldrin group
  3. Resistance to both groups → double resistance

Double resistance
Organophosphorus and carbamate insecticides may be used, but they are more costly.
Resistance to these groups has also been observed, reducing the choice of effective insecticides.

INTEGRATED VECTOR MANAGEMENT - IVM

Definition
Integrated Vector Management (IVM) is a rational decision-making process for the optimal use of resources for vector control.

It aims to improve:

  • Efficacy
  • Cost-effectiveness
  • Ecological safety
  • Sustainability

The ultimate goal is to prevent transmission of vector-borne diseases.

FIVE KEY ELEMENTS OF IVM

  1. Advocacy, social mobilization, regulatory control and community empowerment
  2. Collaboration within the health sector and with other sectors
  3. Integration of chemical and non-chemical vector-control methods
  4. Evidence-based decision-making using operational research and entomological/epidemiological surveillance
  5. Development of adequate human resources, training and capacity building

COMPONENTS OF IVM

Personal protection

  • Long-lasting insecticidal nets
  • Protective clothing
  • Repellents

Chemical control

  • Indoor residual spraying
  • Larviciding
  • Outdoor spraying

Environmental control

  • Environmental management
  • Elimination/reduction of vector breeding and resting sites

BENEFITS OF IVM

  • Better management of insecticides
  • Reduces harmful environmental and health effects
  • Helps manage insecticide resistance
  • Maximizes available resources
  • Sustains vector-control interventions
  • Improves intersectoral accountability
