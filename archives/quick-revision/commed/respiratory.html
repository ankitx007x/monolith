TB - Applied Epidemiology

1. INTRODUCTION

  • TB = infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis mainly lungs, but also meninges, intestine, bones/joints, lymph nodes, skin, etc.
  • Koch 1882 | AFB due to mycolic acid | weakly Gram +ve.

2. WHO CASE DEFINITIONS

  • Presumptive TB → symptoms/signs suggestive of TB.
  • Bacteriologically confirmed → positive smear / culture / WHO-approved rapid test (Xpert MTB/RIF).
  • Clinically diagnosed → active TB diagnosed clinically/radiologically/histologically despite no bacteriological confirmation.
  • Site: Pulmonary / Extra-pulmonary
  • Previous treatment: New / Retreatment → relapse, failure, lost-to-follow-up, other
  • Resistance: Mono-R, RR, Hr, MDR, XDR
  • HIV: + / - / unknown
  • RR-TB → rifampicin resistant ± other resistance
  • Hr-TB → isoniazid resistant, rifampicin sensitive
  • MDR-TB → H + R resistant
  • XDR-TB → MDR + fluoroquinolone resistance + ≥1 second-line drug (know the current definition if specifically asked; don't rely on the older definition in the pasted notes).

3. NEPAL BURDEN - DoHS AHR 2081/82

  • TB notified = 39,151
  • CNR = 133/100,000
  • New + relapse = 38,606
  • 98.6% incident cases
  • 72.4% = pulmonary TB
  • 58.3% of pulmonary TB = bacteriologically confirmed
  • Xpert-confirmed = 24,475
  • 30 high-notification districts: 18 Terai + 12 Hills.
  • One-line recall: 39,151 → 133/100k → 38,606 incident → 72.4% pulmonary → 58.3% BC → 24,475 Xpert.

4. EPIDEMIOLOGICAL DETERMINANTS

  • Agent: M. tuberculosis
    • Non-motile, non-sporing, non-capsulated
    • 2-3 × 0.2-0.4 μm
    • AFB → mycolic acid
  • Human source: untreated/inadequately treated sputum-positive pulmonary TB
  • Bovine source: infected milk
  • Transmission capacity: 1 infectious case → approximately 10-15 persons/year
  • Host factors:
    • Mainly 15-55 years
    • Male > female
    • No inherited immunity
    • Protection: natural infection / BCG
  • Risk ↑ with: Malnutrition, HIV, Diabetes, Alcoholism
  • Environmental factors: Overcrowding + poor housing + poverty + undernutrition + poor education/awareness

5. TRANSMISSION

  • Droplet nuclei from sputum-positive pulmonary TB.
  • Coughing maximum infectious droplets
  • Droplet nuclei 1-10 μm remain suspended for long periods
  • NOT spread by fomites such as utensils/personal articles.
  • Incubation: ~3-6 weeks
  • Clinical features: Fever + weight loss + night sweats + persistent cough + hemoptysis + fatigue + chest pain

6. EPIDEMIOLOGICAL INDICES

  • Prevalence of infection: % tuberculin-positive population
  • Annual risk/incidence of infection: newly infected among previously non-infected/year
  • Prevalence of disease: → sputum-positive TB burden
  • Incidence of new cases: new bacteriologically confirmed cases/population/year
  • Case detection rate: notified new + relapse / estimated incidence
  • Mortality rate: TB deaths/population/year

7. CASE FINDING

  • Indications: Cough >2 weeks, Chest pain, Hemoptysis, Prolonged low-grade fever
  • Samples: 2 → spot + 1 hour later
  • Culture: if smear negative but suspicion remains + for drug sensitivity.
  • X-ray: supportive; mass miniature radiography not recommended routinely.
  • Mantoux test: 1 TU PPD/0.1 mL ID → forearm → read at 72 h
    • Measure INDURATION, NOT ERYTHEMA.
    • >10 mm → positive
    • 6-9 mm → doubtful
    • <6 mm → negative
    • Important: Mantoux indicates infection, not necessarily active TB.

8. DOTS

Directly Observed Treatment Short-course

P-C-S-D-M:

  1. Political commitment + financing
  2. Case detection by quality-assured bacteriology
  3. Standardized treatment + supervision/support
  4. Drug supply/management
  5. Monitoring + evaluation

CB-DOTS: brings treatment/adherence support closer to community.

9. ANTI-TB DRUGS

  • HRZE: Rifampicin, Isoniazid, Pyrazinamide, Ethambutol
  • Old classification:
    • Bactericidal → RHZS
    • Bacteriostatic → E
  • Standard regimen: 2HRZE/4HR
  • Severe forms such as CNS, pericardial, musculoskeletal, miliary TB → prolonged continuation according to national protocol.

10. DR-TB

  • MDR = H + R resistance
  • Causes: Treatment interruption, Wrong prescription, Poor adherence, Drug supply problems
  • Xpert MTB/RIF → rapid molecular diagnosis + detects rifampicin resistance.
  • Nepal: MDR-TB case management introduced 2005; Xpert introduced around 2011/12.

11. BCG

  • Live attenuated bovine vaccine developed by Calmette & Guérin.
  • Strain: Danish 1331
  • Nepal: at birth
  • Dose: 0.05 mL
  • Route: intradermal
  • Site: right upper arm
  • Storage: 2-8°C, protect from light
  • Mainly protects children against TB meningitis + miliary TB
  • Characteristic scar develops after vaccination.
  • Contraindications: Immunocompromised states, leukemia/lymphoma, immunosuppressive therapy, generalized eczema/infective dermatitis, pregnancy.

12. TB-HIV

  • HIV markedly increases progression from latent → active TB.
  • Strategy (3 I's):
    • Intensified case finding
    • Isoniazid preventive therapy
    • Infection control
  • Management: TB treatment + CPT → ART after ~2-8 weeks depending on clinical situation.

13. MATERNAL/NEWBORN TB

  • If mother is infectious: Prompt maternal ATT, Avoid streptomycin in pregnancy
  • If mother is sputum-positive / treated <2 weeks → newborn may require isoniazid prophylaxis
  • Breastfeeding generally encouraged with appropriate infection-control precautions.

14. CONTROL OF TB

Find the case → Treat completely → Stop transmission → Protect susceptible

  • Early detection
  • Complete effective treatment
  • Contact tracing
  • BCG vaccination
  • Infection control
  • Prevent drug resistance
  • HIV/TB integration
  • Epidemiological control: prevalence of natural infection among 0-14 yr <1%.

15. NEPAL NATIONAL TB PROGRAM

Main goals:

  • Reduce TB incidence
  • Reduce TB mortality
  • Zero catastrophic cost for TB-affected families
  • Ultimately End TB by 2035

3 Pillars:

  1. Integrated, patient-centred care & prevention → early diagnosis, DST, screening, treatment, prevention
  2. Bold policies & supportive systems → political commitment, UHC, notification, social protection
  3. Research & innovation → new diagnostics, drugs, vaccines & implementation

INFLUENZA, AVIAN FLU & SWINE FLU

1. INFLUENZA

  • Definition: Acute respiratory infection caused by Influenza virus.
  • Types: A, B, C → All pandemics = Influenza A
  • Classic presentation: sudden C-F-M-C → Chills + Fever + Myalgia + Cough

Epidemiology

  • Worldwide; millions affected/year
  • Epidemics → rapid spread, sudden onset, many subclinical cases
  • Pandemics → antigenic SHIFT
  • Epidemic → antigenic DRIFT
  • Major pandemics: 1918 Spanish → 1957 Asian → 1968 Hong Kong → 2009 H1N1

Nepal — older DoHS 2079/80 figures

  • ILI cases = 2,207
  • H1N1 positive = 85
  • H3 positive = 208
  • Influenza B Victoria = 89
  • Positivity = 5.1%
  • Highest ILI reporting: Karnali (1,560)

2. AGENT & TRANSMISSION ⭐

  • Family: Orthomyxoviridae → A, B, C
  • Influenza A antigens:
    • H = Haemagglutinin → attachment/initiation
    • N = Neuraminidase → release/transmission
  • Antigenic changes:
    • SHIFT = major/sudden → reassortment → PANDEMIC
    • DRIFT = minor/gradual → point mutation → EPIDEMIC
  • Reservoir: humans + animals/birds
  • Source: case/subclinical case respiratory secretions
  • Infective: ~1-2 days before to 1-2 days after symptoms
  • Transmission: droplets/droplet nuclei
  • Entry: respiratory tract
  • Incubation: 18-72 h

3. HOST / ENVIRONMENT

  • All ages + both sexes
  • High risk: >65, children, DM, cardiac/renal/respiratory disease
  • Immunity: subtype-specific; no cross-protection between A/B/C
  • Winter → increased transmission
  • Overcrowding → ↑ spread
  • Closed populations: schools, institutions, ships

4. CLINICAL

  • Sudden fever + chills + myalgia + cough + weakness
  • Complications:
    • Pneumonia = most important
    • Sinusitis
    • Otitis media
    • Purulent bronchitis
    • Reye syndrome → hepatic failure + encephalopathy, especially children
  • Diagnosis: Nasopharyngeal specimen, Viral detection/isolation, Serology: HI/ELISA (4-fold rise in titre = diagnostic)

5. PREVENTION

  • Ventilation + avoid crowds + cough etiquette + stay home
  • Vaccine:
    • Best preventive measure
    • Constantly updated because of antigenic variation
    • Ideally ≥2 weeks before exposure/epidemic
    • Inactivated vaccine → IM/SC
    • Live attenuated → intranasal
    • Immunity → 6-12 months
  • Antivirals: Oseltamivir → 75 mg BD; Zanamivir → inhaled. Used for treatment/prophylaxis of influenza A & B.

6. AVIAN INFLUENZA

  • Influenza A in birds
  • Important strains: H5N1, H7N3, H7N7, H7N9, H9N2
  • Reservoir: migratory wild birds → domestic poultry can become infected.
  • Risk: People exposed to infected birds + droppings + feathers + contaminated surfaces
  • Transmission: Respiratory entry + contaminated dust/equipment/feed/cages/clothing.
  • Incubation: 2–3 days
  • Clinical: Fever + fatigue + sore throat + cough + conjunctivitis + myalgia ± GI symptoms. Severe: viral pneumonia → respiratory failure → renal/multiorgan failure → death
  • Treatment: Oseltamivir 75 mg BD × 5 days, ideally started within 48 h.

7. SWINE FLU / PANDEMIC H1N1 🐷

  • 2009 → Mexico → WHO pandemic declaration: 11 June 2009
  • Why different? Population had little/no immunity; Can cause rapidly progressive lower respiratory disease
  • Agent: Influenza A, especially H1N1
  • Reservoir: pigs
  • High-risk: Pregnancy + <2 yr + >65 yr + COPD/asthma + chronic cardiac/metabolic/renal/hepatic/neurological disease + immunosuppression + obesity
  • Transmission: respiratory
  • Incubation: 2-3 days
  • Clinical: ILI: fever + cough + sore throat + rhinorrhea + headache + myalgia + malaise ± diarrhea/vomiting. Severe: Dyspnea/LRTI, Encephalitis/encephalopathy, Dehydration, Exacerbation of chronic disease

8. DIAGNOSIS & CONTROL OF H1N1

  • Diagnosis: RT-PCR = most sensitive + timely. Nasopharyngeal + throat swab. Lower respiratory specimen if severe LRT disease.
  • Infection control: HAND HYGIENE + COUGH ETIQUETTE + MASK + ventilation. Aerosol-generating procedure: respirator + eye protection + gown + adequately ventilated room.
  • Vaccination: Does not give 100% protection. Protection develops after ~14 days.
    1. Pregnant women
    2. Children >6 months with chronic disease
    3. Healthy young adults
    4. Healthy children
    5. Adults
    6. Elderly

🦠 SARS & COVID — COMPACT REVISION

1. SARS

  • Agent: Novel coronavirus | Reservoir: Horseshoe bat | Incubation: 2–7 d
  • Epi: 8,273 cases (2003) | HCWs 21% | children rarely affected | max respiratory shedding ~day 10
  • Clinical: Fever + cough/SOB + pneumonia/ARDS on CXR + no alternative diagnosis
  • CXR: unilateral patch → bilateral/generalized infiltrates → ARDS
  • Transmission: respiratory droplets + fomites → eyes/nose/mouth; AGPs amplify hospital spread; survives surfaces hours, waste up to 4 d
  • Dx: RT-PCR (multiple specimens/repeated testing) ± culture; serology = acute negative → convalescent positive OR ≥4× titre rise
  • Complications: pneumonia, ARDS, nosocomial infection, tension pneumothorax, non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema
  • Tx: intensive supportive care ± ribavirin, lopinavir/ritonavir, IFN-1, IVIG, steroids (efficacy inconclusive); no classic SARS vaccine
  • Prevention: early detection → isolation → contact tracing → HCW protection → hand hygiene/masks → travel screening/reporting

2. COVID-19

  • Agent: SARS-CoV-2 | Host: Humans | Environment: crowding + poor ventilation
  • Nepal Stats:
    • FY 2079/80: 5,789 EWARS cases
    • 2022: 56,338 cases + 88 deaths (largest outbreak then)
    • Vaccination: 1st 83.1% | 2nd 83.6%
  • Transmission:
    • Droplets/secretions + fomites eyes/nose/mouth
    • Airborne mainly AGPs + poorly ventilated indoor spaces
  • Infectivity: 1-3 d before symptoms; peak viral load around symptom onset; declines thereafter.
  • Urine/feces: virus detected but transmission unclear; vertical transmission: no strong evidence.
  • Diagnosis: RT-PCR (NP/OP/BAL) → ± RDT/GeneXpert, CXR/CT
  • Treatment: Mostly supportive; severe: O2/high-flow ventilation. Drugs: Remdesivir + dexamethasone ± convalescent plasma
  • Management = T-I-T-Q-T-S: Test, Isolate, Trace, Quarantine, Treat, Surveillance
  • Prevention = SMS + V: Social distancing ≥1 m, Mask, Sanitization/hand hygiene + Ventilation + Vaccination + respiratory etiquette + stay home when sick
  • HCW: contact/droplet precautions; airborne precautions during AGPs
  • Vaccines: Sinopharm | Covishield | Janssen | Pfizer
  • Nepal preparedness: Preparedness plan + dedicated COVID hospitals + 5T: Testing → Tracking → Treatment → Training → Isolation

🥹 ARI — COMPACT REVISION

Classification

  • AURI: up to pharynx → cold, pharyngitis, otitis media
  • ALRI: larynx → alveoli → laryngitis, epiglottitis, bronchitis, bronchiolitis, pneumonia

🌍 Burden

  • U5 children: ~5 ARI episodes/child/year
  • 30–50% health-facility visits; 20–40% admissions
  • Pneumonia → major cause of U5 mortality

Nepal — DoHS AHR 2081/82

  • ARI incidence U5 = 616/1,000 nationally
  • Highest: Karnali 992, then Sudurpaschim 857
  • Lowest: Bagmati 468
  • Severe pneumonia = 0.08% of new cases nationally
  • Pneumonia cases treated with antibiotics: ~101% nationally (HMIS indicator can exceed 100 due to reporting/denominator issues) (HMIS)

Determinants

  • Agents:
    • Bacteria → S. pneumoniae, S. pyogenes, H. influenzae, Klebsiella, Bordetella, Corynebacterium, Legionella
    • Viruses → Influenza, RSV, adenovirus, parainfluenza, rhinovirus, measles, coronavirus
  • Host: U5 highest; boys > girls until 3 yr → females > males thereafter
  • Environment: overcrowding + indoor air pollution + poor nutrition + low birth weight + maternal smoking + low SES
  • Transmission: predominantly airborne/respiratory

👟 CB-IMNCI — EXAM GOLD

Assess: Age + cough duration + feeding/drinking + fever + convulsion + cyanosis + consciousness + prior treatment

Fast breathing thresholds:

  • <2 mo: ≥60/min
  • 2-12 mo: ≥50/min
  • 12 mo-5 yr: ≥40/min

Classification & Action

  • Very severe: danger signs → URGENT referral + first antibiotic
  • Severe pneumonia: chest indrawing + fast breathing → referral + antibiotics
  • Pneumonia: fast breathing WITHOUT chest indrawing → oral antibiotic + home care + review
  • No pneumonia: cough/cold, no fast breathing/no indrawing → home care

Note for <2 months: ≥60/min or severe chest indrawing = severe pneumonia; danger signs → very severe.

Danger signs: Can't drink/feed | convulsions | lethargic/unconscious | stridor (calm child) | wheeze | abnormal temp | severe malnutrition

Prevention: Nutrition + clean air + better living conditions + immunization + early treatment

  • MR: 9 & 15 mo
  • Hib: 6, 10, 14 wk
  • PCV: 6, 10 wk + 9 mo

Programs: GAPPD: PROTECT - PREVENT - TREAT

Nepal CB-IMNCI → Vision 90 by 2030:

  • 90% institutional delivery
  • 90% newborn CHX
  • 90% U5 diarrhoea → ORS + zinc
  • 90% U5 pneumonia → appropriate antibiotics

Viral pneumonia

  • Agents: influenza, RSV, adenovirus, VZV, coronavirus
  • Risk: U2 / ≥65, pregnancy, immunocompromised, chronic disease
  • Dx: clinical + CXR ± swab/sputum/blood tests
  • Tx: supportive ± specific antivirals
  • Prevention: hygiene + influenza vaccination + isolation

MEASLES — COMPACT REVISION

1. Basics & Epidemiology

  • Agent: Measles virus → RNA Paramyxovirus
  • Reservoir/source: Humans only; no carriers/animal reservoir
  • Transmission: droplets + droplet nuclei → respiratory tract ± conjunctiva
  • Incubation: 10 d fever | 14 d → rash
  • Infectious: 4 d before to 4 d after rash
  • Immunity: lifelong after infection; single antigenic type
  • Season: no fixed season; dry season tropics, winter temperate
  • Highly infectious, especially prodrome/rash onset

Nepal - DoHS AHR 2081/82

  • 33 measles cases, including 29 laboratory-confirmed
  • MR1 coverage: 87%
  • MR2 coverage: 96%
  • NMNR: 9.4/100,000 (surveillance standard ≥2/100,000)
  • All provinces exceeded the NMNR surveillance standard.
  • Key concern: MR1 remains below the desired ≥95% level.

2. Clinical Features

PRODROMAL → ERUPTIVE → POST-MEASLES

  • Prodrome:
    • Fever
    • Coryza + cough + conjunctivitis
    • Lacrimation/photophobia
    • Koplik spots: 1-2 mm blue-white spots on red buccal mucosa opposite 2nd molar → pathognomonic
  • Epidemiological significance: Prodromal patient is highly infectious but difficult to clinically recognize as measles → not isolated → transmission continues → outbreaks.
  • Eruptive phase:
    • Dusky-red maculopapular rash
    • Starts hairline/face → neck → trunk → limbs/feet
    • Appears ~3–5 d after symptoms
    • May coalesce; fades in same direction over 5–7 d
    • Fever may spike >104°F
  • Post-measles: weight loss + weakness

3. Host & Risk Factors

  • Age: mainly infants/children
  • Sex: similar incidence
  • Malnutrition: → more severe + prolonged viral excretion
  • No age immunity if never infected/vaccinated
  • Environmental: overcrowding, poor nutrition, low SES

4. Complications ⭐

  • Pneumonia ⭐
  • Otitis media
  • Encephalitis → SSPE
  • Bronchitis
  • Severe diarrhoea
  • Blindness
  • Thrombocytopenia
  • Miscarriage/preterm labour
  • Other neurological complications

5. Diagnosis & Treatment

  • Diagnosis: Clinical + measles IgM
  • Treatment: Mainly supportive (Fluids, rest, fever control), Vitamin A (↓ morbidity & mortality), Manage complications, Immunoglobulin for selected immunocompromised/high-risk exposed children

6. Prevention

  • Vaccination: Live attenuated MR vaccine. Nepal: MR1 at 9 months, MR2 at 15 months. Route: SC. Goal: ≥95% immunization coverage to maintain population immunity.
  • Immunoglobulin: Post-exposure IM within 3-4 d. High-risk contacts: <1 yr, immunocompromised, severe HIV. Don't give vaccine + IG simultaneously.
  • Outbreak control: Isolate 7 d after rash, Vaccinate contacts within 2 d (if vaccine contraindicated → IG within 3-4 d, vaccinate 8-12 weeks later). Intensify vaccination immediately during outbreak.

7. MEASLES ELIMINATION STRATEGY

CATCH-UP → KEEP-UP → FOLLOW-UP

  • Catch-up: Supplementary immunization targeting all children 9 months-14 years, irrespective of previous vaccination history.
  • Keep-up: Routine immunization aiming for >95% coverage of each successive birth cohort with MR1 + MR2.
  • Follow-up: Nationwide campaigns every 2-4 years, targeting children 9-59 months.

National strategies: Achieve & sustain high population immunity; Achieve & sustain ≥95% MCV-1 coverage; Investigate all suspected measles-like outbreaks with program response; Use measles elimination platform for Rubella/CRS control; Continue case-based measles surveillance.

8. Surveillance

  • Integrated with AFP surveillance → 2003
  • Case-based surveillance → 2007
  • Suspected case: Fever + maculopapular rash + cough/coryza/conjunctivitis
  • Surveillance actions: Notify → investigate → collect blood → laboratory confirmation → manage outbreak response
  • District rapid response team investigates suspected outbreaks.
  • Surveillance goal: Identify cases/outbreaks by time & place, age/vaccination status, high-risk populations; Verify outbreaks; Improve timeliness/completeness; Provide feedback.
  • NMNR = 9.4/100,000 in 2081/82 compared with the ≥2/100,000 standard indicates adequate surveillance sensitivity.

CHICKENPOX (VARICELLA) — COMPACT REVISION

1. Basics & Epidemiology

  • Agent: VZV = Human α-herpesvirus-3, enveloped dsDNA
  • Source: infected person respiratory secretions + skin/mucosal lesions
  • Incubation: 14-17 d (range 10-21)
  • Infectious: 1-2 d before to 4-5 d after rash; crusts not infectious
  • Transmission: droplets/droplet nuclei + direct contact; portal = URT/conjunctiva
  • Secondary attack rate: up to 90%
  • Children <10 yr common; adults more severe
  • Primary infection → lifelong immunity
  • Pregnancy → congenital varicella syndrome, especially early pregnancy
  • Crowding favors spread; tropics coolest/driest months

2. Clinical Features

  • Prodrome: Fever + malaise + headache + anorexia
  • Rash evolution: Macule → Papule → Vesicle → Pustule → Crust
  • Centripetal distribution: trunk → face + extremities
  • Crops + pleomorphic rash = pathognomonic → lesions at different stages simultaneously
  • Severe itching, mucosal lesions
  • Usually heals without scars unless secondarily infected; hypo/hyperpigmentation may remain

3. Complications

  • Secondary bacterial skin infection ⭐
  • Pneumonia
  • Meningoencephalitis
  • Acute cerebellar ataxia
  • Transverse myelitis
  • GBS

4. Diagnosis

  • Mainly clinical
  • PCR = preferred confirmation
  • Anti-IgM
  • Tzanck smear → multinucleated giant cells

5. Treatment

  • Symptomatic: antipyretic + antipruritic + hygiene
  • Isolate
  • Acyclovir / valacyclovir / famciclovir × 5 d, ideally started within 24 h
  • VZIG for appropriate high-risk exposure

6. Prevention & Control ⭐

  • Case control: Isolate ~6 d after rash onset; Disinfect articles contaminated with respiratory secretions; Early antiviral therapy
  • VZIG: Give within 96 h (preferably ≤72 h) after exposure. Indications: Susceptible immunocompromised, Pregnant women, Hospitalized premature infants, Significant exposure in susceptible individuals. Repeat dose after 3 weeks where indicated.
  • Vaccine: Live attenuated varicella vaccine. ≥1 yr, ≤12 yr: single dose (as per source). Seronegative >12 yr: 2 doses, 4-8 weeks apart. Contraindicated: pregnancy + immunocompromised.

DIPHTHERIA — COMPACT REVISION

1. Basics & Epidemiology

  • Agent: Corynebacterium diphtheriae Gram +, non-motile, non-capsulated, club-shaped bacillus. Toxigenic strains produce powerful exotoxin.
  • Source: cases + carriers (carriers important)
  • Infective material: nasopharyngeal secretions, skin lesions, contaminated fomites
  • Transmission: mainly droplets; also direct contact/fomites
  • Portal: respiratory; also skin, umbilicus, eye, ear, genitalia
  • Incubation: 2-6 days
  • Infectivity: ~14-28 days from disease onset
  • Host: traditionally children 1-5 yr, but age distribution can shift toward school age
  • Season: all seasons; winter predominance
  • Risk: overcrowding + poor sanitation
  • Nepal: EPI with BCG + DPT began 1979 in Bara, Kaski & Bhaktapur → expanded nationwide by 1989. Vaccination caused a dramatic decline in diphtheria. Source reports 4 cases in 2023. (For 2081/82: comparable national total not directly verified). (HMIS)

2. Clinical Features

A. Respiratory

  • Pharyngotonsillar (commonest): Sore throat + dysphagia, Low-grade fever, Gray/white pseudomembrane, Membrane bleeds on attempted removal, Severe bull neck
  • Laryngotracheal: Hoarseness + croupy cough, Stridor/dyspnoea, Membrane → airway obstruction
  • Nasal: Usually mildest, Nasal discharge/lesion, Nasal carriers particularly dangerous

B. Non-respiratory

  • Cutaneous: punched-out ulcers
  • Ocular: conjunctivitis/corneal damage
  • Intestinal: dysphagia/bloody diarrhoea
  • Genital: ulcerative lesions

3. Complications

  • Myocarditis
  • Polyneuropathy → post-diphtheritic paralysis
  • Mechanical asphyxia from pseudomembrane
  • Pneumonia
  • Otitis media
  • Diphtheria toxin = HEART + NERVES

4. Diagnosis

  • Suspect clinically: Sore throat + fever + pseudomembrane ± hoarseness/stridor/palatal paralysis
  • Confirm: Throat/nasal swab → Culture of C. diphtheriae on Loeffler's serum slope → characteristic colonies
  • Do not wait for laboratory confirmation before treatment if clinically suspected.

5. Prevention

  • Vaccination: Nepal EPI: Pentavalent (DPT-HepB-Hib) → 6, 10, 14 weeks. Boosters: After primary series DT <7 yr, dT ≥7 yr. Periodic boosters maintain protection.
  • Control of case/carriers: Early detection → isolate → swab contacts → treat. Search contacts/family/friends → Nose + throat swabs → Isolate suspected cases/carriers. Diphtheria antitoxin (DAT) immediately. Antibiotics: erythromycin / penicillin.

6. Contact Management

  • Previously adequately immunized + recent: generally no additional action if adequately protected.
  • Immunized >2 yr ago: → booster diphtheria toxoid
  • Unimmunized: → prophylactic penicillin/erythromycin → DAT 1,000-2,000 U → start/complete active immunization

Contacts: swab + prophylactic antibiotic + booster/active immunization as indicated.

Exam trigger: If asked "Diphtheria control", write: Vaccination → early detection → isolation → contact tracing/swabbing → DAT → antibiotics → immunization of contacts.

🍾 WHOOPING COUGH (PERTUSSIS)

1. Basics & Epidemiology

  • Agent: Bordetella pertussis
  • Reservoir: Humans only; cases = source, no carrier state
  • Infective material: nasopharyngeal/bronchial secretions + freshly contaminated objects
  • Transmission: droplets + direct contact
  • Incubation: 7–14 d
  • Infectious: mainly catarrhal stage → ~3 wk after paroxysmal stage begins
  • Secondary attack rate: ~90% in unimmunized household contacts
  • Host: infants & preschool children; fatality higher in females
  • Season: year-round → ↑ winter/spring
  • Overcrowding + low SES → increased transmission
  • Nepal: Source reports 11 cases in 2023. Nepal uses whole-cell pertussis vaccine (wP) in the routine programme. Don't carry the 11-case figure forward as 2081/82 data unless specifically asked for historical data; couldn't verify a comparable 2081/82 total from current DoHS report. (HMIS)

2. Clinical Course ⭐⭐⭐

  1. 1. CATARRHAL — 7–10 d
    Coryza + low-grade fever + mild cough → progressively worse.
    🔥 MOST INFECTIOUS STAGE
  2. 2. PAROXYSMAL — 1–6 wk
    • Repeated rapid paroxysms of cough
    • Thick mucus difficult to expel
    • Inspiratory whoop ⭐
    • Cyanosis
    • Post-tussive vomiting
    • Exhaustion
    • Often worse at night
    • ~15 attacks/day on average
  3. 3. CONVALESCENT — 7–10 d
    • Gradual recovery
    • Paroxysms decrease → disappear over 2–3 wk
    • Cough may recur with subsequent respiratory infections for months

🧠 Classic sequence: Coryza → cough → paroxysm → WHOOP → vomiting

3. Complications

  • Bronchopneumonia ⭐
  • Bronchitis
  • Bronchiectasis
  • Subconjunctival haemorrhage
  • Epistaxis
  • Punctate cerebral haemorrhage

4. Diagnosis

  • Nasopharyngeal culture → highly specific
  • PCR → rapid + highly sensitive
  • Serology / fluorescent antibody staining
  • 🔥 During suspected outbreak: PCR + obtain culture confirmation of at least one suspected case.

5. Prevention ⭐

Active Immunization

  • Best prevention = vaccination
  • Nepal → whole-cell pertussis (wP), usually as DTwP/pentavalent
  • Primary series: 6 wk → 10 wk → 14 wk
  • WHO: 3-dose primary series
  • Protection declines over time
  • Booster 1–6 yr after primary series, preferably 2nd year
  • Some countries give adolescent/adult booster, especially HCWs/young parents.

Passive

  • Hyperimmune globulin → benefit not established

6. Control of Cases & Contacts

  • Case: Early diagnosis → isolation → treatment → disinfect discharges. Isolate until non-infectious. Erythromycin = drug of choice (Alternatives: ampicillin etc.). Antibiotics during paroxysmal phase don't significantly alter clinical course, but eliminate bacteria → reduce transmission.
  • Contacts: Keep infants/young children away from case. Erythromycin/ampicillin × 10 d prophylaxis. Ensure DPT vaccination.
